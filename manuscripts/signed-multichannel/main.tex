\begin{abstract}
For the signed heat kernel of even order $p=2m$, we construct a
multichannel reconstruction formula on a specified Banach completion
of dyadic polynomial cylinders. The coefficient fields are genuine
Bochner $L^1$ limits of native predictable Taylor coefficients and
have degree-independent bounds on the original observable norm.
The centered observable pairing is the sum of $p-1$ bounded signed
channels in the continuous test dual. The proof begins with an exact
finite signed telescope, proves quantitative increment and
adjacent-refinement estimates, and only then passes to the observable
completion. A joint diagonal realizes every completed observable
through actual signed polynomial sums. The pairing is indefinite;
no positive path-space measure or stochastic isometry is asserted.
Static Appell reconstruction, unique higher-order polynomial Stein weight,
missing first polynomial coordinate dual and actual Schwartz-density duals
are proved separately from the chronological formula. In fourth order,
these dual densities propagate in $L^2$ at positive time and extend
to derivative-of-Dirac functionals at zero in the pointwise Schwartz-test topology.
We distinguish canonical coefficient uniqueness from uniqueness of channel synthesis. Exact variation/path-law, quartic-error, deterministic $L^2$-input and terminal-channel obstructions delimit stronger interpretations. A normalized
polynomial sequence also proves nonclosability of the mean in compact-open
path topology, including the Gaussian case; zero-start and all-start
Schwartz testing are distinguished. All principal results are supported by a frozen
Lean development. Novelty relative to earlier higher-order signed
calculus remains unresolved.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope and normalization}
Fix $m\in\N$, $m>0$, a physical horizon $T>0$, and put
\[
 p=2m,\qquad c=(-1)^{m+1}.
\]
The fundamental signed kernel $k_\tau$, $\tau>0$, has Fourier
transform $e^{-\tau\xi^p}$ and generator $c\partial_x^p$.
We normalize the Fourier transform by
\[
 k_\tau(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R e^{-\tau\xi^p}e^{-i\xi x}\,d\xi.
\]
Changing the Fourier sign gives the same even real kernel.
For each finite partition, independent increments mean the product
of these signed densities; they do not mean independent random
variables under an assumed positive path law.

The analytic domain $\D$ will be defined explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:domain}.
It has a dense injective polynomial core, a bounded signed mean
$\E_T:\D\to\R$, and a bounded symmetric pairing
\[
 J_T:\D\longrightarrow\D^*,\qquad
 J_T(P)(G)=\E_T(PG)
\]
on polynomial cylinders. Products in this last identity are finite
polynomial products; bounded multiplication on the whole completion
is not being assumed. Let $\lambda$ denote Lebesgue measure on
$[0,1]$, so time in the coefficient fields is normalized time and
the physical horizon occurs in all kernel parameters and channel
constants. Write $\mathcal F=L^1(\lambda;\D^*)$ for Bochner $L^1$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There are canonical bounded linear maps $C_q:\D\to\mathcal F$,
$q\geq0$, satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Cbound}
 \norm{C_q u}_{\mathcal F}\leq
 \frac{\norm{J_T}e^T}{q!}\norm u_{\D}.
\end{equation}
For $1\leq q\leq p-1$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:channel}
 (I_q A)(v)=cpT(p-1)_{q-1}
 \int_0^1 A(s)(D_s^{p-q}v)\,ds,
\end{equation}
where $(r)_k=r(r-1)\cdots(r-k+1)$ and $D_s$ is the bounded cutoff
derivative defined below. Then every $u\in\D$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 J_T\big(u-\E_T(u)1\big)
 =\sum_{q=1}^{p-1} I_q(C_q u)\qquad\text{in }\D^*.
\end{equation}
On every fixed polynomial cylinder, $C_q$ is the norm limit in
$\mathcal F$ of its native predictable Taylor coefficient fields.
Its values lie in the closed span of actual adapted polynomial
step fields. Every completed observable admits a simultaneous
polynomial/mesh approximation of all surviving fields and the
right side of \eqref{eq:main}.
\end{theorem}

The equality is in the test dual, not an assertion of equality in
$\D$ obtained by inverting $J_T$. Injectivity of $J_T$ on the entire
completion is not supplied. The theorem is therefore a signed
Clark--Ocone-type reconstruction with its topology specified.

Higher-order signed heat calculus has substantial antecedents,
including Hochberg's work~\cite{hochberg}. Complex random-walk
constructions give another generalized calculus~\cite{bcm}, while
time-changed pseudoprocesses are studied in~\cite{co}. The present
paper does not claim the first higher-order It\^o calculus. Its
precise subject is the simultaneous native coefficient limit and
bounded observable completion for these actual signed cylinder
laws. Priority for this package remains unresolved.

\section{Actual finite signed laws and polynomial conditioning}
The function $e^{-\tau\xi^p}$ is Schwartz. Fourier inversion shows
that $k_\tau$ is real, even and Schwartz, has integral one, and
satisfies $k_\tau*k_\sigma=k_{\tau+\sigma}$. Thus polynomial
functions are integrable against its total variation. Its moments
are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
 \mu_r(\tau)=\int x^r k_\tau(x)\,dx
 =\begin{cases}(pk)!(c\tau)^k/k!,&r=pk,\\0,&p\nmid r.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $r=0$ this gives one. To obtain the formula, differentiate the
Fourier transform at zero; Schwartz decay justifies differentiation
under the integral. The derivative of $e^{-\tau\xi^p}$ vanishes
unless the order is a multiple of $p$, and the resulting powers
of $i$ give $c^k$. Equivalently, for every polynomial $f$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heat}
 \int f(x)k_\tau(x)\,dx
 =\big(e^{c\tau\partial^p}f\big)(0).
\end{equation}
The exponential series terminates on each polynomial.

An integration-by-parts identity that will control all power
channels is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stein}
 \int xf(x)k_\tau(x)\,dx
 =cp\tau\int f^{(p-1)}(x)k_\tau(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}
It follows either from the moments \eqref{eq:moments}, by testing
monomials, or from differentiation of the Fourier transform.
All boundary terms vanish by Schwartz decay.

On the dyadic grid with $N=2^n$ cells set $h=1/N$, $\tau=Th$,
and use increment variables $x_0,\ldots,x_{N-1}$. The signed
expectation $\E_{T,n}$ integrates against
$\prod_j k_\tau(x_j)\,dx_j$. The position variables are
$y_i=x_0+\cdots+x_i$. Refinement replaces each coarse increment
by the sum of two fine increments. Convolution implies consistency
of these actual signed expectations.

For $0\leq j\leq N$, let $\Pi_jP$ integrate increments with
indices $i\geq j$, leaving $x_0,\ldots,x_{j-1}$ untouched.
It is a polynomial in the retained increments. In particular
$\Pi_0P=\E_{T,n}P$, $\Pi_NP=P$, and $\Pi_j\Pi_k=\Pi_{\min(j,k)}$.
These formulas follow from finite signed Fubini, with absolute
integrability of polynomials against the product variation.
The notation is polynomial conditioning; no general conditional
expectation theorem for an indefinite path law is invoked.


\section{Static Appell reconstruction and its actual density duals}
Fix one kernel time $\tau>0$ and abbreviate $a=c\tau\ne0$.
Let $L_a(P)=\int P(x)k_\tau(x)\,dx$ on real polynomials.
Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} says, as a \emph{formal power series},
\[
 L_a(e^{xz})=e^{az^p}.
\]
This notation means applying $L_a$ separately to each polynomial
coefficient. No exchange of an infinite exponential series with an
integral is required. Define monic polynomials $H_n$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appellgenerating}
 e^{xz-az^p}=\sum_{n\geq0}H_n(x)\frac{z^n}{n!},\qquad
 H_n(x)=n!\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/p\rfloor}
       \frac{(-a)^j x^{n-pj}}{j!(n-pj)!}.
\end{equation}
Each sum is finite. Formal differentiation and the preceding moment
identity give
\[
 H_0=1,\qquad H_n'=nH_{n-1}\ (n\geq1),\qquad
 L_a(H_n)=\mathbf1_{n=0}.
\]
Differentiating with respect to $z$ also yields the raising identity
$H_{n+1}=xH_n-paH_n^{(p-1)}$, so these are exactly the source's
pure-order Appell polynomials, not merely another monic family.
The exponential-polynomial mechanism is classical. Hile and
Stanoyevitch~\cite{hile}, Sections 3--4, construct broader heat
polynomials by generating functions; their first-time-order case
has exponential symbol $e^{tQ(z)}$, including the displayed family
with $Q(z)=-az^p$ at time one. No new Appell construction is claimed.

Since $H_n$ is monic of degree $n$, triangular elimination gives a
unique finite expansion of every polynomial:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appellcoordinates}
 P=\sum_{n=0}^{\deg P}c_n(P)H_n,\qquad
 c_n(P)=\frac{L_a(P^{(n)})}{n!}.
\end{equation}
Indeed $H_k^{(n)}=(k)_nH_{k-n}$ for $n\leq k$, and is zero otherwise.
Applying $L_a$ leaves only $k=n$, with value $n!$, proving the
coordinate formula and uniqueness without an orthogonality assumption.

\subsection*{Nondegenerate pairing does not supply polynomial coordinate duals}
The signed polynomial pairing $[P,Q]=L_a(PQ)$ is nondegenerate.
Here is an explicit witness for every nonzero $Q$ of degree $d$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appelltop}
 [H_{(p-1)d},Q]
 =\operatorname{lc}(Q)\,((p-1)d)!\,(pa)^d\ne0.
\end{equation}
To prove it, first use formal coefficients in
\[
 L_a(e^{xz-az^p}e^{xw})
 =\exp\!\left(a\big((z+w)^p-z^p\big)\right).
\]
The coefficient of $w^d$, multiplied by $d!$, is a polynomial in
$z$ of degree at most $(p-1)d$. A product of factors with respective
$w$-degrees $j_1,\ldots,j_r\geq1$, summing to $d$, has $z$-degree
$pr-d\leq(p-1)d$. Equality forces $r=d$ and every $j_i=1$.
Thus its leading coefficient is $(pa)^d$. Comparing the coefficient
of $z^n/n!$ proves
\[
 [H_n,x^d]=0\quad(n>(p-1)d),\qquad
 [H_{(p-1)d},x^d]=((p-1)d)!(pa)^d.
\]
The lower monomials of $Q$ vanish at the last index, proving
\eqref{eq:appelltop}, including $d=0$.

This gives the unique polynomial higher-order Stein weight:
\[
 L_a\!\left(P\frac{x}{pa}\right)=L_a(P^{(p-1)}).
\]
Existence is \eqref{eq:stein}. If another polynomial $W$ has this
identity for all $P$, then $[P,W-x/(pa)]=0$ for all $P$, and
\eqref{eq:appelltop} forces $W=x/(pa)$.

In contrast, for $p>2$ there is no polynomial $Q$ with
$[H_n,Q]=\mathbf1_{n=1}$ for all $n$. If such $Q$ were zero the
$n=1$ test would fail. Otherwise put $d=\deg Q$ and test at
$n=(p-1)d$. This index cannot equal one, since $p-1\geq2$;
the requested pairing is zero, contradicting \eqref{eq:appelltop}.
In particular there is no polynomial first-order weight $W$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nofirststein}
 L_a(PW)=L_a(P')\qquad\hbox{for every polynomial }P.
\end{equation}
Such a weight would represent $c_1$, since
$L_a(H_n')=\mathbf1_{n=1}$. The Gaussian case $p=2$ is excluded
from this obstruction: its familiar weight $x/(2a)$ does satisfy the
first-order identity. Nondegeneracy tests whether a polynomial is
annihilated by \emph{all} pairing probes; it does not assert that every
linear coordinate functional is represented by a polynomial weight.

\subsection*{Actual Schwartz-density coordinate duals}
There is nevertheless a concrete dual for every coordinate. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appellactualdensity}
 \rho_n(x)=\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}k_\tau^{(n)}(x).
\end{equation}
These are real Schwartz functions, so $P\rho_n$ is absolutely
Lebesgue integrable for every polynomial $P$. Repeated ordinary
integration by parts, with vanishing boundary terms, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:appellbiorthogonal}
 \int P(x)\rho_n(x)\,dx=c_n(P),\qquad
 \int H_j(x)\rho_n(x)\,dx=\mathbf1_{j=n}.
\end{equation}
The $(-1)^n$ from integration by parts cancels that in
\eqref{eq:appellactualdensity}. Thus the polynomial basis has actual
Schwartz-density biorthogonal coordinates, even though its first
coordinate has no polynomial dual in the original signed pairing
when $p>2$. In particular $\rho_1$ cannot equal $Wk_\tau$ almost
everywhere for any polynomial $W$: such equality would imply
\eqref{eq:nofirststein} after integrating every polynomial test.
This statement excludes polynomial weights only. It supplies no
$L^2(|k_\tau|\,dx)$ score bound, bounded completion of all coordinate
maps, positive covariance or probability interpretation.


Finally define the centered polynomial primitive by
\[
 \mathcal I P=\sum_n\frac{c_n(P)}{n+1}H_{n+1}.
\]
It satisfies $(\mathcal I P)'=P$ and $L_a(\mathcal I P)=0$.
Any other such primitive differs by a constant with mean zero, hence
by zero. Testing on the basis gives, for every integer $r\geq0$,
\[
 \mathcal I^r P^{(r)}=P-\sum_{n<r}c_n(P)H_n.
\]
For $n\geq r$ the falling derivative factors cancel the successive
primitive denominators; for $n<r$ the derivative is zero. The omitted
part is all low Appell coordinates, rather than just the constant.
For example $c_1(x^{p+1})=(p+1)p!a\ne0$, so reconstruction from the
$p$th derivative by this primitive does not equal mere centering.
These are finite polynomial identities. The primitive is not declared
to be a chronological stochastic integral; the multitime analytic
completion and native coefficient limits still require the separate
proofs below.

\subsection*{Fourth-order propagation and the singular initial dual}
Here specialize to $p=4$, so the generator is $-\partial_x^4$
and the Appell parameter at time $t>0$ is $a=-t$.
Write $k_t$ for this kernel and
$\rho_{n,t}=(-1)^n\partial_x^n k_t/n!$.
Let $S_t$ be the Fourier multiplier $e^{-t\xi^4}$ on
Lebesgue $L^2(\R)$, or on Schwartz functions when appropriate.
The Fourier normalization is the angular one fixed in Section 1;
the formal sources instead use $2\pi$ frequency, with the same
physical operator. All distribution pairings below are complex
linear, without conjugating the test function.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dualpropagation}
For $s\geq0$, $T>0$ and $n\geq0$,
\[
 S_s\rho_{n,T}=\rho_{n,T+s},\qquad
 \norm{\rho_{n,T+s}}_2\leq\norm{\rho_{n,T}}_2.
\]
The map $s\mapsto\rho_{n,T+s}$ is continuous in $L^2$ and
its right derivative at $s=0$ is $-\partial_x^4\rho_{n,T}$.
There is also a continuous extension to $t=0$ as tempered
distributions with the topology of pointwise evaluation on
Schwartz tests:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dualinitial}
 R_{n,t}(\varphi)=\frac1{n!}\int_\R k_t(x)\varphi^{(n)}(x)\,dx
 \quad(t>0),\qquad
 R_{n,0}(\varphi)=\frac{\varphi^{(n)}(0)}{n!}.
\end{equation}
For positive time, $R_{n,t}$ is the distribution of the actual
Schwartz density $\rho_{n,t}$. For every fixed Schwartz test,
including the right derivative at zero,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dualgenerator}
 \frac{d}{dt}R_{n,t}(\varphi)
   =-\frac1{n!}\int k_t(x)\varphi^{(n+4)}(x)\,dx
 \quad(t>0),\qquad
 R'_{n,0+}(\varphi)=-\frac{\varphi^{(n+4)}(0)}{n!}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Fourier transformation sends $\partial_x^n$ to multiplication
by $(i\xi)^n$. It therefore commutes with $S_s$, and multiplying
$e^{-s\xi^4}$ and $e^{-T\xi^4}$ gives the first identity.
Plancherel and $|e^{-s\xi^4}|\leq1$ give the contraction.
For each fixed $T$, the Fourier transform of $\rho_{n,T}$ is
a polynomial times $e^{-T\xi^4}$. Dominated convergence proves
strong continuity, using $4|\widehat\rho_{n,T}|^2$ as a squared
majorant. For the right derivative use
\[
 \left|\frac{e^{-s\xi^4}-1}{s}\right|\leq\xi^4\quad(s>0).
\]
The squared majorant $\xi^8|\widehat\rho_{n,T}|^2$ is integrable,
and the multiplier limit is $-\xi^4$. This identifies the derivative.

Repeated integration by parts gives
$\int\varphi\rho_{n,t}=\int k_t\varphi^{(n)}/n!$, with no boundary
terms because both functions are Schwartz. For any Schwartz $g$,
Fourier inversion and evenness of the multiplier give
\[
 \int k_t g=\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-t\xi^4}\widehat g(\xi)\,d\xi
   \longrightarrow g(0).
\]
The integrable majorant is $|\widehat g|$, independently of $t$.
This proves continuity of every evaluation in
\eqref{eq:dualinitial}. Each evaluation at zero is a continuous
Schwartz functional, as follows from the defining derivative
seminorms. Thus the extension has exactly the asserted
pointwise distribution topology. No stronger dual topology
is required here.

Finally the difference quotients of the last Fourier integral
are dominated by $\xi^4|\widehat g(\xi)|$ at zero and at any
positive time. Differentiating gives the mean of $-g^{(4)}$.
Take $g=\varphi^{(n)}$ and divide by $n!$ to obtain
\eqref{eq:dualgenerator}, with the displayed signs.
\end{proof}

The initial functional is $(-1)^n\delta_0^{(n)}/n!$, since a
distributional derivative acts by $\delta_0^{(n)}(\varphi)
=(-1)^n\varphi^{(n)}(0)$. It is not an initial $L^2$ density.
For clarity, the following quantitative consequence is a human
Fourier calculation, rather than an additional mapped Lean theorem.
Plancherel in angular frequency gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:duall2scaling}
 \norm{\rho_{n,t}}_2^2
 =\frac1{2\pi(n!)^2}\int_\R \xi^{2n}e^{-2t\xi^4}\,d\xi
 =C_n t^{-(2n+1)/4},\qquad
 C_n=\frac1{2\pi(n!)^2}\int_\R y^{2n}e^{-2y^4}\,dy>0.
\end{equation}
The change of variables is $y=t^{1/4}\xi$; the last integral is
finite by exponential decay and positive on an interval avoiding
zero. Hence these densities have unbounded $L^2$ norm as
$t\downarrow0$, although every Schwartz evaluation converges.
One can also see directly why the endpoint cannot be represented
by an $L^2$ function: choose a smooth compactly supported $\psi$
with $\psi^{(n)}(0)\ne0$ and put
$\psi_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon^n\psi(x/\varepsilon)$.
Its $n$th derivative at zero is fixed, whereas
$\norm{\psi_\varepsilon}_2=\varepsilon^{n+1/2}\norm\psi_2\to0$,
contradicting the Cauchy--Schwarz bound for such a representation.

This is deterministic evolution of coordinate densities and
test functionals. It does not give bounded coordinate maps on
the completed cylinder domain, a positive path law, or a
chronological stochastic integral. The underlying fourth-order
Fourier fundamental solution is classical; for instance,
D'Ovidio~\cite{dovidio}, equations (4.1)--(4.2), gives this
equation and its inverse Fourier kernel. We use neither that
paper's subsequent multiplication assertions nor its
fractional time-change results. No novelty is claimed for
derivative propagation or the scaling calculation.

\section{The common factorial domain}\label{sec:domain}
Let $S$ be the union of the positive dyadic grid times in $(0,1]$.
A cylinder is a polynomial in finitely many formal position
coordinates $Y_s$, $s\in S$. The direct-limit construction using
increment refinement gives exactly this polynomial algebra: old
positions are retained at their original times, and the cumulative
coordinate change is invertible on each finite grid.

For a finite multiindex $\alpha:S\to\N$, let
$|\alpha|=\sum_s\alpha_s$. For a polynomial
$P=\sum_\alpha a_\alpha Y^\alpha$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:norm}
 \norm P_{\D}=\sum_\alpha |\alpha|!\,|a_\alpha|.
\end{equation}
This is the factorial of total degree, not the product of coordinate
factorials. The coefficients here are in \emph{position} coordinates,
after converting a given increment polynomial by the inverse
cumulative substitution. Refinement is an isometry because it only
retains the same position labels. It also does not increase total
degree. The norm is nondegenerate because polynomial coefficients
are unique.

Define $\D$ as the completion of this normed polynomial space.
More concretely, it is the closed span of the vectors
$(|\alpha|!a_\alpha)_\alpha$ in $\ell^1$ indexed by finitely
supported time multiindices. The Lean construction uses all times
in $(0,1]$ as an ambient index and takes the closed span of the
dyadic core. This yields the same space just described. It is a
specified analytic coefficient completion, not a space of all
bounded smooth path observables.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:operators}
For any direction $v:S\to\R$ with $|v_s|\leq1$, the polynomial
directional derivative $\partial_v=\sum_s v_s\partial_{Y_s}$
extends to $\D$ with norm at most one. Position substitutions that
send each variable to another variable or zero, without scaling
by a coefficient of modulus greater than one, are contractions.
The finite signed projection $\Pi_j$ has norm at most $e^T$ on
each cylinder norm.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a monomial of degree $d>0$, the ratio of the weighted norm of
its directional derivative to $d!$ is at most
$\sum_s\alpha_s/d=1$. The degree-zero derivative vanishes.
Summation proves the derivative bound. Substitutions preserve
total degree or lower it; merging equal monomials can only reduce
the sum of absolute coefficients.

In position coordinates, differentiation in the increment $x_j$
is the direction which is one on positions after that increment
and zero before it. Its norm is therefore at most one.
Integrating one increment is heat evolution
$e^{c\tau\partial_{x_j}^p}$ followed by setting that increment
to zero. Setting it to zero merges later positions with their
predecessors, so is contractive. The heat operator has norm at
most $e^\tau$ by its exponential series. Composing future heat
operators has norm at most $e^{\sum\tau}\leq e^T$, and the
remaining clamping substitution is contractive.
\end{proof}

Taking the empty-past projection gives a bounded signed mean
$\E_T$ on $\D$, with $\norm{\E_T}\leq e^T$ and $\E_T1=1$.
The cutoff derivative in \eqref{eq:channel} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ds}
 D_s=\partial_{v(s)},\qquad v(s)_t=1_{\{s\leq t\}}.
\end{equation}
It is contractive, as are all its powers. Applied to a fixed
polynomial, it is a finite step function of $s$. Approximation
by core polynomials shows $s\mapsto D_s^r u$ is strongly measurable
for every $u\in\D$: the applied fields converge pointwise, even
uniformly in the norm bound. This gives a bounded map into
$L^\infty(\lambda;\D)$.


\subsection*{A genuine initial-time gap in the coefficient norm}
Bounded causal projections do not supply strong continuity at time zero
in this particular completion. Write $s_n=2^{-n}$ and let $P_n$ be the
completed signed projection onto the first cell of the level-$n$ grid;
its physical cutoff is $Ts_n$. Let $P_0^{\rm empty}u=(\E_Tu)1$,
where the superscript distinguishes the empty past from the level-zero
first-cell projection. All these maps are bounded by $e^T$.
For the single fixed terminal cylinder $U=Y_1$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:initialgap}
 P_nU=Y_{s_n},\qquad P_0^{\rm empty}U=0,\qquad
 \norm{(P_n-P_0^{\rm empty})U}_{\D}=1,\qquad \norm U_{\D}=1.
\end{equation}
Here and below polynomial cylinders are identified with their
canonical embeddings into $\D$.

Every identity has an elementary finite-law proof. On the level-$n$
grid the terminal position is the sum of all increments. The projection
retains the first increment and integrates the others. Each future
increment has signed first moment zero by \eqref{eq:moments}, and
all its polynomial pairings are integrable, so the result is the first
position $Y_{s_n}$. The empty projection integrates the terminal
linear position to zero. The coefficient norm \eqref{eq:norm} of
any one position variable is exactly $1!\cdot1=1$, independent of
its time label. Refinement retains that label and is isometric.
Thus the identities persist in the completed operators, whose values
on core cylinders agree with the original projections.

It follows from \eqref{eq:initialgap} that $P_nU$ does not converge
to the empty-past value in $\D$. In particular $P_n$ fails to converge
strongly to $P_0^{\rm empty}$, and the operator norm obeys
\[
 \norm{P_n-P_0^{\rm empty}}\geq1.
\]
Nevertheless, for every fixed continuous zero-start path $\omega$,
$Y_{s_n}(\omega)=\omega(s_n)\to0$. This is pointwise path evaluation,
not convergence in the factorial coefficient norm. The same gap holds
at order $p=2$; it is not a failure caused only by signed laws.
Under the separate Brownian realization with generator $\partial_x^2$,
the first-position law has second moment $2Ts_n$, so its actual
Brownian $L^2$ norm tends to zero as well. That last norm comparison
is a human consequence of the variance convention and does not identify
the whole coefficient completion with Brownian $L^2$.

\subsection*{An actual arithmetic transform outside the one-coordinate realization}
The domain restriction can be tested on another verified family in the
same library. In one position coordinate, the norm
\eqref{eq:norm} is $\sum_{n\geq0}n!|c_n|$. Set $u_n=n!c_n$.
The corresponding one-coordinate function realization is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:factorialrealization}
 f_u(x)=\sum_{n\geq0}u_n\frac{x^n}{n!},\qquad
 u\in\ell^1(\N;\R).
\end{equation}
This is the original coefficient realization, not a replacement
Sobolev or weighted $L^2$ domain. On $|x|\leq R$, the series
and each differentiated series converge uniformly: for the $q$th
derivative, the sum of absolute terms is at most
$e^R\sum_{n\geq q}|u_n|$. Termwise differentiation is therefore
valid, and
\[
 f_u^{(q)}(0)=u_q,\qquad
 \sum_{q\geq0}|f_u^{(q)}(0)|<\infty.
\]
This condition is much stronger than being entire.

For a concrete exclusion, let $m\geq1$ be an integer, $t\in\R$,
and use the actual arithmetic theta kernel
\[
 K(v)=\sum_{j\geq1}(2\pi^2j^4e^{9v}-3\pi j^2e^{5v})
                         e^{-\pi j^2e^{4v}}\quad(v\geq0),
 \qquad
 F_{m,t}(x)=\int_0^\infty e^{tv^{2m}}K(v)\cos(xv)\,dv.
\]
The kernel is continuous and strictly positive: each summand factors as
\[
 \pi j^2e^{5v}(2\pi j^2e^{4v}-3)e^{-\pi j^2e^{4v}}>0.
\]
The series converges uniformly on compact intervals. At infinity
it is bounded by $C\exp(Cv-c e^{4v})$, so every moment remains
integrable after multiplication by $e^{tv^{2m}+R v}$ for every
fixed $m,t,R$. These bounds follow by separating half of the
decaying exponential to sum the polynomial $j$ factors. They
justify all differentiations of the integral, including
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arithmeticderivatives}
 F_{m,t}^{(2n)}(0)=(-1)^n\int_0^\infty
                   v^{2n}e^{tv^{2m}}K(v)\,dv.
\end{equation}
Continuity and positivity give
$d=\min_{2\leq v\leq3}e^{tv^{2m}}K(v)>0$. Hence the interval
of length one supplies the lower bound
\[
 |F_{m,t}^{(2n)}(0)|\geq\int_2^3 v^{2n}d\,dv\geq d\,4^n.
\]
These derivative magnitudes do not even tend to zero. Thus no
$u\in\ell^1$ can satisfy $f_u=F_{m,t}$ on the real line,
since equality of the smooth functions would identify all their
derivatives at zero, contradicting \eqref{eq:factorialrealization}.
The exact frozen theorem also records the bridge from complex
flow derivatives to this real-function derivative conclusion.

Consequently the bounded derivative and heat operators on this
one-coordinate factorial realization cannot simply be applied to
these integer-order arithmetic transforms. This is a concrete
domain obstruction, not a counterexample to the multichannel
reconstruction theorem. It neither asserts injectivity of every
completed product pairing nor excludes a different representation
in another space or quotient. No arithmetic zero conclusion or
threshold relation follows from this comparison. The coefficient
growth mechanism is elementary; no novelty is claimed for it.

\subsection{A concrete comparison with Fourier-measure tests}
Let $\mathcal A$ denote the faithful real function range in (15),
with its coefficient norm $\sum_n|u_n|$. Let $\mathcal F$ be the
real-valued functions representable as
\[
 f(x)=\int_\R e^{i\xi x}\,d\mu(\xi),\qquad
 \int_\R(1+|\xi|)^k\,d|\mu|(\xi)<\infty
 \quad\hbox{for every integer }k\geq0,
\]
where $\mu$ is a complex measure. This is the one-coordinate
Fourier-measure test class used in Nishioka's construction
\cite[Section 2]{nishioka}, restricted here to real-valued outputs.
These statements compare actual functions on the whole real line.

\begin{proposition}
Neither $\mathcal A$ nor $\mathcal F$ contains the other. More
precisely, $x\mapsto x$ belongs to $\mathcal A\setminus\mathcal F$,
and $x\mapsto\cos(\xi x)$ belongs to $\mathcal F$ for every real
$\xi$, but belongs to $\mathcal A$ exactly when $|\xi|<1$.
In the latter case its coefficient norm is $(1-\xi^2)^{-1}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The sequence $u_1=1$, $u_n=0$ otherwise realizes the polynomial
$x$ and has norm one. Every function in $\mathcal F$ is bounded
by $|\mu|(\R)$, directly from the integral and $|e^{i\xi x}|=1$.
Thus the unbounded polynomial is not in $\mathcal F$.
For any fixed $\xi$, the finite atomic measure
$(\delta_\xi+\delta_{-\xi})/2$ has every required weighted moment
and represents $\cos(\xi x)$. Its derivatives at zero are zero
in odd orders and $(-1)^j\xi^{2j}$ in order $2j$.
Faithful derivative recovery therefore forces these exact
coefficients for any realization in $\mathcal A$.
Their absolute sum is $\sum_{j\geq0}|\xi|^{2j}$, finite exactly
when $|\xi|<1$, with the claimed sum. When finite, the resulting
factorial series is the ordinary cosine series, proving sufficiency
as well as necessity.
\end{proof}
The frequency threshold belongs to this fixed coefficient norm;
it is not invariant under a rescaling of that norm. This elementary
comparison rules out either identity inclusion for these faithful
one-coordinate function spaces. It does not rule out other
multivariate presentations, quotient representations or bounded
maps on smaller common domains. In particular it neither identifies
the two weak reconstruction topologies nor proves priority for the
completed multichannel theorem.

\section{The product pairing and its signed character}
Ordinary multiplication does not have norm at most the product
of the norms in \eqref{eq:norm}. Instead define on polynomials
\[
 B(P,Q)(Y)=P(Y/2)Q(Y/2).
\]
For monomials of degrees $d,e$, its weighted norm ratio is
$2^{-d-e}(d+e)!/(d!e!)\leq1$. Summing absolute coefficients
gives $\norm{B(P,Q)}\leq\norm P\norm Q$, so $B$ extends
as a bounded symmetric bilinear map $\D\times\D\to\D$.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairing}
 J_T(u)(v)=\E_{2^pT}\big(B(u,v)\big).
\end{equation}
Then $\norm{J_T}\leq e^{2^pT}$. Homogeneity of the moments shows
that half-dilation at horizon $2^pT$ restores horizon $T$:
$\mu_r(2^p\tau)2^{-r}=\mu_r(\tau)$.
Applying this to each increment monomial proves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:actualpair}
 J_T(P)(G)=\E_T(PG)
\end{equation}
for every pair of cylinders, and $J_T(u)(1)=\E_Tu$ by continuity.
This construction supplies the bounded product \emph{pairing}
without assuming bounded ordinary multiplication.

There is an explicit obstruction to any bounded ordinary-product
extension. Fix one dyadic coordinate $Y=Y_s$ and let
$P_n=Y^n/n!$. By \eqref{eq:norm},
\[
 \norm{P_n}_{\D}=1,\qquad \norm{Y}_{\D}=1,
 \qquad \norm{YP_n}_{\D}=n+1.
\]
Thus even multiplication by this fixed polynomial $Y$ has no
bounded linear extension agreeing with the polynomial products.
In particular no bounded bilinear map on $\D\times\D$ can agree
with ordinary multiplication on every polynomial pair.

The bounded replacement $B$ has a different limitation: it is
commutative but not associative. On the same faithful polynomial
core,
\[
 B(1,1)=1,\qquad B(1,Y)=Y/2,
\]
and consequently
\[
 B(B(1,1),Y)=Y/2\ne Y/4=B(1,B(1,Y)).
\]
The two outputs are distinct since their coefficient norm
difference is $1/4$. These are direct human consequences of the
printed core formulas, not new named formal theorems. They rule
out treating this damped operation as a Banach-algebra product.
The construction of $J_T$ uses $B$ once and retains the explicit
horizon correction; it requires no associativity of $B$.

\section{The bounded channel operators}
For $A\in\mathcal F$ and $v\in\D$, the integrand
$A(s)(D_s^r v)$ is integrable because its absolute value is at
most $\norm{A(s)}\norm v$. Strong measurability follows from
the bounded bilinear evaluation map and the applied-field
measurability proved above. Define
\[
 Q_r(A)(v)=\int_0^1 A(s)(D_s^r v)\,ds.
\]
Then $Q_r:\mathcal F\to\D^*$ is bounded with norm at most one.
In addition to the surviving channels in \eqref{eq:channel}, use
the same formula for $q=p$. In that case
\begin{equation}\label{eq:driftchannel}
 I_p=cp!T Q_0.
\end{equation}
It is a drift channel, which will cancel exactly after centering.
For $1\leq q\leq p$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ibound}
 \norm{I_q A}\leq pT(p-1)_{q-1}\norm A_{\mathcal F}.
\end{equation}

For recursive calculations put
$K_k=cpT\binom{p-1}{k}Q_{p-1-k}$, $0\leq k<p$.
Thus $I_q=(q-1)!K_{q-1}$.
The factorial distinction is needed: the native Taylor coefficient
contains $1/q!$, whereas conversion of a power channel contributes
$(q-1)!$. Losing either factor changes the reconstruction formula.

\section{Exact finite predictable innovations}
Fix a grid and a polynomial $P$ of total degree at most $d$.
For cell $j$, put $Q_j=\Pi_{j+1}P$, and let $R_j$ set $x_i=0$
for $i\geq j$. Define the native coefficients
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativejet}
 a_{j,q}(P)=\frac1{q!}R_j\partial_{x_j}^q Q_j.
\end{equation}
They depend only on strict-past increments, vanish for $q>d$,
and Lemma~\ref{lem:operators} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jetbound}
 \norm{a_{j,q}(P)}_{\D}\leq\frac{e^T}{q!}\norm P_{\D}.
\end{equation}
Only the current and past increments survive in $Q_j$.
Its exact Taylor expansion in the current increment therefore is
$Q_j=\sum_{q=0}^d a_{j,q}(P)x_j^q$.
Integrate that increment to obtain
$\Pi_jP=\sum_{q=0}^d a_{j,q}(P)\mu_q(Th)$.
Subtracting proves the cell identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cell}
 \Pi_{j+1}P-\Pi_jP
 =\sum_{q=1}^d a_{j,q}(P)\big(x_j^q-\mu_q(Th)\big).
\end{equation}
The zeroth term vanishes because $\mu_0=1$.
Summing over cells telescopes exactly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:telescope}
 P-\E_TP=\sum_{q=1}^d\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
 a_{j,q}(P)\big(x_j^q-\mu_q(Th)\big).
\end{equation}
No term has been discarded on a scaling heuristic. Every integral
in this derivation is an actual finite signed polynomial integral.

Refine a fixed base polynomial $P$ to successive grids and denote
its refinement by $P_n$. Its position-coordinate norm is unchanged
and its total degree stays at most $d$. Define the native field
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativefield}
 A_{n,q}(P)=\sum_j W_j\big(a_{j,q}(P_n)\big).
\end{equation}
Since cell lengths sum to one, \eqref{eq:jetbound} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fieldbound}
 \norm{A_{n,q}(P)}_{\mathcal F}
 \leq\frac{\norm{J_T}e^T}{q!}\norm P_{\D}.
\end{equation}
This estimate is independent of degree and mesh, but does not by
itself prove that the sequence converges. That requires the next
two quantitative arguments.

\section{Signed power estimates in the test dual}\label{sec:powers}
For strict-past $H$ and integer $r\geq0$, signed integration by
parts gives the exact dual identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power}
 J_T(Hx_j^{r+1})
 =\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(r)_k K_k\big(W_j(Hx_j^{r-k})\big).
\end{equation}
Terms with $k>r$ vanish and can be omitted. To check the identity,
test first against a polynomial $G$ on a common finer grid.
The coarse increment is the sum of fine increments in its cell.
Apply \eqref{eq:stein} to each fine increment and use Leibniz's
rule on $x_j^rG$. Derivatives of $H$ vanish because it is strict
past. The $k$ derivatives on $x_j^r$ give $(r)_k x_j^{r-k}$,
the other derivatives give $D_s^{p-1-k}G$, and summing the fine
durations gives exactly the cell integral in $K_k$.
This proves \eqref{eq:power} on every polynomial test. Density and
continuity give the identity in $\D^*$.

Define nonnegative constants recursively by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:powerconstants}
 B_0=1,\qquad B_{r+1}=pT\sum_{k=0}^{\min(r,p-1)}
 \binom{p-1}{k}(r)_k B_{r-k}.
\end{equation}
They depend on $p,T,r$, not on the mesh or $H$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:powerbounds}
For a strict-past polynomial $H$ and a cell of normalized length
$h\leq1$,
\begin{align}
 \norm{J_T(Hx_j^r)}&\leq B_r\norm{J_T(H)},\label{eq:uniformpower}\\
 \norm{J_T(Hx_j^q)}&\leq B_qh\norm{J_T(H)}\quad(q\geq1),\label{eq:positivepower}\\
 \norm{J_T(Hx_j^q)}&\leq B_qh^2\norm{J_T(H)}\quad(q>p).\label{eq:highpower}
\end{align}
For $1\leq q\leq p$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:powererror}
 \norm{J_T(Hx_j^q)-I_q(W_jH)}
 \leq B_qh^2\norm{J_T(H)}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The channel bound for a step is
$\norm{K_k(W_jV)}\leq pT\binom{p-1}{k}h\norm{J_T(V)}$.
Apply it in \eqref{eq:power} and induct on $r+1$, using
\eqref{eq:powerconstants}. This first proves
\eqref{eq:positivepower}; since $h\leq1$, it also proves
\eqref{eq:uniformpower} and closes the induction.
For $q>p$, every surviving index $k\leq p-1$ has $q-1-k\geq1$.
Use \eqref{eq:positivepower} on that residual power in each term
of \eqref{eq:power}. The additional step length supplies $h^2$,
proving \eqref{eq:highpower}.
For $q\leq p$, the last term $k=q-1$ is exactly
$(q-1)!K_{q-1}(W_jH)=I_q(W_jH)$.
Every other term has a positive residual power, so the same
argument bounds their sum by $B_qh^2\norm{J_T(H)}$.
\end{proof}

By \eqref{eq:moments}, $\mu_q(Th)=0$ for $1\leq q<p$ and
$\mu_p(Th)=cp!Th$. For $q>p$, $|\mu_q(Th)|\leq M_qh^2$
with a mesh-independent $M_q$: if $q=pk$, take
$M_q=q!T^k/k!$ and note $k\geq2$; otherwise take zero.
Thus summing over $N=1/h$ cells, under any uniform bound
$\norm{J_T(H_j)}\leq M$, gives the following three limits:
\begin{align}
 J_T\left(\sum_jH_j(x_j^q-\mu_q(Th))\right)
 &\longrightarrow I_q(A),&&1\leq q<p,\label{eq:lowl}\\
 J_T\left(\sum_jH_j(x_j^p-\mu_p(Th))\right)
 &\longrightarrow0,\label{eq:criticall}\\
 J_T\left(\sum_jH_j(x_j^q-\mu_q(Th))\right)
 &\longrightarrow0,&&q>p,\label{eq:highl}
\end{align}
where the first line also assumes $\sum_jW_jH_j\to A$ in
$\mathcal F$. The errors are $O(h)M$ in dual norm.
For the critical line, \eqref{eq:driftchannel} gives
$I_p(W_jH_j)=cp!ThJ_T(H_j)$, which cancels the centering exactly.
This is why the final centered reconstruction has $p-1$ channels.

\section{Causal coefficients and convergence under refinement}
It remains to prove the field convergence needed in
\eqref{eq:lowl}. Introduce the causal comparison coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{eq:causaljet}
 b_{j,q}(P)=\frac1{q!}\Pi_j(\partial_{x_j}^qP),
\end{equation}
and its step field $\widetilde A_{n,q}=\sum_j W_j(b_{j,q}(P_n))$.
The derivative here is taken before the strict-past projection.
It does not in general commute through that projection.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:heatshift}
For every grid polynomial,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heatshift}
 b_{j,q}(P)=\frac1{q!}R_j
 e^{cTh\partial_{x_j}^p}\partial_{x_j}^q\Pi_{j+1}P.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for a fixed cylinder under refinement,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heaterror}
 \norm{\widetilde A_{n,q}-A_{n,q}}_{\mathcal F}
 \leq\frac{\norm{J_T}e^T}{q!}(e^{Th}-1)\norm P_{\D}
 \longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The current coordinate is retained by $\Pi_{j+1}$, so its derivative
commutes with the future integrations. Integrating that one current
coordinate after future conditioning is exactly heat evolution
followed by clamping, by \eqref{eq:heat}. This proves
\eqref{eq:heatshift}. The norm of
$e^{cTh\partial_{x_j}^p}-\mathrm{id}$ is at most $e^{Th}-1$.
Use contractivity of derivatives and clamping, the $e^T$ projection
bound, and the sum of normalized cell lengths equal to one.
\end{proof}

The heat correction proves equality of the eventual native and
causal limits, but does not itself prove that either sequence has
a limit. For that we need control of a moving conditioning cut.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cut}
For each degree bound $d$, there is $L_d\geq0$, depending only
on $p,T,d$, such that on every grid and every degree-at-most-$d$
polynomial $P$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cut}
 \norm{J_T(\Pi_bP-\Pi_aP)}
 \leq L_d(b-a)h\norm P_{\D},\qquad0\leq a\leq b\leq N.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For each $q\geq1$ there is $V_q\geq0$ with
$|\mu_q(Th)|\leq V_qh$: use zero if $p\nmid q$, and
$V_q=q!T^k/k!$ if $q=pk$, since $h^k\leq h$.
The cell identity \eqref{eq:cell}, the positive power estimate,
and \eqref{eq:jetbound} imply
\[
 \norm{J_T(\Pi_{j+1}P-\Pi_jP)}
 \leq h\norm P_{\D}\norm{J_T}e^T
       \sum_{q=1}^d\frac{B_q+V_q}{q!}.
\]
Take the finite nonnegative sum as $L_d$, and sum the cell
differences from $a$ to $b-1$ by the triangle inequality.
The argument proves dual-norm continuity of conditional increments;
it does not assert continuity of moving-time positions in the
strong factorial source norm.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:fieldlimit}
For each fixed cylinder $P$ and every $q\geq0$, the native fields
$A_{n,q}(P)$ converge in $\mathcal F$ to a field $C_qP$.
The causal fields converge to the same limit, and the bound
\eqref{eq:Cbound} holds on the core.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Compare adjacent grids. Each coarse increment splits into two
fine increments. Differentiating the pulled-back polynomial in
either fine coordinate is the pullback of differentiating in the
coarse coordinate. Its degree stays at most $d=\deg P$, and its
factorial norm after any number of such derivatives is at most
$\norm P_{\D}$.

At the first half of a coarse cell, the strict-past projections
agree by signed convolution consistency. At the second half,
the fine conditioning cut has advanced by just one fine cell.
Apply \eqref{eq:cut} to the derivative polynomial. Dividing by
$q!$ and integrating over the unit normalized horizon gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjacent}
 \norm{\widetilde A_{n+1,q}-\widetilde A_{n,q}}_{\mathcal F}
 \leq\frac{L_d}{q!}2^{-(n+1)}\norm P_{\D}.
\end{equation}
This bound is summable in $n$. Thus the causal fields are Cauchy
in the complete space $\mathcal F$ and converge. Equation
\eqref{eq:heaterror} gives the same limit for the native fields.
The norm bound follows by passing to the limit in
\eqref{eq:fieldbound}. Each native field is an adapted polynomial
step field; closedness gives $C_qP\in\mathcal A_T$.
\end{proof}

For $q>d$ all native fields are zero, hence $C_qP=0$.
If two base-grid presentations define the same cylinder, their
refinements agree on a common tail. Uniqueness of norm limits
therefore makes the limit independent of the presentation.
Finite-grid linearity and continuity of addition and scalar
multiplication make $P\mapsto C_qP$ linear. These observations
are necessary before extending the maps to completed observables.

\section{The multichannel formula and observable completion}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:polyformula}
Every polynomial cylinder satisfies
\[
 J_T(P-\E_TP)=\sum_{q=1}^{p-1}I_q(C_qP).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Refine the fixed polynomial to all sufficiently fine grids.
Choose $d'\geq\max(\deg P,p-1)$ once and pad the exact telescope
\eqref{eq:telescope} to degree $d'$ with zero coefficients.
Refinement preserves the degree bound, so this is the same finite
number of terms on every grid. The left side, after applying
$J_T$, is the same dual element on each grid.

For each $q$, \eqref{eq:jetbound} gives a uniform bound on the
paired native coefficients. Proposition~\ref{prop:fieldlimit}
provides the $L^1$ convergence for the low powers.
Apply \eqref{eq:lowl} to $q<p$, the exact critical cancellation
\eqref{eq:criticall} to $q=p$, and \eqref{eq:highl} to $q>p$.
Pass through the one fixed finite sum. The surviving terms are
exactly those in the displayed identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
The degree-independent bound \eqref{eq:Cbound} on the dense core
extends each linear coefficient map uniquely to a bounded linear
map $C_q:\D\to\mathcal F$ with the same bound. For a completed
observable choose polynomials $P_k\to u$ in $\D$.
Then $C_qP_k\to C_qu$. Closedness of $\mathcal A_T$ preserves
adapted membership. The centered map
$u\mapsto J_T(u-\E_T(u)1)$ is bounded linear, as is each $I_qC_q$.
Thus Proposition~\ref{prop:polyformula} passes to the limit and
proves \eqref{eq:main} for every $u\in\D$.

For the simultaneous actual signed approximation, assemble the
$p-1$ native fields and the native surviving power sum into one
finite product Banach space. For each fixed $P_k$, the preceding
arguments show convergence of that package as the mesh tends
to zero. Its limiting package is the collection
$(C_1P_k,\ldots,C_{p-1}P_k,J_T(P_k-\E_TP_k))$,
which tends to the analogous package for $u$ by continuity.
Choose a mesh index $n_k$ at least $k$ and at least the base-grid
index of $P_k$, large enough that the package error is less than
$1/(k+1)$. The triangle inequality gives simultaneous convergence
to the package for $u$. Every approximating sum comes from the
actual finite signed polynomial laws by \eqref{eq:actualpair}.
\end{proof}

The diagonal is a proved selection; it is not every arbitrary
joint degree/mesh limit. The convergence constants for one fixed
polynomial may depend on its degree, as $L_d$ shows. The extension
to $\D$ instead uses the degree-independent coefficient bound.
Those two kinds of bounds have distinct roles in the proof.

\section{Uniqueness and the biharmonic case}
The lower derivative orders cannot all be replaced by a coefficient
depending only on the $p$th derivative. The terminal cylinder
$P=Y_1$ has $D_s^pP=0$ for every $s$, and $\E_TP=0$.
Nevertheless
\[
 J_T(P)(Y_1^{p-1})=\E_T(Y_1^p)=cp!T\neq0.
\]
Thus any proposed reconstruction which vanishes whenever the
$p$th derivative vanishes fails on this actual signed polynomial
pairing. This elementary obstruction explains the role of lower
channels without invoking a positive pseudo-$L^2$ interpretation.

If another bounded linear coefficient map agrees with $C_q$ on
all canonical polynomial limits, it equals $C_q$ by density.
This proves uniqueness of the coefficient extension.
It does not assert that an arbitrary channel representation has
unique integrands. Let
\[
 S:(\mathcal F)^{p-1}\to\D^*,\qquad
 S(A_1,\ldots,A_{p-1})=\sum_{q=1}^{p-1}I_qA_q.
\]
Then $S(A)=S(B)$ if and only if $A-B\in\ker S$.
No theorem here sets that kernel equal to zero.

For $p=4$ the three surviving channels are
\begin{align*}
 (I_1A)(v)&=-4T\int_0^1 A(s)(D_s^3v)\,ds,\\
 (I_2A)(v)&=-12T\int_0^1 A(s)(D_s^2v)\,ds,\\
 (I_3A)(v)&=-24T\int_0^1 A(s)(D_sv)\,ds.
\end{align*}
The native coefficient fields retain factors $1$, $1/2!$, and
$1/3!$ respectively. The fourth channel is the drift
$I_4=-24TQ_0$ and cancels against the fourth moment centering.
This example keeps the signs, factorials, and complementary
test derivative orders visible.

For $p=2$ the same construction has one surviving channel
$I_1=2TQ_1$, with the kernel normalization of generator

\section{Why the reconstruction stays in the test dual}\label{sec:signed-obstructions}

\subsection{A finite-variation path-law obstruction}
Assume $p=2m>2$. The moment formula~\eqref{eq:moments} gives
$\int x^2k_\tau(x)dx=0$, while $\int k_\tau=1$.
If $k_\tau\geq0$ almost everywhere, the nonnegative integrable
function $x^2k_\tau(x)$ would vanish almost everywhere. Since
Lebesgue measure has no atom at zero, this would force $k_\tau=0$
almost everywhere, contradicting its unit mass. Thus the kernel is
not nonnegative. Writing $k_\tau=k_\tau^+-k_\tau^-$ shows
\[
 \int|k_\tau|=1+2\int k_\tau^->1.
\]
Every integral is finite because the kernel is Schwartz.

The inverse Fourier integral gives the exact scaling
\begin{equation}\label{eq:signed-scaling}
 k_\tau(x)=\tau^{-1/p}k_1(x\tau^{-1/p}),\qquad
 A_p:=\int_\R|k_\tau(x)|dx=\int_\R|k_1(x)|dx>1.
\end{equation}
Indeed substitute $\xi=\tau^{-1/p}\eta$ in its absolutely convergent
inverse transform and then change variables in the absolute integral.
Thus the absolute mass does not become small with the time step.
For $N$ increments on a fixed horizon $T$, the actual signed product
law $\mu_N(dx)=\prod_{j=1}^Nk_{T/N}(x_j)dx_j$ satisfies, by Tonelli,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TVproduct}
 |\mu_N|(\R^N)=\prod_{j=1}^N\int|k_{T/N}|=A_p^N.
\end{equation}

Suppose a countably additive signed measure $\nu$ of finite total
variation on any measurable space had measurable $N$-increment
observations with these pushforward laws for every $N$. For every
measurable map $F$, $|F_\#\nu|(\text{whole space})\leq
|\nu|(\text{whole space})$. To prove this inequality, pull back any
finite measurable partition used in the variation supremum; the sum
of the absolute signed masses is bounded by the total variation of
$\nu$. Applying it to these observations would give
$A_p^N\leq|\nu|(\text{whole space})$ for all $N$, impossible.
In particular there is no such finite signed path law on $[0,T]$
realizing all the indicated uniform-grid increment distributions.
No path continuity or consistency of different observation maps was
needed for this contradiction.

The formal path-obstruction declaration uses arbitrarily many
successive increments at a fixed positive step, hence increasing
horizons. The fixed-horizon refinement conclusion just proved is
an explicit classical deduction combining the same variation
contraction with exact kernel scaling. It is not misattributed to
a different formal path declaration. For $p=2$, $A_2=1$ and this
obstruction does not apply. It concerns finite total variation;
it does not preclude generalized integration or the bounded
test-dual construction proved above. The classical obstruction is
also discussed in the primary introduction of~\cite{bcm}.

\subsection{Unbounded means on continuous cylinder unit balls}
The variation obstruction also has a direct functional form which does
not presume a measure representation theorem. Fix $p=2m>2$ and $T>0$.
Let $\mathcal C$ be the algebra of bounded continuous dyadic cylinder
functions on continuous zero-start paths, with their path supremum
norm. This is a different test class from the unbounded polynomial
cylinders used to construct $\D$. Its finite signed expectation is
well defined by the same actual increment laws and their refinement
consistency. Then for every real $R$ there is $F\in\mathcal C$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuousunit}
 \|F\|_\infty\leq1,\qquad \E_TF>R.
\end{equation}
Consequently no bounded linear functional on this cylinder algebra,
its sup-norm closure, or the whole bounded-continuous path space can
agree with all these expectations.

Here is a proof with actual continuous tests. On a grid of $N=2^n$
increments of duration $h=T/N$, write
$\rho_N(x)=\prod_{j=1}^Nk_h(x_j)$ on $\R^N$.
This density is continuous and integrable, and
\[
 \int_{\R^N}|\rho_N(x)|\,dx=A_p^N,
 \qquad A_p>1
\]
by the scaling and product calculation above. For $\varepsilon>0$
the soft sign
\[
 g_\varepsilon(x)=
       \frac{\rho_N(x)}{|\rho_N(x)|+\varepsilon}
\]
is continuous and bounded in absolute value by one, and
\[
 \int\rho_Ng_\varepsilon
   =\int\frac{\rho_N^2}{|\rho_N|+\varepsilon}
       \longrightarrow\int|\rho_N|=A_p^N.
\]
The integrand tends pointwise to $|\rho_N|$, including at its zeros,
and is dominated by that integrable function, which proves the limit.
Choose a dyadic $N$ with $A_p^N>R$ and then a sufficiently small
$\varepsilon$ with the displayed integral greater than $R$.
Compose $g_\varepsilon$ with the continuous map taking a path to
its successive grid increments. The resulting $F$ is an actual
bounded continuous cylinder with norm at most one. Its signed
expectation is the displayed integral, proving
\eqref{eq:continuousunit}. If a bounded agreeing functional had norm
$C$, choose $R>C$ to contradict its unit-ball bound.

This is a sup-norm obstruction on bounded continuous tests, distinct
both from the finite-variation path-measure obstruction and from the
later compact-open mean nonclosability example on unbounded
polynomials. It does not contradict bounded reconstruction on the
specified factorial domain. At Gaussian order $p=2$, $A_p=1$ and
this growth argument does not apply.

\subsection{Signed quartic cancellation versus absolute error}
Take the biharmonic order $p=4$, so $c=-1$, and put $h=T/N$.
For the actual law $\mu_N$ above define
$Q_N(x)=\sum_{j=1}^N x_j^4$. Its pointwise value is nonnegative,
but its signed mean is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negativequartic}
 \int Q_N\,d\mu_N=N(-24h)=-24T.
\end{equation}
The one-increment moments are
$\int x^4k_h=-24h$ and $\int x^8k_h=8!h^2/2=20160h^2$.
Product integration, justified by absolute polynomial integrability,
therefore gives
\begin{align*}
 \int Q_N^2\,d\mu_N
 &=20160Nh^2+576N(N-1)h^2\\
 &=576T^2+19584T^2/N.
\end{align*}
Expanding the centered square and using~\eqref{eq:negativequartic}
yields the exact cancellation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:signedquarticerror}
 \int(Q_N+24T)^2\,d\mu_N=19584T^2/N\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
This is a signed integral of a square, not a norm-convergence statement.
In fact $Q_N+24T\geq24T$ everywhere. Consequently the actual absolute
error has the opposite behavior:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:absolutequarticerror}
 \int|Q_N+24T|^2\,d|\mu_N|
 \geq576T^2 A_4^N\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
Each integral is finite, since the product variation has every
polynomial moment. The absolute error even has the lower bound
$576T^2$ under the normalized variation probability
$|\mu_N|/A_4^N$. Thus neither ordinary probability convergence to
$-24T$ under those probability laws nor an $L^2$ isometry can be
read from~\eqref{eq:signedquarticerror}. The signed identity and the
divergent variation error concern precisely the same native sums.

\subsection{An $L^2$-small deterministic control with a fixed law}\label{sec:fixed-law-control}
The proper control scale can be tested before any completed observable
calculus. If $a$ is constant on finitely many disjoint time intervals
of lengths $h_j>0$, define its biharmonic signed integral law by the
pushforward of the product increment law under $\sum_j a_jx_j$.
Multiplying the native Fourier transforms proves
\[
 \int e^{i\xi y}\,d\mathcal L_a(y)
   =\exp\left(-\xi^4\sum_jh_ja_j^4\right).
\]
The sum is the fourth-power control clock, not its second-power norm.
On $[0,1]$ take the actual step functions
\[
 a_n(s)=(n+1)1_{(0,(n+1)^{-4}]}(s),\qquad n\geq0.
\]
They belong to both $L^2$ and $L^4$, and exactly
\[
 \|a_n\|_2=(n+1)^{-1}\longrightarrow0,\qquad
 \int_0^1 a_n^4=1.
\]
Thus $\widehat{\mathcal L_{a_n}}(1)=e^{-1}$ for every $n$, whereas
the zero control gives the Dirac law with Fourier value one.
Any scalar functional on $L^2$ agreeing with these native Fourier
values and equal to one at zero is discontinuous at zero, by this
explicit convergent sequence. A weakly continuous law extension
agreeing with the native laws is likewise impossible, since the
real and imaginary parts of $e^{iy}$ are bounded continuous tests.
This concerns a plain $L^2$ input norm for the fourth-order integral;
it does not contradict the factorial observable-domain theorem or
an extension on a stronger fourth-power control norm.

The formal source uses Fourier phases $e^{2\pi i\xi y}$, so its
unit-frequency witness is $e^{-(2\pi)^4}$ rather than $e^{-1}$.
We use the angular-frequency convention fixed at the start of this
paper. These are the same physical signed laws at correspondingly
rescaled frequencies, with the same strict failure of continuity.

\subsection{The last complementary derivative channel is essential}
Return to the analytic completion and assume $p>2$. Let $X$ be the
terminal coordinate polynomial at horizon $T$. On the polynomial
core its signed mean and pairing satisfy
\[
 \E_T(X^{p-1})=0,\qquad J_T(X^{p-1})(X)=p!cT\ne0.
\]
Both follow directly from~\eqref{eq:moments}.
For $1\leq q\leq p-2$ one has $p-q\geq2$. Since $X$ is linear,
$D_s^{p-q}X=0$ for every $s$, and the channel definition
\eqref{eq:channel} gives
\[
 (I_q A)(X)=0\qquad\text{for every admissible field }A.
\]
Every sum of those channels annihilates $X$, whereas the centered
pairing of $X^{p-1}$ does not. Hence those channels alone cannot
reconstruct that observable. In particular the first channel alone
fails. In the full $p-1$ formula, only its last channel can contribute
to this particular terminal test. This proves necessity of the last
complementary-derivative channel for the displayed reconstruction;
it does not prove an arbitrary general minimality theorem or
injectivity of channel synthesis. The collective obstruction is also formalized in
\code{HigherHeatMultichannelSharpness.all_lower_channels_insufficient}.
The source uses zero-based channel indexing $r=q-1$.


\subsection{Compact-open path convergence does not close the mean}
The norm in Section~\ref{sec:domain} is substantive. Consider instead
$\mathcal P=\{\omega\in C(\R,\R):\omega(0)=0\}$ and the space
$C(\mathcal P,\R)$ with uniform convergence on compact subsets of
$\mathcal P$. Dyadic polynomial cylinders have a faithful realization
in this space. Indeed each dyadic evaluation map is continuous; every
finite list of prescribed values is realized by a piecewise linear
path starting at zero. Equality of two realized cylinder polynomials
therefore gives equality of the finite polynomials after passage to
a common refinement. Thus the original cylinder mean defines an
unambiguous linear functional on this realized polynomial algebra.
The path coordinate is normalized time: $U(\omega)=\omega(1)$
has the original terminal signed moments at physical horizon $T$.

Set $a=cT\ne0$ and, for $n\geq0$, form the actual cylinder
\[
 F_n(\omega)=\frac{n!}{(pn)!a^n}U(\omega)^{pn}.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} gives $\E_T(F_n)=1$ exactly.
Nevertheless $F_n\to0$ uniformly on every compact subset of
$\mathcal P$. To prove this, for compact $K\subset\mathcal P$,
continuity of $U$ gives $|U|\leq R$ on $K$ for a finite $R\geq0$.
The integer inequality $(pn)!\geq(n!)^2$ holds for $p\geq2$:
$\binom{2n}{n}\geq1$ gives $(2n)!\geq(n!)^2$ and factorials
increase from $2n$ to $pn$. Consequently
\[
 \sup_{\omega\in K}|F_n(\omega)|
 \leq\frac{(R^p/|a|)^n}{n!}\longrightarrow0.
\]
For the limit, if $A=R^p/|a|$, the consecutive ratio of
$A^n/n!$ is $A/(n+1)$, eventually at most $1/2$; the zero case
is immediate. This proves the compact-open convergence.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:compactopenmean}
The realized polynomial mean has no closed linear partial-operator
extension from $C(\mathcal P,\R)$ to $\mathbb C$ with the
compact-open input topology. In particular its graph is not closable
in that topology.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Its graph contains $(F_n,1)$, which converges to $(0,1)$ by the
proved uniform-on-compacts limit. Every closed graph containing the
polynomial graph must therefore contain $(0,1)$. The graph of a
linear operator also contains $(0,0)$ and has only one value at
zero. This is impossible. In particular the closure of the original
graph cannot be the graph of a linear partial operator.
\end{proof}
This obstruction holds already at $m=1$, where the kernel is the
positive Gaussian heat law of variance $2T$. It therefore does not
prove absence of a probability representation. Compact-open control
of unbounded polynomials does not control their integrals, even in
that positive case. It also does not contradict the bounded mean on
the stronger factorial analytic completion: this sequence is not
asserted to converge in the norm of that completion. No claim about
expectations of uniformly bounded path functions follows from this
particular sequence.

\subsection{One starting point does not separate Schwartz tests}
A different testing distinction survives even at fixed positive time.
The nonzero real Schwartz function $f(x)=xe^{-x^2}$ is odd, whereas
every $k_t$ in this paper is even. Their product is integrable:
$f$ is bounded and $k_t\in L^1$, or one may use Schwartz decay.
Changing $x$ to $-x$ then gives
\[
 \int_\R f(x)k_t(x)\,dx=0\qquad\hbox{for every }t>0,
 \qquad f(1)=e^{-1}>0.
\]
Thus all zero-start heat marginals together do not separate the
Schwartz class. This is an elementary parity obstruction, not a
failure of Fourier injectivity or a proof that the completed
analytic pairing $J_T$ is noninjective.

In fact all starting points at one fixed positive time do separate
Schwartz functions. If $f\in\mathcal S(\R,\mathbb C)$ satisfies
\[
 \int_\R f(x+y)k_t(y)\,dy=0\qquad\hbox{for every }x\in\R,
\]
then evenness of $k_t$ identifies this function with $k_t*f$.
Both factors are Schwartz, and the convolution Fourier formula gives
\[
 e^{-t\xi^p}\widehat f(\xi)=0\qquad(\xi\in\R)
\]
in the angular-frequency convention of Section 1. The multiplier
never vanishes, so $\widehat f=0$ pointwise and Fourier inversion
gives $f=0$. This proves separation without a bounded inverse:
dividing by $e^{-t\xi^p}$ need not preserve the Schwartz class
for an arbitrary proposed output. These topology and testing
obstructions use classical factorial and Fourier mechanisms; no
new general extension principle is claimed.

\subsection{A separate Schwartz observable identity for actual $L^p$ controls}
The $L^2$ discontinuity test of Section~\ref{sec:fixed-law-control} does not exclude a
control theory in the correct power norm. The frozen library also
constructs the following deterministic-control identity. It uses
Schwartz terminal tests, separately from the factorial multitime
completion in Theorem 1.1; no inclusion of these domains is assumed.
Fix $T\geq0$, $p=2m$, $c=(-1)^{m+1}$ and a real
$u\in L^p([0,T])$. Set
\[
 A_u(t)=\int_0^t|u(s)|^pds\quad(0\leq t\leq T),\qquad
 \nu_{u,t}=\begin{cases}k_{A_u(t)}(x)\,dx,&A_u(t)>0,\\
                         \delta_0,&A_u(t)=0.
             \end{cases}
\]
These are actual finite signed measures of mass one. The clock is
continuous, nondecreasing and absolutely continuous, with derivative
$|u(t)|^p$ almost everywhere. Its definition is unchanged when $u$
is replaced by an equal almost-everywhere representative.
For $f\in\mathcal S(\R,\mathbb C)$ write
$\mathsf E_u(t)f=\int f\,d\nu_{u,t}$, an actual signed integral.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:lpclock}
For every such $u,f$ the scalar curve $t\mapsto\mathsf E_u(t)f$
is absolutely continuous and satisfies
\[
 \mathsf E_u(T)f-f(0)
   =\int_0^T|u(t)|^p c\,\mathsf E_u(t)f^{(p)}\,dt.
\]
The drift is absolutely integrable, bounded in modulus by
$M_f|u(t)|^p$, where in angular frequencies
\[
 M_f=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R |\xi|^p|\widehat f(\xi)|\,d\xi<\infty.
\]
The derivative identity holds almost everywhere, including times
of zero accumulated clock.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Schwartz Fourier inversion and the actual kernel transform give,
for $r\geq0$ including $r=0$,
\[
 F_f(r)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R\widehat f(\xi)e^{-r\xi^p}d\xi,
 \qquad \mathsf E_u(t)f=F_f(A_u(t)).
\]
For $r>0$ the signed-kernel equality is justified by absolute
Fubini, since $f$ is bounded, the kernel is integrable and
$\widehat f\in L^1$. At $r=0$ Fourier inversion gives $F_f(0)=f(0)$,
agreeing with $\delta_0$. Differentiating under the frequency
integral gives a continuous right derivative at zero and an
ordinary continuous derivative for $r>0$:
\[
 F_f'(r)=-\frac1{2\pi}\int\xi^p\widehat f(\xi)e^{-r\xi^p}d\xi
              =cF_{f^{(p)}}(r),\qquad |F_f'(r)|\leq M_f.
\]
Domination uses $|\xi|^p|\widehat f(\xi)|$, valid for every
$r\geq0$; the sign follows from $c(i\xi)^p=-\xi^p$.
Extend $F_f$ to negative arguments by
$F_f(r)=f(0)+rF_f'(0)$ for $r<0$. This is a genuine $C^1$ function
on $\R$, with derivative bounded by $M_f$, agreeing with the
heat expectation for nonnegative arguments. It is an auxiliary
linear extension, not backward heat evolution.

The extended function is globally Lipschitz. Composition with the
absolutely continuous clock is absolutely continuous; the chain
rule at almost every clock differentiability point gives
\[
 \frac{d}{dt}\mathsf E_u(t)f
     =|u(t)|^p c\,\mathsf E_u(t)f^{(p)}.
\]
The $C^1$ extension makes this chain rule valid also where the
clock equals zero. The derivative bound gives an integrable
majorant $M_f|u(t)|^p$. Applying the absolutely continuous
fundamental theorem of calculus proves the integrated identity.
\end{proof}
