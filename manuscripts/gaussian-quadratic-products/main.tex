\begin{abstract}
Centered Gaussian quadratic forms extend from finite matrices to the
complete second Wiener chaos. We give complete proofs of the sharp
dimension-independent fourth-moment bounds, bounded multiplication
into Gaussian $L^2$, its exact norm, and the degree-zero/two/four
product decomposition. A nonzero completed quadratic has strictly
larger fourth moment than a Gaussian of the same variance, although
the lower universal coefficient is not attained. Polarization gives
nonnegative covariance of quadratic squares with exact contraction
conditions for equality. For finite symmetric matrices these conditions
reduce to matrix annihilation, yielding the classical independence
test. The completion and its source/operator normalization are made
explicit. A centered first/second-chaos mixture has negative
square covariance, delimiting the homogeneous-chaos hypothesis.
Combined tensor symmetrization retains a double-skew determinant
whose actual transported law is explicitly Laplace. A two-source product
has a sharper lower fourth-moment coefficient of nine, and an explicit
anticommuting pair delimits incomplete independence tests.
Full second-chaos prediction compresses both completed matrix coordinates;
its exact error retains the mixed observed/unobserved block.
Classical quadratic-form theory is credited throughout;
no new independence criterion or fourth-moment phenomenon is claimed.
\end{abstract}

\section{The actual Gaussian space and the main statements}
Let $(G_i)_{i\geq0}$ be independent standard real Gaussian variables
on their product probability space. Every $L^p$ space below uses
this positive probability law. Let $\Hh_2$ be the closed subspace of
$L^2$ generated by the centered quadratic monomials
\[
 G_i^2-1,\qquad G_iG_j\quad(i<j).
\]
This is the second Wiener chaos. Orthogonality of the tensor Hermite
polynomials also defines the closed degree-$k$ spaces $\Hh_k$ and
their orthogonal projections $P_k$. We use the probabilists'
normalization $H_2(x)=x^2-1$, $H_4(x)=x^4-6x^2+3$.
Here $\Hh_0$ is the constant space and $P_0F=(\E F)1$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Every $F\in\Hh_2$ belongs to $L^4$. If $F\ne0$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mainbounds}
 3(\E F^2)^2<\E F^4\leq15(\E F^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The upper coefficient is attained. The lower coefficient $3$ is
optimal over the entire space, but equality there forces $F=0$.
Multiplication is a bounded bilinear map
\[
 (F,G)\longmapsto FG:\Hh_2\times\Hh_2\to L^2,
 \qquad \|FG\|_2\leq\sqrt{15}\|F\|_2\|G\|_2,
\]
whose norm is exactly $\sqrt{15}$. Its range lies in
$\Hh_0\oplus\Hh_2\oplus\Hh_4$, rather than in $\Hh_2$ alone.
The exact fourth-cumulant identity is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cumulant}
 \E F^4-3(\E F^2)^2=\frac32\|P_2(F^2)\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
For all $F,G\in\Hh_2$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:polarized}
 \Cov(F^2,G^2)
 &=2\langle F,G\rangle^2
 +\frac12\langle P_2(F^2),P_2(G^2)\rangle
 +\|P_2(FG)\|_2^2\\
 &\geq2\langle F,G\rangle^2\geq0.\nonumber
\end{align}
Every term on the first line's right side is nonnegative. In
particular equality $\Cov(F^2,G^2)=0$ holds precisely when all
three displayed terms vanish.
\end{theorem}

The assertion about the middle contraction pairing is special to
quadratic chaos. It is not a generic assertion that inner products
of arbitrary orthogonal projections are nonnegative. Likewise,
uncorrelated quadratic observables need not be independent.

Lancaster's primary quadratic-form work \cite{Lancaster} identifies
cumulants with matrix traces. Ogawa's primary account \cite{Ogawa}
states the classical centered Gaussian matrix-annihilation
independence criterion. The present paper reconstructs the exact
completed-space mechanisms in a verified library; it does not claim
priority for those classical results. Primary full-text access for
Lancaster's detailed hypotheses remains incomplete.

\section{Finite matrices, observable normalization and moments}
For a real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix $A$ define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finiteQ}
 Q_A=\sum_{i,j<n}A_{ij}(G_iG_j-\delta_{ij})
     =G^T A G-\tr A.
\end{equation}
If a matrix is not symmetric, its observable equals that of
$S(A)=(A+A^T)/2$: transpose the double sum to see that the
antisymmetric part vanishes. Thus the source observable identifies
matrices differing by a skew-symmetric matrix. We first work on the
symmetric representative to keep its norm nondegenerate.

The generating function of independent standard Gaussian coordinates
is $\E e^{\langle u,G\rangle}=e^{\|u\|^2/2}$. Expanding it to
degree four gives
\[
 \E G_iG_jG_kG_l
 =\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}
       +\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isometry}
 \E Q_A=0,\qquad
 \langle Q_A,Q_B\rangle=2\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij},\qquad
 \E Q_A^2=2\|A\|_\HS^2.
\end{equation}
The Hilbert--Schmidt norm here is the Frobenius norm on finite
matrices, $\|A\|_\HS^2=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2$.

An orthogonal change of coordinates preserves the standard Gaussian
law, since it preserves its characteristic function
$e^{-\|u\|^2/2}$. Diagonalize $A$ by a real orthogonal matrix. If
its eigenvalues are $a_i$, then $Q_A$ has the law of
$\sum_i a_i(G_i^2-1)$. Set $U_i=G_i^2-1$. The elementary normal
moments $\E G^{2k}=(2k-1)!!$ follow by Gaussian integration by parts,
and give
\[
 \E U_i=0,\qquad \E U_i^2=2,\qquad \E U_i^4=60.
\]
In the fourth power of the independent sum, only the four-equal
and two-pair index patterns survive. Thus
\begin{align}\label{eq:finitefourth}
 \E Q_A^4
 &=60\sum_i a_i^4+24\sum_{i<j}a_i^2a_j^2\\
 &=12\Bigl(\sum_i a_i^2\Bigr)^2+48\sum_i a_i^4.
 \nonumber
\end{align}
Since $\sum_i a_i^4\leq(\sum_i a_i^2)^2$, this proves the finite
upper bound $\E Q_A^4\leq15(\E Q_A^2)^2$. Also
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finitecumulant}
 \E Q_A^4-3(\E Q_A^2)^2
 =48\|A^2\|_\HS^2,
\end{equation}
because the eigenvalues of $A^2$ are $a_i^2$. No simultaneous
diagonalization of two unrelated symmetric matrices is used.

\section{The full second chaos and its original source field}
Let $\mathcal S_2$ be the real Hilbert space of symmetric square-summable
matrices $A=(A_{ij})_{i,j\geq0}$, with its Hilbert--Schmidt norm.
Let $A^{(n)}$ retain its first $n$ rows and columns and set all other
entries to zero. Then $A^{(n)}\to A$ in that norm. By
\eqref{eq:isometry}, the actual random variables $Q_{A^{(n)}}$
are Cauchy in $L^2$. Define
\[
 Q_A=\lim_{n\to\infty}Q_{A^{(n)}}\quad\hbox{in }L^2.
\]
The resulting map satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fullisometry}
 \|Q_A\|_2^2=2\|A\|_\HS^2.
\end{equation}
It is onto $\Hh_2$: its range is closed by this isometry and
contains every centered quadratic monomial. Conversely, every
finite-box observable lies in the closed span defining $\Hh_2$.
This proves both inclusions and uniqueness of the symmetric matrix.
The definition does not require $\sum_i A_{ii}$ to converge.
The formal expression $G^TAG-\tr A$ is only the finite formula;
the completed centered quadratic is an $L^2$ limit, not an
unjustified subtraction of two infinite quantities.

The same matrix has an actual Gaussian linear source field. Let
$H=\ell^2(\mathbb N)$ with orthonormal basis $(e_i)$ and define
\[
 V_A=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i,j<n}A_{ij}e_iG_j
       \quad\hbox{in }L^2(\Omega;H).
\]
Orthonormality gives $\|V_A\|^2=\|A\|_\HS^2$. On a finite
linear field $V=(V_i)$, the Gaussian divergence is
\[
 \delta V=\sum_i(G_iV_i-\partial_i V_i).
\]
Hence $\delta V_A=Q_{S(A)}$ for finite matrices, and
$\|\delta V_A\|_2^2=2\|S(A)\|_\HS^2$. It extends boundedly
to the completion of linear fields, with norm $\sqrt2$, and
agrees with the original closed divergence there. To check the last
assertion, for each smooth cylindrical test $f$, finite Gaussian
integration by parts gives
\[
 \E(f\,\delta V_{A^{(n)}})=\E\langle Df,V_{A^{(n)}}\rangle_H.
\]
Pass to the two $L^2$ limits. This is precisely the adjoint-domain
identity defining the original divergence, with a unique output.
Thus bounded completion is compatible with the original operator,
rather than defining a second unrelated divergence.

For symmetric $A$, the recovery field of $Q_A$ is $V_A$ and has
energy $\|V_A\|^2=\frac12\|Q_A\|_2^2$. On finite boxes this
is also $\frac12D Q_A$, since $D Q_A=2V_A$. The source's recovery
and symmetric-field unitary extend this identity to the full second
chaos. In this paper it suffices to retain the explicit coefficient
isometry and finite-box approximations. The matrix notation is a
human coordinate realization of those source constructions.


\section{Full quadratic prediction is a two-coordinate compression}
Let $M\subseteq H$ be any closed subspace, $P$ its orthogonal projection
and $R=I-P$. Put $W(h)=\sum_i h_iG_i$, with convergence in $L^2$,
and let $\mathcal F_M=\sigma(W(h):h\in M)$, completed under the
original probability law. No finite-rank hypothesis on $P$ is imposed.
A symmetric square-summable matrix defines a self-adjoint
Hilbert--Schmidt operator $A$ on $H$; its centered observable is the
actual $Q_A$ already constructed above.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:matrixprediction}
For every such $A$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:matrixprediction}
 \E[Q_A\mid\mathcal F_M]&=Q_{PAP},&
 \|\E[Q_A\mid\mathcal F_M]\|_2^2&=2\|PAP\|_{\HS}^2,\\
 \|Q_A-\E[Q_A\mid\mathcal F_M]\|_2^2
 &=4\|RAP\|_{\HS}^2+2\|RAR\|_{\HS}^2.\nonumber
\end{align}
For every $Y\in L^2(\mathcal F_M)$, the exact error decomposition is
\[
 \|Q_A-Y\|_2^2=\|Q_A-Q_{PAP}\|_2^2+\|Q_{PAP}-Y\|_2^2.
\]
Thus $Q_{PAP}$ is the unique best predictor even among nonlinear
square-integrable functions of all observations. Zero prediction error
holds exactly when $A=PAP$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For finite lists $h_1,\ldots,h_k$, the joint Gaussian characteristic
function is $\exp(-\|\sum_jt_jh_j\|^2/2)$. Orthogonality of $M$ and
$M^\perp$ therefore factors every mixed characteristic function.
The two generated sigma algebras are independent: finite cylinder events
are independent, and two applications of the monotone-class theorem
extend this to the generated sigma algebras. Null completions preserve
independence.

Split $h=Ph+Rh$. Independence, zero means and covariance isometry give
\[
 \E[W(a)W(b)-\langle a,b\rangle\mid\mathcal F_M]
 =W(Pa)W(Pb)-\langle Pa,Pb\rangle.
\]
For example the unobserved product contributes the deterministic
$\langle Ra,Rb\rangle$, which cancels that part of the centering;
each mixed product has conditional mean zero. Symmetric finite-rank
tensors span a dense subspace of the symmetric Hilbert--Schmidt
operators. Summing this identity proves the prediction formula there.
For an arbitrary $A$, choose symmetric finite-rank $A_n\to A$ in
Hilbert--Schmidt norm. Conditional expectation is an $L^2$ contraction,
$\|P(A_n-A)P\|_{\HS}\leq\|A_n-A\|_{\HS}$, and
\eqref{eq:fullisometry} passes the identity to the actual limits.

The four blocks of an operator relative to $H=M\oplus M^\perp$ are
orthogonal in Hilbert--Schmidt inner product. Symmetry gives
$\|PAR\|_{\HS}=\|RAP\|_{\HS}$, so
\[
 \|A\|_{\HS}^2=\|PAP\|_{\HS}^2
       +2\|RAP\|_{\HS}^2+\|RAR\|_{\HS}^2.
\]
This follows directly by summing squared matrix entries in orthonormal
bases of the two closed subspaces, including empty or finite bases.
The isometry gives both norm formulas. Conditional-expectation duality
makes $Q_A-Q_{PAP}$ orthogonal to $L^2(\mathcal F_M)$, proving the
error decomposition and uniqueness. Finally its error vanishes exactly
when the two remaining blocks vanish, equivalently $A=PAP$.
\end{proof}

The source represents the recovery field of $Q_A$ by its square-summable
matrix coordinates. Applying the source projection in the direction
coordinate and conditional expectation in the random coordinate sends
$V_A$ to $V_{PAP}$. This can be verified on $aW(b)$, where it gives
$(Pa)W(Pb)$, and then by bounded completion. Compression commutes with
transposition because $P=P^*$. It consequently preserves symmetric
fields. The completed divergence identity
\[
 \delta V_{PAP}=Q_{PAP}=\E[\delta V_A\mid\mathcal F_M]
\]
retains the original closed divergence, not a new formal matrix operator.
In coordinates its exact prediction energy is
\[
 2\sum_{i,j\geq0}
 \big\langle (Pe_i)W(Pe_j),V_A\big\rangle_{L^2(\Omega;H)}^2
 =\|\E[Q_A\mid\mathcal F_M]\|_2^2.
\]
Indeed the inner product is $\langle Pe_i,A Pe_j\rangle$, the
$(i,j)$ coefficient of $PAP$; Parseval gives the sum. The source
proves this for every completed second-chaos target, not only finite
quadratic polynomials. The least-error and block-error formulations
above are explicit human Hilbert-space consequences.

\subsection*{Conditional mean zero does not make the residual independent}
One can say more about the error without discarding its dependence.
Let $C=RAP$ and $D=RAR$. The observed $H$-valued Gaussian field
\[
 U_C=\sum_j C m_j W(m_j)\quad\hbox{in }L^2(\Omega;H)
\]
uses an orthonormal basis $(m_j)$ of $M$. It is well defined since
$\sum_j\|Cm_j\|^2=\|C\|_{\HS}^2$ and is $\mathcal F_M$-measurable.
The following additional identity is a human conditional-moment
deduction, not a new mapped formal conditional-variance declaration:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrixconditionalvariance}
 \E[(Q_A-Q_{PAP})^2\mid\mathcal F_M]
 =4\|U_C\|_H^2+2\|D\|_{\HS}^2\quad\hbox{in }L^1.
\end{equation}
To prove it, first use finite adapted bases of $M$ and $M^\perp$.
Writing their independent standard coordinates as $Y$ and $Z$, the
residual is $2Z^TCY+Q_D(Z)$. Conditional on $Y$, its two terms are
uncorrelated: the mixed third centered Gaussian moments vanish.
Their variances are $4\|CY\|^2$ and $2\|D\|_{\HS}^2$.
This proves the finite formula. Truncate all three operator blocks in
these adapted bases. Hilbert--Schmidt convergence gives residual
convergence in $L^2$, hence squared-residual convergence in $L^1$ by
Cauchy--Schwarz. Also $U_{C_n}\to U_C$ in $L^2(\Omega;H)$, so its
squared norm converges in $L^1$ by the same inequality. Conditional
expectation is an $L^1$ contraction. These facts pass the finite
identity to \eqref{eq:matrixconditionalvariance}, without a pointwise
infinite quadratic expression or an $H$-valued standard Gaussian.

For a concrete warning take $M=\operatorname{span}\{e_0\}$ and
$A_{01}=A_{10}=1$, all other entries zero. Then $Q_A=2G_0G_1$,
$PAP=RAR=0$ and $\E[Q_A\mid G_0]=0$, but
\[
 \E[Q_A^2\mid G_0]=4G_0^2
\]
is nonconstant. The residual is therefore not independent of the
observations. This also shows why the mixed block contributes twice
in the Hilbert--Schmidt error and cannot be omitted.

Gaussian conditioning as second quantization is classical.
Alpay--Kipnis~\cite{AlpayKipnis}, Section 2, printed page 5,
identifies conditional expectation with the projected chaos operator
before equation (2.8). Our proof uses this mechanism directly on
quadratic tensors and proves its completion. Only the conditioning
identity is used from that comparison; no implication from conditional
mean zero to independence of a mixed chaos is assumed.

\section{Strong fourth-moment completion and bounded multiplication}
The finite upper bound applies to the symmetric difference of two
box matrices. Therefore
\[
 \|Q_{A^{(n)}}-Q_{A^{(m)}}\|_4
 \leq15^{1/4}\|Q_{A^{(n)}}-Q_{A^{(m)}}\|_2.
\]
The sequence is Cauchy in $L^4$. Its $L^4$ limit has the same
$L^2$ class as $Q_A$, because the probability measure gives
$\|X\|_2\leq\|X\|_4$. Completeness and uniqueness identify the
limits. Hence every $F\in\Hh_2$ belongs to $L^4$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L4bound}
 \|F\|_4\leq15^{1/4}\|F\|_2.
\end{equation}
This also proves the upper bound in \eqref{eq:mainbounds} on the
full space. No continuity of squaring on unrestricted $L^2$ is used.

By H\"older and \eqref{eq:L4bound},
\[
 \|FG\|_2\leq\|F\|_4\|G\|_4
 \leq\sqrt{15}\|F\|_2\|G\|_2.
\]
The product is the actual almost-everywhere pointwise product, since
both factors have $L^4$ representatives. For box approximations,
\begin{align*}
 \|F_nG_n-FG\|_2
 &\leq\|F_n-F\|_4\|G_n\|_4
       +\|F\|_4\|G_n-G\|_4\longrightarrow0.
\end{align*}
In particular $F_n^2\to F^2$ strongly in $L^2$. Bilinearity on
equivalence classes is inherited from pointwise multiplication.

Take $F=G=G_0^2-1$. Its variance is $2$ and fourth moment is
$60$. The quotient $\|F^2\|_2/(\|F\|_2\|F\|_2)$ is
$\sqrt{60}/2=\sqrt{15}$. Thus the bilinear norm bound is exact,
and the upper fourth-moment coefficient is attained. The image
need not be quadratic: $(G_0^2-1)^2$ has a nonzero fourth-chaos
component, identified below.

\section{Degree projections and the completed cumulant}
For finite symmetric matrices the degree-two product component is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixedprojection}
 P_2(Q_AQ_B)=2Q_{AB+BA},\qquad P_2(Q_A^2)=4Q_{A^2}.
\end{equation}
One direct verification uses the degree-two product rule
\begin{align*}
 &(G_iG_j-\delta_{ij})(G_kG_l-\delta_{kl})\\
 &\quad=\text{degree-four Wick term}
 +\delta_{ik}(G_jG_l-\delta_{jl})
 +\delta_{il}(G_jG_k-\delta_{jk})\\
 &\qquad\quad+\delta_{jk}(G_iG_l-\delta_{il})
 +\delta_{jl}(G_iG_k-\delta_{ik})
 +\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}.
\end{align*}
This identity follows by expanding the multivariate Hermite
generating function $\exp(\langle t,G\rangle-\|t\|^2/2)$ in its
two pairs of variables. Equivalently, subtract the listed lower
degree terms from the product and check its orthogonality to
degrees zero through three by Gaussian moments. Summing with
coefficients $A_{ij}B_{kl}$ gives \eqref{eq:mixedprojection}.
It gives $P_0(FG)=\langle F,G\rangle1$ as well.

For finite observables the product has only degrees $0,2,4$.
The sum of those three mutually orthogonal closed subspaces is
closed: if a sequence in their sum is Cauchy, each projected
component is Cauchy and converges in its closed subspace. Strong
product convergence therefore gives, for the full second chaos,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomposition}
 FG=P_0(FG)+P_2(FG)+P_4(FG).
\end{equation}
All other degree projections vanish. This is a decomposition in
the original Gaussian $L^2$, not a new multiplication internal
to $\Hh_2$.

For a finite symmetric $A$, \eqref{eq:mixedprojection} and
\eqref{eq:isometry} give
\[
 \|P_2(Q_A^2)\|_2^2=32\|A^2\|_\HS^2.
\]
Combining this with \eqref{eq:finitecumulant} proves
\eqref{eq:cumulant} on boxes. Strong $L^4$ approximation of $F$
and strong $L^2$ approximation of its square, followed by the
bounded orthogonal projection $P_2$, pass every term to the limit.
This proves \eqref{eq:cumulant} on the entire second chaos.
The argument uses finite-dimensional spectral theory only for the
finite fourth-moment calculation; no infinite spectral expansion
or trace-class hypothesis on the original quadratic matrix is needed.

Orthogonality in \eqref{eq:decomposition} also yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P4energy}
 \|P_4(F^2)\|_2^2
 =2\|F\|_2^4+\frac12\|P_2(F^2)\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $\|F^2\|_2^2=\|F\|_2^4+\|P_2(F^2)\|_2^2+
\|P_4(F^2)\|_2^2$, and \eqref{eq:cumulant} identifies the left
side. In particular,
\[
 \|P_2(F^2)\|_2^2\leq8\|F\|_2^4,\qquad
 2\|F\|_2^4\leq\|P_4(F^2)\|_2^2\leq6\|F\|_2^4.
\]
These are exact degree-energy consequences, not pointwise positivity
statements about the projected functions.

\section{Strictness without a uniform gap above three}
We prove that the second-degree square contraction of a nonzero
completed quadratic cannot vanish. This strictness does not follow
merely by taking limits of positive finite cumulants, since positive
numbers can tend to zero.

Represent $F=Q_A$ by its unique symmetric square-summable matrix.
For fixed $i,j$, its finite-box contraction satisfies, for $n>i,j$,
\[
 \frac14\bigl[\text{recovery of }P_2(Q_{A^{(n)}}^2)\bigr]_{ii}
 =\sum_{k<n}A_{ik}^2\geq A_{ij}^2.
\]
Here recovery sends $Q_C$ to $V_C$; an individual source coefficient
is a bounded linear functional on the completed field. Recovery
itself is bounded on the second chaos by the energy identity in
Section 3. Since $Q_{A^{(n)}}^2\to F^2$ in $L^2$, the coefficient
inequality passes to the limit. If $P_2(F^2)=0$, its recovered
diagonal coefficient is zero. Thus $A_{ij}^2=0$ for every fixed
pair $i,j$, so $A=0$ and $F=0$. This proves
\[
 F\ne0\ \Longrightarrow\ P_2(F^2)\ne0.
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:cumulant} now gives the strict lower inequality
in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Since every $F\in\Hh_2$ is centered,
no nonzero such variable has a Gaussian law: a centered Gaussian
of variance $\E F^2$ has fourth moment exactly $3(\E F^2)^2$.

Nevertheless there is no larger universal lower coefficient.
For $n\geq1$, set $T_n=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(G_i^2-1)$. The finite
moment calculation gives
\[
 \E T_n^2=2n,\qquad \E T_n^4=12n^2+48n,\qquad
 \frac{\E T_n^4}{(\E T_n^2)^2}=3+\frac{12}{n}\longrightarrow3.
\]
If a constant $c$ were a lower fourth-moment coefficient for every
quadratic in the completed space, these explicit tests would give
$c\leq3+12/n$ for every $n$, hence $c\leq3$. Thus strictness for
each nonzero target and optimality of the nonattained lower
coefficient coexist. No distributional limit theorem for $T_n$
is needed or asserted in this proof.

\section{Mixed square covariance by polarization}
We next prove the exact mixed identity \eqref{eq:polarized}.
Set $U=P_2(F^2)$, $V=P_2(G^2)$ and $W=P_2(FG)$.
Apply \eqref{eq:cumulant} to $F+G$ and $F-G$. Subtract twice
its versions for $F$ and $G$ from the sum of those two identities.
The fourth-moment contribution is $12\E(F^2G^2)$.
Writing $a=\|F\|_2^2$, $b=\|G\|_2^2$, $c=\langle F,G\rangle$,
the Gaussian subtraction contributes $-12ab-24c^2$.
On the contraction side,
\[
 P_2((F\pm G)^2)=U+V\pm2W.
\]
The parallelogram identity then gives the remaining contraction
contribution $6\langle U,V\rangle+12\|W\|_2^2$.
Dividing by $12$ proves \eqref{eq:polarized}.
All products and fourth moments exist by Section 4, so polarization
is valid on the completed space.

It remains to show $\langle U,V\rangle\geq0$.
For finite symmetric $A,B$, \eqref{eq:mixedprojection} gives
\begin{align}\label{eq:middlepair}
 \langle P_2(Q_A^2),P_2(Q_B^2)\rangle
 &=32\tr(A^2B^2)=32\|AB\|_\HS^2\geq0.
\end{align}
The matrix identity follows from
$\|AB\|_\HS^2=\tr((AB)^TAB)=\tr(BA^2B)=\tr(A^2B^2)$,
using symmetry and cyclicity of finite traces. Approximate $F,G$
by their finite boxes. Strong square convergence and boundedness
of $P_2$ make the two projected squares converge strongly, so
their inner products converge. Nonnegativity therefore passes
to arbitrary $F,G\in\Hh_2$. This proves the lower bounds and
the exact equality conditions in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\section{Finite-matrix annihilation and the independence boundary}
For finite symmetric $A,B$, the preceding identities specialize to
\begin{align}\label{eq:matrixcov}
 \Cov(Q_A^2,Q_B^2)
 &=8(\tr AB)^2+16\|AB\|_\HS^2
       +8\|AB+BA\|_\HS^2.
\end{align}
Here $\langle Q_A,Q_B\rangle=2\tr AB$ and
$P_2(Q_AQ_B)=2Q_{AB+BA}$, while
\eqref{eq:middlepair} gives the middle term. Every term on the
right is nonnegative. It follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finitecriterion}
 \Cov(Q_A^2,Q_B^2)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad AB=0.
\end{equation}
The reverse implication also uses $BA=(AB)^T=0$.
If $Q_A,Q_B$ are independent, their squares are independent and
their finite fourth moments imply factorization of the product of
squares. Equation \eqref{eq:finitecriterion} then forces $AB=0$.
This is the source-mapped necessary independence test.

For completeness, the classical converse is an elementary
finite-dimensional corollary, rather than an additional formal
declaration asserted in the source map. If $AB=0$, symmetry gives
$BA=0$ and $\operatorname{ran}A\perp\operatorname{ran}B$:
$\langle Ax,By\rangle=\langle x,ABy\rangle=0$.
Choose an orthonormal basis adapted to these two orthogonal ranges
and their joint orthogonal complement. A symmetric matrix
annihilates the orthogonal complement of its range; hence $Q_A$
depends only on the Gaussian coordinates in the first range, and
$Q_B$ only on those in the second. Those coordinate vectors are
independent, so the two observables are independent. Thus
\eqref{eq:finitecriterion} is also equivalent to independence in
this finite centered Gaussian setting. This is classical
quadratic-form theory, not a newly claimed criterion.

Orthogonality of the targets is weaker. The explicit source example in
Section~\ref{sec:anticommuting} also has vanishing mixed degree-two
contraction, while its square-contraction pairing remains positive.

\section{Sharp two-source product bounds}\label{sec:twosource}
The general lower coefficient $3$ is not the sharp coefficient on the
smaller class of centered products of two linear Gaussian sources.
Let $W$ be the actual isonormal embedding of the real source Hilbert
space and set
\[
 X=W(u),\quad Y=W(v),\quad a=\|u\|^2,\quad b=\|v\|^2,
 \quad c=\langle u,v\rangle,\quad A=ab,\quad Z=XY-c.
\]
These definitions allow arbitrary completed source vectors, not only
finitely supported vectors. Their two-dimensional joint Gaussian law
has moment generating function
\[
 \E e^{sX+tY}=\exp\!\left(\frac{as^2+2cst+bt^2}{2}\right).
\]
Indeed every linear combination is the embedded source $W(su+tv)$.
This identity also covers singular covariance matrices. All polynomial
moments are finite: Cauchy--Schwarz bounds each mixed absolute moment
by the corresponding one-variable Gaussian moments.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:twosource}
The actual centered product satisfies
\begin{align}\label{eq:twosourcemoments}
 \E Z^2&=A+c^2,\\
 \E Z^4&=9A^2+42Ac^2+9c^4.\nonumber
\end{align}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twosourcebounds}
 9(\E Z^2)^2\leq\E Z^4\leq15(\E Z^2)^2,
 \qquad
 15(\E Z^2)^2-\E Z^4=6(A-c^2)^2.
\end{equation}
If $Z\ne0$ in $L^2$, lower equality holds exactly when $c=0$;
upper equality holds exactly when $A=c^2$. Thus both constants are
attained by nonzero targets. The latter equality is precisely equality
in the source Cauchy--Schwarz inequality.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expanding the displayed exponential, the coefficient of $s^kt^k$
is the sum over nonnegative integers $i,j,l$ with $2i+j=k$ and
$j+2l=k$ of
\[
 \frac{(a/2)^i c^j(b/2)^l}{i!j!l!}.
\]
Multiply by $(k!)^2$. For $k=1,2,3,4$ this gives respectively
\[
 \E XY=c,\quad \E X^2Y^2=A+2c^2,\quad
 \E X^3Y^3=9Ac+6c^3,
\]
\[
 \E X^4Y^4=9A^2+72Ac^2+24c^4.
\]
Expanding $(XY-c)^2$ and $(XY-c)^4$ proves
\eqref{eq:twosourcemoments}. Subtracting nine times the squared
variance gives $24Ac^2\geq0$; subtracting the fourth moment from
fifteen times the squared variance gives the stated squared Gram
determinant. For a nonzero target, $A>0$: if $a=0$ or $b=0$,
the corresponding centered Gaussian is zero almost surely and so is
$Z$. Thus lower equality is equivalent to $c=0$, while upper equality
is equivalent to $A=c^2$. Orthogonal nonzero vectors give the first
case, and $u=v\ne0$ gives the second.
\end{proof}

For $A>0$ put $r=c^2/A\in[0,1]$. A further elementary human
reorganization of the moment identity is
\[
 \frac{\E Z^4}{(\E Z^2)^2}
 =9+\frac{24r}{(1+r)^2}.
\]
Its derivative with respect to $r$ is $24(1-r)/(1+r)^3\geq0$.
This describes the exact interpolation between an orthogonal product
and a rank-one square. It is not a lower bound of nine for the whole
completed second chaos; the many-coordinate examples in the earlier
section still approach three. The formal module
\texttt{GaussianMixedQuadraticFourthMoment} supplies
\texttt{mixed\_fourth\_moment},
\texttt{mixed\_fourth\_upper\_deficit},
\texttt{mixed\_fourth\_upper\_equality\_iff} and
\texttt{mixed\_fourth\_lower\_bound} retain the actual source targets.
The lower equality characterization and scalar monotonicity just
proved are explicit human consequences. These Gaussian product
moments are classical: Gaunt~\cite{Gaunt}, Section 2.7, gives the same
central moments and kurtosis after normalizing the variances and
setting the number of product summands to one.

\section{A vanishing mixed contraction does not give independence}\label{sec:anticommuting}
The three conditions in the square-covariance identity must be kept
together. Let $X,Y$ be two actual independent standard source
coordinates and take
\[
 A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 F=X^2-Y^2,\quad G=2XY.
\]
Both are centered members of $\Hh_2$. Matrix multiplication gives
$AB=-BA=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
so their anticommutator vanishes but their product does not.
In particular
\[
 \langle F,G\rangle=0,\qquad \E F^2=\E G^2=4.
\]
The first equality follows from odd one-coordinate Gaussian moments;
the variances follow from $\E X^4=\E Y^4=3$.

Using $H_3(x)=x^3-3x$, cancellation of the two degree-two terms gives
\[
 FG=2H_3(X)Y-2XH_3(Y)\in\Hh_4,\qquad P_2(FG)=0.
\]
Yet the square contractions are
\[
 P_2(F^2)=P_2(G^2)=4\bigl(H_2(X)+H_2(Y)\bigr).
\]
For the first identity expand $F^2=X^4+Y^4-2X^2Y^2$ with
$x^4=H_4(x)+6H_2(x)+3$ and $x^2=H_2(x)+1$.
For the second use $G^2=4X^2Y^2$. Since $H_2(X)$ and $H_2(Y)$
are orthogonal and each has squared norm two, their contraction
pairing is $64$. Therefore the earlier polarization identity yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:anticommutingcovariance}
 \Cov(F^2,G^2)=\tfrac12\,64=32>0.
\end{equation}
One can also check this without any chaos projection:
\[
 \E F^2G^2=4\E(X^6Y^2-2X^4Y^4+X^2Y^6)
 =4(15-18+15)=48,
\]
and subtracting $4\cdot4$ gives $32$. Independence would factor
these square moments, a contradiction. This is a classical explicit
calibration of the matrix-annihilation criterion, not a replacement
criterion. Even orthogonality together with $P_2(FG)=0$ omits the
nonnegative square-contraction pairing in \eqref{eq:polarized}.
The pointwise algebraic identity
\[
 F^2+G^2=(X^2+Y^2)^2
\]
is consistent with this dependence, but the positive covariance above
is the actual proof. All these source identifications and constants
are recorded in \texttt{GaussianQuadraticAnticommutingExample};
no assumption that nonlinear Gaussian targets are jointly Gaussian
has been made.

\section{Centered mixed chaoses need not have associated squares}
The restriction to homogeneous second chaos in
\eqref{eq:polarized} cannot be replaced by centering alone, even
for degree-two polynomials of one Gaussian coordinate. Let $Z=G_0$
and, for $a\in\R$, define the actual random variables
\[
 F_a=Z+a(Z^2-1),\qquad G_a=Z-a(Z^2-1).
\]
They lie in $\Hh_1\oplus\Hh_2$, are centered, and have moments
of every order. Orthogonality of $H_1$ and $H_2$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixturenorm}
 \|F_a\|_2^2=\|G_a\|_2^2=1+2a^2,\qquad
 \langle F_a,G_a\rangle=1-2a^2.
\end{equation}
There is an exact product identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixtureproduct}
 F_aG_a=(1-2a^2)H_0(Z)+(1-4a^2)H_2(Z)-a^2H_4(Z).
\end{equation}
Indeed the left side is $Z^2-a^2(Z^2-1)^2$, while
$Z^2=H_2+1$ and $(Z^2-1)^2=H_4+4H_2+2$. No completion
or formal product is involved: these are polynomial equalities
pointwise for every real $Z$. Gaussian Hermite orthogonality,
with squared norms $1,2,24$ in degrees $0,2,4$, yields
\begin{align}\label{eq:mixturecovariance}
 \E(F_a^2G_a^2)&=(1-2a^2)^2+2(1-4a^2)^2+24a^4
                    =3-20a^2+60a^4,\notag\\
 \Cov(F_a^2,G_a^2)&=2-24a^2+56a^4.
\end{align}
Subtracting $(1+2a^2)^2$ proves the second identity.
The roots of the latter polynomial in $a^2$ are
$(3-\sqrt2)/14$ and $(3+\sqrt2)/14$. Thus the square covariance
is negative throughout the open interval between those values
of $a^2$, despite both targets being centered. These parameter
formulas are direct polynomial deductions from the displayed
family; no additional general formal theorem is being claimed.

The mapped witness is $a=1/2$, for which
\[
 F_{1/2}G_{1/2}=\tfrac12-\tfrac14H_4(Z),\qquad
 \|F_{1/2}\|_2^2=\|G_{1/2}\|_2^2=\tfrac32,
\]
and hence
\[
 \E(F_{1/2}^2G_{1/2}^2)=\tfrac14+\tfrac1{16}\,24
 =\tfrac74<\tfrac94,
 \qquad \Cov(F_{1/2}^2,G_{1/2}^2)=-\tfrac12.
\]
Thus even the weaker inequality
$\E(F^2G^2)\geq\E F^2\E G^2$ fails on centered mixed-chaos
polynomials. This is neither a signed-measure effect nor failure
of moment integrability. The measure is the same positive
Gaussian product probability as in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
At $a\ne0$ both the first- and second-chaos projections of each
target are nonzero; neither target belongs to a single homogeneous
chaos. Consequently the counterexample does not contradict the
homogeneous-chaos square inequalities of
Malicet--Nourdin--Peccati--Poly~\cite{MNPP}, nor an inequality
whose targets are jointly Gaussian linear variables. The mixed
pair is a nonlinear polynomial image of a Gaussian variable,
which is a different hypothesis.


\section{Two skew factors and an actual determinant law}
\subsection{Symmetrize the combined coefficient, not its factors}
For a finite real square matrix $A$, write
$S_A=(A+A^T)/2$, $K_A=(A-A^T)/2$. For matrices $A,B$ on
possibly different finite index sets, the tensor coefficient acts on
pairs of indices by $(A\otimes B)_{(i,a),(j,b)}=A_{ij}B_{ab}$.
Its full symmetrization is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensorsymmetry}
 S_{A\otimes B}=S_A\otimes S_B+K_A\otimes K_B.
\end{equation}
Indeed $(A\otimes B)^T=A^T\otimes B^T$. Substitute
$A=S_A+K_A$, $A^T=S_A-K_A$ and the corresponding formulas
for $B$: the two mixed terms cancel while the two displayed
terms remain. This is an entrywise identity, with no positivity
or commutation hypothesis. A symmetric factor tensored with a
skew factor is fully skew and has zero diagonal quadratic
contraction, but two skew factors give a fully symmetric tensor.

Take
\[
 J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad T=J\otimes J.
\]
Then $S_J=0$, whereas $S_T=T\ne0$: its quadratic contraction
on four coordinates is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:detobservable}
 Q_T=2(G_{00}G_{11}-G_{01}G_{10}).
\end{equation}
Here the four $G_{ia}$ are independent standard Gaussians and
$\tr T=0$, so this is exactly the centered observable
\eqref{eq:finiteQ}. Also $T^T=T$, $T^2=I_4$ and
$\|T\|_\HS^2=4$, by $J^T=-J$ and $J^2=-I_2$.
Equations \eqref{eq:isometry} and \eqref{eq:finitefourth} give
\[
 \E Q_T^2=8,\qquad \E Q_T^4=12\cdot4^2+48\cdot4=384,
 \qquad \frac{\E Q_T^4}{(\E Q_T^2)^2}=6.
\]
Thus componentwise symmetrization would erase a genuinely nonzero
second-chaos target. The single-factor skew field $V_J=JG$ has
$\delta V_J=Q_J=0$, while the combined field $V_T=TG$ satisfies
\[
 \delta V_T=Q_T,\qquad DQ_T=2V_T,\qquad \|V_T\|_{L^2(H)}^2=4.
\]
These are the original polynomial derivative/divergence identities
of Section 3, now applied to the combined coefficient. This does
not say that the ordinary product of two zero random variables
is nonzero. Forming a tensor of coefficient matrices and then
one quadratic observable is a different operation from multiplying
the two individual quadratic observables.

\subsection{The actual four-coordinate law, including arbitrary embeddings}
For completeness the construction works with any four orthonormal
vectors $h_{ia}\in\ell^2$ in the original countable Gaussian source.
Define $W(h)$ by the $L^2$ limit of $\sum_{j<n}h_jG_j$.
The four $W(h_{ia})$ have the independent standard Gaussian law.
Here is a direct verification that retains the actual embedding.
For any finite real coefficients $t_{ia}$, their linear combination
is $W(\sum t_{ia}h_{ia})$. Its finite coordinate sums have
characteristic function tending to
$\exp(-\|\sum t_{ia}h_{ia}\|^2/2)$.
The limit is valid since $L^2$ convergence implies $L^1$
convergence and $|e^{ix}-e^{iy}|\leq|x-y|$.
Orthonormality makes the squared norm $\sum t_{ia}^2$.
The joint characteristic function therefore factors into four
standard Gaussian characteristic functions; uniqueness identifies
the full product law. In particular the two rows are independent
Gaussian vectors, not merely uncorrelated polynomial targets.
Substitution in \eqref{eq:detobservable} transports the same law
to the original ambient quadratic observable for every such frame.

Write $U=(G_{00},G_{01})$, $V=(G_{10},G_{11})$. Conditional
on the first row $U=u$, \eqref{eq:detobservable} is the linear
Gaussian combination $-2u_2V_1+2u_1V_2$. Hence, for real $t$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:detconditional}
 \E(e^{itQ_T}\mid U=u)=e^{-2t^2(u_1^2+u_2^2)}.
\end{equation}
The formula also holds at $u=0$, where the conditional variable
is zero. Every phase is bounded by one, so Fubini applies without
a moment or uniform-integrability assumption. For a standard
Gaussian $Z$ and $c\geq0$, combining its density with
$e^{-cZ^2}$ and scaling the Gaussian integral gives
\[
 \E e^{-cZ^2}=(1+2c)^{-1/2}.
\]
Indeed the integral of $e^{-(c+1/2)x^2}$ divided by that of
$e^{-x^2/2}$ has exactly this value by the substitution
$y=\sqrt{1+2c}\,x$. Integrate \eqref{eq:detconditional} over
the two independent first-row coordinates to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:detchar}
 \E e^{itQ_T}=\frac1{1+4t^2}.
\end{equation}
This identifies the whole law, beyond the fourth-moment diagnostic.

The nonnegative density $p(x)=e^{-|x|/2}/4$ integrates to one.
Its characteristic function is, by absolutely convergent half-line
integration,
\[
 \int_\R e^{itx}p(x)\,dx
 =\frac14\left(\frac1{1/2-it}+\frac1{1/2+it}\right)
 =\frac1{1+4t^2}.
\]
For example the first half-line formula follows by integrating
$e^{-(1/2-it)x}$ from zero to a finite endpoint and then letting
that endpoint tend to infinity; the boundary exponential tends
to zero in modulus. The negative half follows by $x\mapsto-x$.
Characteristic-function uniqueness and \eqref{eq:detchar} prove
that $Q_T$ has the actual Laplace density $p$. In particular its
law has no atoms, is independent of the chosen orthonormal
source frame, and the unscaled determinant has density
$e^{-|x|}/2$. No higher-dimensional determinant law is asserted.

This distribution is a classical antecedent, not a new consequence
claimed as a probability discovery. Nyquist--Rice--Riordan
\cite{NRR}, page 98, explicitly display the unscaled two-dimensional
Gaussian determinant density $e^{-|x|}/2$ and already call that
special case well known. The formalization contribution is the
exact combined-index symmetry, original derivative/divergence
normalization, full law transport from arbitrary orthonormal source
coordinates and direct density identification. No geometric or
stochastic-process application follows solely from this tensor
calibration.
