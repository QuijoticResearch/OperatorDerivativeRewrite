\begin{abstract}
We assemble exact diagnostics for velocity gradients, spectral ratios
and threshold occupation.
A family of nonnormal restricted Euler matrices has fixed full spectrum but
variable strain spectrum, with arbitrarily small positive normalized
stretching along trajectories whose raw stretching diverges. Its affine
realization solves the local Navier--Stokes equations with quadratic
pressure, but has infinite whole-space energy. A trace computation then
detects an incompatible mixture of full and trace-free pressure
conventions: the proposed strain right side has trace equal to the squared
Frobenius norm of the velocity gradient under the pressure Poisson
constraint. Finally a smooth periodic viscous ABC flow has zero averaged
stretching derivative, while a displayed plus-pressure balance gives
three at the initial time. A fixed smooth periodic Gaussian-series shear
has a specified normalized two-sector spectral ratio strictly above one
at every finite nonnegative time, with its full lattice spectrum and
antipodal conic partition retained. For threshold labels, an exact
null-boundary criterion separates stable occupation from smooth
positive-sector counterexamples. Complete elementary proofs reconstruct frozen
formal results. These are calibration examples in classical model
families, not new fluid existence or singularity results; priority for
the diagnostic formulations remains unresolved.
Low-direction nonlinear responses retain an explicit high-background dependence,
even for Gaussian amplitudes of size $1/N$, and a scalar norm reduction
requires an upper frequency ratio. An actual stationary Burgers vortex additionally separates an axial pressure flux from a scalar Gaussian observation, and a repaired helical field gives exact finite-slab trace and full-Hessian pairings with their nonzero axial boundary discrepancy. \end{abstract}

\section{Conventions and purpose}
For a real $3\times3$ matrix $A$, use
\[
 S=\tfrac12(A+A^{\mathsf T}),\qquad W=A-S,\qquad
 \omega=(A_{32}-A_{23},A_{13}-A_{31},A_{21}-A_{12}).
\]
For an actual velocity $u$, $A_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$ and $\omega=\nabla\times u$.
The pressure Hessian is $P=\nabla^2p$, so the incompressible pressure
constraint is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson}
 -\tr P=\tr(A^2).
\end{equation}
This convention agrees with the velocity-gradient formulation in
Cantwell~\cite{Cantwell} and the explicit pressure equations of
Yang, Xu, Pumir and He~\cite{Yang}. Full pressure and its deviatoric
part $P_0=P-\tfrac13\tr(P)I$ must be distinguished.

The first test concerns the matrix ODE
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RE}
 A'=-A^2+\tfrac13\tr(A^2)I,\qquad \tr A=0.
\end{equation}
The second is an algebraic test of a specified strain right side. The
third tests a specified spatially averaged identity on an actual smooth
periodic Navier--Stokes flow. Each test has its own hypotheses; neither
an ODE trajectory nor an infinite-energy affine field is promoted to a
finite-energy singularity theorem.


\section{A low-direction derivative can depend on an unseen high mode}
We next test a possible retained-field derivative estimate using actual
smooth periodic velocities, keeping input and output projections distinct.
Use coordinates $(x,y,z)$ on $\mathbb T^3$. Write
$\operatorname{av} F=(2\pi)^{-3}\int_{[0,2\pi]^3}F$ for normalized
spatial average, and fix an integer $N\geq4$. For real $a,c$ put
\[
 u_{a,c}(x,y,z)=(0,-2a\sin x,-2c\sin Ny).
\]
These fields are smooth, periodic, mean zero and divergence free: each
nonzero component is independent of its own coordinate. Their only
Fourier modes are $\pm(1,0,0)$ and $\pm(0,N,0)$. With the convention
$u=\sum_k\widehat u(k)e^{ik\cdot x}$, their positive-mode coefficients
are respectively $ia e_2$ and $ic e_3$, with the negative conjugates.
Here $e_2,e_3$ denote the second and third Cartesian directions.

Let $\mathbb P$ be the usual periodic Leray projection and
$\mathcal B(u)=\mathbb P[(u\cdot\nabla)u]$. Direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:highlowadvection}
 (u_{a,c}\cdot\nabla)u_{a,c}
 =(0,0,4acN\sin x\cos Ny).
\end{equation}
Indeed $u_1=0$, all $z$ derivatives vanish, and the only remaining
term is $(-2a\sin x)\partial_y(-2c\sin Ny)$ in the third component.
This output is itself divergence free and mean zero. Its Fourier
waves are $(\pm1,\pm N,0)$ and their vectors point along $e_3$,
perpendicular to every wave. Thus Leray projection leaves it unchanged.

At the high background $u_{0,c}$, the low-direction variation
$\partial_a u_{a,c}|_{a=0}=(0,-2\sin x,0)$ has fixed frequency one.
The actual response is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:highlowderivative}
 \left.\partial_a\mathcal B(u_{a,c})\right|_{a=0}
 =(0,0,4cN\sin x\cos Ny),\qquad
 \operatorname{av}\left|\left.\partial_a\mathcal B(u_{a,c})\right|_{a=0}\right|^2
 =4c^2N^2.
\end{equation}
The last equality uses the normalized means of $\sin^2x$ and
$\cos^2Ny$, both $1/2$, and Fubini on the compact torus.
All expressions are smooth, so these are classical derivatives and
ordinary integrals, with no weak-realization assumption.

Let $\Pi_K$ retain the velocity modes $|k|\leq K$, and set $K=N/2$.
The low input mode is retained, while both the background and all
response modes lie strictly above $K$. In particular
\[
 \Pi_{N/2}u_{0,c}=0,\qquad
 \|\Pi_{N/2}u_{0,c}\|_{H^1}=0.
\]
Consequently, for $c\ne0$, no finite constant $C$ can give
\[
 \left\|\left.\partial_a\mathcal B(u_{a,c})\right|_{a=0}\right\|_{L^2}
 \leq C\|\Pi_{N/2}u_{0,c}\|_{H^1}.
\]
The left side is $2|cN|>0$ and the right side is zero.
The source also checks the retained Fourier synthesis and its
coefficient-defined $H^1$ energy directly; their vanishing agrees
with the actual field projection used here.

\section{A scalar norm reduction needs an upper frequency ratio}
A separate algebraic check prevents reversing a spectral inequality.
For positive real $A,B$ and $C\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normreduction}
 A^{3/2}B^{3/2}\leq CA^2B
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\leq C^2A.
\end{equation}
Divide by the positive $A^{3/2}B$ to obtain
$\sqrt B\leq C\sqrt A$, and square the two nonnegative sides.
These steps are reversible. In particular, $A\leq B$ gives the
opposite ordering from the one needed at $C=1$, except at equality.
For $A=1,B=4$, the proposed intermediate and target values are
$8$ and $4$, respectively.

The failure persists in explicit finite spectral data with bounded
lowest moment. For a finite index set and positive weights $w_i$, define
\[
 E_j=\sum_i w_i^j a_i^2,\qquad A=\sqrt{E_1},\quad B=\sqrt{E_3},
\]
assuming $E_1,E_3>0$. In a one-coordinate family choose
$w=L^2$, $a=1/L$, $L\geq1$. Then
\[
 E_0=L^{-2}\leq1,\quad E_1=1,\quad E_3=L^4,\quad
 A=1,\quad B=L^2.
\]
The ratio of the intermediate expression to $A^2B$ is $L$, so
no universal $C$ bounds it even with $w\geq1$, $E_0\leq1$ and $A\leq B$.
For any proposed $C\geq0$, $L=C+1$ is already a counterexample.

\section{Fixed full spectrum and arbitrarily small normalized stretching}
For $p>2$ set $a=\sqrt{(p-2)(p+1)}>0$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M_{p,a}=\begin{pmatrix}-p&0&a\\0&1&0\\-a&0&p-1\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad c(t)=(1-t)^{-1},\qquad A(t)=c(t)M_{p,a}.
\end{equation}
For $\omega\ne0$ let
\[
 \sigma(A)=\frac{\omega^{\mathsf T}S\omega}{|\omega|^2},\qquad
 \rho(A)=\frac{\sigma(A)}{\|S\|_F}.
\]
The normalization uses the Frobenius norm of the symmetric strain,
not the spectral radius of the nonsymmetric full matrix.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:family}
For every $p>2$ the trajectory \eqref{eq:M} solves \eqref{eq:RE} for
$t<1$. Its strain eigenvalues in increasing order are
$c(t)(-p,1,p-1)$, and its nonzero vorticity is aligned with the
intermediate eigendirection. Its full characteristic polynomial is
\[
 \det(rI-A(t))=(r-c(t))^2(r+2c(t)),
\]
whereas
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ratios}
 \sigma(A(t))=c(t),\qquad
 \rho(A(t))=\frac1{\sqrt{p^2+1+(p-1)^2}}.
\end{equation}
For every $\epsilon>0$ a choice of $p>2$ makes
$0<\rho(A(t))<\epsilon$ for all $0\leq t<1$, while
$\sigma(A(t))\to\infty$ as $t\uparrow1$. No positive uniform lower
threshold for this normalized quantity holds on these exact profiles.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The middle coordinate of $M$ is one. On the first and third coordinates,
direct multiplication and $a^2=p^2-p-2$ give
\[
 M^2+M=2I,\qquad \tr M=0,\qquad \tr M^2=6.
\]
Thus $-M^2+\tr(M^2)I/3=M$. Since $c'=c^2$ and the ODE right side
is homogeneous of degree two, $A'=c^2M$ is precisely that right side.
Also
\[
 S(M)=\operatorname{diag}(-p,1,p-1),\qquad \omega(M)=(0,2a,0).
\]
Multiplying by $c>0$ proves the strain ordering and alignment and gives
$|\omega(A)|^2=4a^2c^2$ and $S(A)\omega(A)=c\omega(A)$.
The stretching formulas \eqref{eq:ratios} follow, since
$\|S(A)\|_F^2=c^2(p^2+1+(p-1)^2)$.

The determinant factors as
\[
 \det(rI-M)=(r-1)((r+p)(r-p+1)+a^2)=(r-1)^2(r+2).
\]
Rescaling gives the stated polynomial for $A$. The matrix is nonnormal:
the $(1,3)$ entries of $M^{\mathsf T}M$ and $MM^{\mathsf T}$ are
$-a(2p-1)$ and $a(2p-1)$, respectively, and are unequal.
Hence the varying symmetric spectrum does not contradict the fixed
full spectrum.

For $\epsilon>0$, choose $p=2+\epsilon^{-1}$ and the specified $a$.
The denominator in \eqref{eq:ratios} is strictly larger than $p$, and
$\epsilon p=2\epsilon+1>1$, so $0<\rho<1/p<\epsilon$.
Meanwhile $c(t)\to\infty$ as $t\uparrow1$, proving raw stretching
divergence along each selected trajectory.
\end{proof}

For example $p=3,a=2$ gives strain ratios $-3:1:2$; $p=4,a=\sqrt{10}$
gives $-4:1:3$ and normalized stretching $1/\sqrt{26}$. A particular
strain ratio is therefore not universal even within this elementary
self-similar family. Cantwell's general restricted-Euler solution already
distinguishes full-gradient and strain/rotation geometry~\cite{Cantwell}.
The examples here are exact calibration data, not a new solution theory.

\section{An affine realization and its spatial limitation}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:affine}
With the same parameters, define on $\R^3$, for $t<1$,
\[
 u(t,x)=c(t)M_{p,a}x,\qquad p_{\rm aff}(t,x)=-c(t)^2|x|^2.
\]
These smooth fields satisfy
\[
 \nabla\cdot u=0,\qquad
 \partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p_{\rm aff}=\nu\Delta u
\]
for every fixed $\nu\geq0$, pointwise on this time interval. They are
not periodic and $u(t,\cdot)\notin L^2(\R^3)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The spatial gradient is $cM$, the divergence is $c\tr M=0$, and
$\Delta u=0$. Moreover
\[
 \partial_tu=c^2Mx,\qquad
 (u\cdot\nabla)u=c^2M^2x,\qquad \nabla p_{\rm aff}=-2c^2x.
\]
The momentum identity is therefore $c^2(M+M^2-2I)x=0$.
Its pressure Hessian is $P=-2c^2I$ and satisfies
$-\tr P=6c^2=\tr(A^2)$, so the Poisson convention is consistent.
The middle velocity coordinate is $u_2=c x_2$, which is not periodic.
Since $c>0$, integrating $|u|^2\geq c^2x_2^2$ over expanding boxes
gives an infinite integral. Thus these fields do not meet the
whole-space finite-energy hypothesis.
\end{proof}

They also provide a local pressure-sign test. Define
$q=\omega^{\mathsf T}S\omega$, $b=|S\omega|^2$ and
$h=\omega^{\mathsf T}P\omega$ for this affine field. Directly,
\[
 q=4a^2c^3,\qquad b=4a^2c^4,\qquad h=-8a^2c^4.
\]
Since $q$ is spatially constant and $c'=c^2$,
\[
 (\partial_t+u\cdot\nabla)q=12a^2c^4=b-h\ne b+h.
\]
This equality is a computation for these fields, not a proposed general
formula with all other terms suppressed. It calibrates the sign of the
displayed pressure contraction locally; it supplies no finite global
integrals of the affine field.

\section{A trace obstruction to mixed pressure conventions}
The strain right side to be tested is the explicit matrix expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bad}
 R_+(A,P)=-S^2-W^2-P+
 \frac{\tr(S^2)+|\omega|^2/2}{3}I.
\end{equation}
It is this expression, including the isotropic plus sign and the
meaning of $P$, that the next result excludes as a trace-preserving
incompressible strain evolution.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:trace}
For every real $A,P$ satisfying \eqref{eq:poisson},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:badtrace}
 \tr R_+(A,P)=\|A\|_F^2.
\end{equation}
In particular it is strictly positive if $A\ne0$. Adding a trace-free
matrix correction cannot restore trace zero. The full-pressure
expression and the trace-free-pressure expression
\begin{align}
 R_{
 \rm full}(A,P)&=-S^2-W^2-P,\label{eq:full}\\
 R_{\rm dev}(A,P_0)&=-S^2-W^2-P_0+
 \frac{\tr(S^2)-|\omega|^2/2}{3}I\label{eq:dev}
\end{align}
are trace-consistent under their respective conventions. Under
\eqref{eq:poisson}, \eqref{eq:full} and \eqref{eq:dev} agree.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The symmetric/skew splitting is Frobenius orthogonal. The skew matrix
associated with the above vorticity has squared Frobenius norm
$|\omega|^2/2$, and hence
\begin{align*}
 \tr W^2&=-|\omega|^2/2,\\
 \tr A^2&=\tr S^2-|\omega|^2/2,\\
 \|A\|_F^2&=\tr S^2+|\omega|^2/2
             =\tr A^2+|\omega|^2.
\end{align*}
For example $|\omega|^2$ is the sum over $i<j$ of
$(A_{ij}-A_{ji})^2$, while the off-diagonal contribution to
$\tr A^2$ is $2\sum_{i<j}A_{ij}A_{ji}$; adding proves the last identity
directly. Taking the trace of \eqref{eq:bad} gives
$|\omega|^2-\tr P$. Substituting \eqref{eq:poisson} proves
\eqref{eq:badtrace}. The Frobenius squared norm is positive unless all
entries vanish, and a trace-free addition leaves this trace unchanged.

The trace of \eqref{eq:full} is $-\tr A^2-\tr P$, which is zero under
\eqref{eq:poisson}. The trace of \eqref{eq:dev} is zero because
$\tr P_0=0$ and $\tr(S^2)-|\omega|^2/2=\tr A^2$.
Finally $P=P_0+\tr(P)I/3=P_0-\tr(A^2)I/3$ under the constraint;
inserting this in \eqref{eq:full} gives \eqref{eq:dev}.
\end{proof}

If $\tr A=0$, the strain trace must remain zero along an incompressible
evolution. Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} thus gives an immediate nonzero-state
obstruction to \eqref{eq:bad}. The theorem is a consistency test, not
a derivation or closure of a nonlocal pressure law.

\section{A periodic positive-viscosity pressure-sign test}
On the $2\pi$-periodic three-torus let
\[
 U(x,y,z)=(\sin z+\cos y,\ \sin x+\cos z,\ \sin y+\cos x).
\]
This is the classical unit-parameter ABC profile; the same convention
is used in~\cite{XYZ}. Write $\langle f\rangle=(2\pi)^{-3}
\int_{[0,2\pi]^3}f$ for normalized spatial average. For a smooth
velocity $v$ with strain $S_v$ and vorticity $\omega_v$, define
\[
 Q(v)=\langle\omega_v^{\mathsf T}S_v\omega_v\rangle,\quad
 B(v)=\langle|S_v\omega_v|^2\rangle,\quad
 H(v,p)=\langle\omega_v^{\mathsf T}(\nabla^2p)\omega_v\rangle,
\]
and the specific viscous contraction
\[
 J(v)=\left\langle\sum_{k,i,j}(S_v)_{ij}
               (\partial_k\omega_{v,i})(\partial_k\omega_{v,j})\right\rangle.
\]
These are ordinary derivatives and integrals of smooth periodic fields.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ABC}
For each $\nu>0$, the fields
\[
 u(t)=e^{-\nu t}U,\qquad p(t)=-\tfrac12|u(t)|^2
\]
are a smooth periodic Navier--Stokes solution for all $t\geq0$.
They satisfy $Q(u(t))=0$ for every $t$, whereas at $t=0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ABCvalues}
 B(U)=\tfrac32,\qquad H(U,-|U|^2/2)=\tfrac32,\qquad J(U)=0.
\end{equation}
In particular the identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:plusbalance}
 \frac{d}{dt}Q(u(t))=B(u(t))+H(u(t),p(t))-2\nu J(u(t))
\end{equation}
fails at $t=0$: its sides are zero and three respectively. Reversing the
pressure sign makes the two sides agree on this test at $t=0$, without
asserting a general evolution formula from this agreement.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Differentiation gives $\nabla\cdot U=0$, $\nabla\times U=U$ and
$\Delta U=-U$. For a Beltrami field,
$(U\cdot\nabla)U=\nabla K$, $K=|U|^2/2$, by the vector identity
$(U\cdot\nabla)U=\nabla K-U\times(\nabla\times U)$.
Moreover $S_U U=\nabla K$, because the skew part of $\nabla U$
annihilates its vorticity $U$. For $u=e^{-\nu t}U$, the nonlinear
advection is canceled by $\nabla p$, and
$\partial_tu=-\nu u=\nu\Delta u$. This proves the PDE assertion.

Periodic integration by parts and $\nabla\cdot U=0$ give
$Q(U)=\langle U\cdot\nabla K\rangle=0$. At time $t$, stretching is
scaled by $e^{-3\nu t}$, so $Q(u(t))=0$ and its derivative is zero.
The kinetic gradient is
\[
 \nabla K=(\cos x\cos z-\sin x\sin y,
 \ \cos y\cos x-\sin y\sin z,
 \ \cos z\cos y-\sin z\sin x).
\]
Each component has mean square $1/4+1/4=1/2$; the cross terms have
zero average by separation of variables. Since $S_UU=\nabla K$,
this proves $B(U)=3/2$.
For the pressure contraction, integrating $U_iU_j\partial_i\partial_j(-K)$
by parts in $i$ gives
\[
 H(U,-K)=-\langle((U\cdot\nabla)U)\cdot\nabla(-K)\rangle
 =\langle|\nabla K|^2\rangle=\tfrac32.
\]
All boundary terms cancel by periodicity.

It remains to check the precise viscous contraction, rather than omit it.
The strain has zero diagonal and
\[
 S_{12}=\tfrac12(\cos x-\sin y),\quad
 S_{13}=\tfrac12(\cos z-\sin x),\quad
 S_{23}=\tfrac12(\cos y-\sin z).
\]
As $\omega_U=U$, the three terms
$(\partial_kU)^{\mathsf T}S_U(\partial_kU)$, for $k=x,y,z$, are
\begin{align*}
 &-(\cos y-\sin z)\cos x\sin x,\\
 &-(\cos z-\sin x)\sin y\cos y,\\
 &-(\cos x-\sin y)\cos z\sin z.
\end{align*}
Each has zero normalized average; for example integration in $x$ kills
the first. Thus $J(U)=0$. These values prove both the failure of
\eqref{eq:plusbalance} and the limited sign-reversal calibration.
\end{proof}

The affine sign test and periodic test address different possible
loopholes. The former is local and has no finite whole-space energy;
the latter is smooth, periodic, and has arbitrary positive viscosity.
Neither asserts that all pressure contractions have one sign, or that
changing one sign establishes a complete general strain balance.

\section{A fixed smooth infinite-spectrum shear and an angular ratio}
The preceding periodic example tests pressure signs. We now test a different,
explicitly defined spectral ratio, using a second actual periodic solution.
The heat-governed shear mechanism is classical; the purpose is to retain
the complete spectrum, all denominator signs and the angular partition
when evaluating the formula.

\subsection{The actual solution and every Fourier coefficient}
On the $2\pi$-periodic three-torus with coordinates $(x_0,x_1,x_2)$, put
\[
 \Theta_s(y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-sn^2}\cos(ny),\qquad s>0,
\]
and for $\nu\geq0,t\geq0$ set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infshear}
 u_\nu(t,x)=\left(2\Theta_{1+\nu t}(x_1)
                         +e^{-\nu t}\sin x_2,\ 0,\ 0\right),\qquad p=0.
\end{equation}
The initial smoothing is fixed at one; it is not changed as a cutoff or
observation horizon varies.

\begin{lemma}[Classical periodic shear]\label{lem:infshear}
Formula \eqref{eq:infshear} is a smooth spatially periodic incompressible
Navier--Stokes solution for every nonnegative viscosity, with the printed
zero pressure. Its normalized integral-defined Fourier coefficients have
only the following nonzero first components:
\begin{align}
 \widehat u_0(t,(0,n,0))&=2e^{-(1+\nu t)n^2}\quad(n\in\mathbb Z),\label{eq:shearaxis}\\
 \widehat u_0(t,(0,0,1))&=-\tfrac i2e^{-\nu t},\qquad
 \widehat u_0(t,(0,0,-1))=\tfrac i2e^{-\nu t}.\label{eq:shearother}
\end{align}
All other components and frequencies vanish. In particular the zero
coefficient is two, and the solution has nonzero modes beyond every finite
axis cutoff at every finite time.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For every integer $m\geq0$ and $s_0>0$, the series

\section{Threshold labels require control of boundary occupation}
The smoothness of a field or its sector energies is different from
the stability of time spent in a threshold-defined diagnostic region.
We record the exact finite-measure distinction. Let $(X,\mu)$ be a
finite measure space, let $\rho,\zeta:X\to\R$ be measurable scores,
and fix thresholds $A,B\in\R$. Define the prioritized label
\[
 \ell(\rho,\zeta)=
 \begin{cases}
 0,&\rho<A,\\
 1,&\rho\geq A,\ \zeta<B,\\
 2,&\rho\geq A,\ \zeta\geq B.
 \end{cases}
\]
Its occupation totals are $O_j=\mu\{\ell=j\}$. On a finite
time interval these are ordinary durations. The relevant boundary is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corridorboundary}
 D=\{\rho=A\}\ \cup\ \{\rho\geq A,\ \zeta=B\}.
\end{equation}
The second threshold is irrelevant where $\rho<A$. In particular
$\{\zeta=B\}$ need not be null there. These arbitrary score pairs
are not identified with the preceding centroid ratio or a physical
evolution law merely by this notation.

Suppose measurable approximations obey
$|\rho'-\rho|\leq\varepsilon_\rho$ and
$|\zeta'-\zeta|\leq\varepsilon_\zeta$ everywhere, with
nonnegative error bounds. Their disagreement set $E$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corridorband}
 E\subseteq\{|\rho-A|\leq\varepsilon_\rho\}
 \ \cup\ \{\rho\geq A,\ |\zeta-B|\leq\varepsilon_\zeta\}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, outside the first strip the original and approximating
first scores lie on the same side of $A$: their distance is too
small to cross it. If that side is below, both labels are zero.
Otherwise $\rho\geq A$, and outside the second strip the two second
scores likewise lie on the same side of $B$. Their labels agree.
Each label-region symmetric difference is contained in $E$; thus
\begin{align}\label{eq:corridoroccupationbound}
 |O'_j-O_j|&\leq\mu(E)\notag\\
 &\leq\mu\{|\rho-A|\leq\varepsilon_\rho\}
       +\mu\{\rho\geq A,|\zeta-B|\leq\varepsilon_\zeta\}.
\end{align}
The first inequality follows by subtracting the two indicator
integrals and bounding the absolute difference by $1_E$.
For example bounds $C_\rho\varepsilon_\rho^{\gamma_\rho}$ and
$C_\zeta\varepsilon_\zeta^{\gamma_\zeta}$ on these actual strip
masses immediately give their sum as an occupation error rate.
This is an elementary consequence, not a rate asserted without
an assumption on the score distribution.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:corridorstability}
For the displayed finite-measure classifier, the following are
equivalent: $\mu(D)=0$; every pair of measurable uniform score
approximations has disagreement mass tending to zero; and every
such approximation has $O'_j\to O_j$ for all three labels.
In the last condition it suffices to test labels zero and two for
the simultaneous downward shifts
$(\rho_n,\zeta_n)=(\rho-\delta_n,\zeta-\delta_n)$,
$\delta_n=1/(n+1)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Off $D$, any pointwise-convergent scores eventually have the same
label. If $\rho<A$, convergence of the first score alone gives
label zero eventually. Otherwise $\rho>A$ and $\zeta\ne B$;
the strict distances to both relevant thresholds preserve the
label for all sufficiently large $n$. In fact convergence of the
second score is needed only on $\{\rho\geq A\}$. If $\mu(D)=0$,
the disagreement indicators tend to zero almost everywhere and
are bounded by the integrable constant one. Dominated convergence
gives vanishing disagreement mass, and the first inequality in
\eqref{eq:corridoroccupationbound} gives occupation convergence.
This proves the forward assertions, even for this weaker almost
everywhere score convergence, hence for uniform convergence.

Conversely take the displayed downward shifts, which are uniformly
within $\delta_n$ of the scores. At a point with $\rho=A$, its
old label is one or two and every shifted first score is below
$A$, so the label changes. At a point with $\rho>A,\zeta=B$,
the shifted first score remains above $A$ eventually, while the
second lies below $B$: label two changes to one. At points outside
$D$ the labels eventually agree by the preceding argument.
Thus dominated convergence gives the exact limit
\begin{equation}\label{eq:downboundarylimit}
 \mu\{\ell(\rho_n,\zeta_n)\ne\ell(\rho,\zeta)\}
       \longrightarrow\mu(D).
\end{equation}
Universal vanishing disagreement therefore forces $\mu(D)=0$.

Occupation convergence alone could in principle hide label exchanges,
so a further argument is required. Here the downward shifts never
increase a label: the prioritized rule is nondecreasing in each
coordinate. For ordered labels $j'\leq j$ in $\{0,1,2\}$ one has
\[
 1_{\{j'\ne j\}}\leq
 (1_{\{j'=0\}}-1_{\{j=0\}})
 +(1_{\{j=2\}}-1_{\{j'=2\}}).
\]
For unchanged labels both sides are zero; for changes $1\to0$
and $2\to1$ both sides are one; for $2\to0$ the right side is
two. These are all possible strict decreases. Integrating yields
\[
 \mu(E_n)\leq(O_{0,n}-O_0)+(O_2-O_{2,n}).
\]
If the two endpoint occupations converge, the nonnegative left
side tends to zero. Equation \eqref{eq:downboundarylimit} now
forces the null-boundary condition. Universal occupation convergence
includes this particular shift test, completing every implication.
\end{proof}

The necessity quantifies over all measurable uniform perturbations
of these scores. It is not automatically a necessity theorem for
a narrower physically attainable family. For the converse direction
in such a restricted family, the detecting perturbations would have
to be realizable within that family. The following finite-sector
example gives a concrete failure without a vanishing denominator
or a tie for the largest sector.

\section{Smooth sector energies: kinks and full occupation errors}
\subsection{A maximum preserves a Lipschitz bound, not differentiability}
For finitely many functions $e_i$,
\[
 |\max_i e_i(t)-\max_i e_i(s)|
 \leq\max_i|e_i(t)-e_i(s)|.
\]
To prove the one-sided inequality, choose an index attaining the
maximum at $t$, replace its value at $s$ by the maximum there,
and then reverse $s,t$. Thus a common Lipschitz constant is retained.
It need not retain differentiability even for strictly positive
smooth sector energies. Take
\[
 e_\pm(t)=1\pm t+t^2=(t\pm\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac34.
\]
Their total is $2+2t^2>0$ but their maximum is $1+t^2+|t|$.
The normalized maximum concentration is consequently
\[
 \chi(t)=\frac{\max(e_+(t),e_-(t))}{e_+(t)+e_-(t)}
 =\frac12+\frac{|t|}{2(1+t^2)}.
\]
At zero its right derivative is $1/2$ and left derivative is
$-1/2$, so it is continuous and locally Lipschitz but not
differentiable. This normalized maximum is a different statistic
from the squared-weight concentration in the earlier centroid ratio.

There is a useful denominator-controlled bound. Suppose $N$ sector
energies are nonnegative at $t,s$, their totals $E(t),E(s)\geq d>0$,
and $|e_i(t)-e_i(s)|\leq K|t-s|$. Write $M=\max_i e_i$.
Then $|M(t)-M(s)|\leq K|t-s|$,
$|E(t)-E(s)|\leq NK|t-s|$, and $0\leq M(s)/E(s)\leq1$.
Use the exact identity
\[
 \frac{M(t)}{E(t)}-\frac{M(s)}{E(s)}
 =\frac{M(t)-M(s)}{E(t)}
 +\frac{M(s)}{E(s)}\frac{E(s)-E(t)}{E(t)}
\]
to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maxconcentrationlip}
 |\chi(t)-\chi(s)|\leq\frac{(N+1)K}{d}|t-s|.
\end{equation}
If $\chi(s)\leq A-\eta$ and the right side is less than
$\eta$, then $\chi(t)<A$. This is a local threshold-persistence
test with actual quantitative hypotheses; a qualitative smoothness
claim supplies neither those constants nor an occupation bound.

\subsection{A strictly dominant sector can sit at a threshold}
On the time interval $[0,1]$ with Lebesgue measure, let $|p|\leq1/8$
and define constant-in-time energies
\[
 e_0^p=\tfrac12+p,\qquad e_1^p=e_2^p=\tfrac14-\tfrac p2.
\]
All are positive, their total is one, and the gap from the first
sector to either other sector is $1/4+3p/2\geq1/16$. Thus the
same sector is strictly dominant throughout the family, and its
maximum concentration is exactly $\rho_p=1/2+p$. Every energy
converges uniformly to the $p=0$ energy as $p\to0$, with error
at most $|p|$. The functions are smooth in time and their parameter.
Take the second score $\zeta_p=1$ and thresholds $A=1/2$, $B=2$.
For $p=0$ every time has label one, whereas for every $p<0$ every
time has label zero. Therefore $O_{0,p}=1$, $O_{0,0}=0$, and
their difference is exactly one for arbitrarily small negative $p$.
The relevant boundary has full time measure at $p=0$.
The obstruction is threshold mass, despite strict spectral-sector
dominance, positive energies and a fixed total. These energies are
an actual finite family of scalar diagnostic data; no realization
as a Navier--Stokes trajectory is claimed.

The null-boundary and strip arguments are classical continuity-set
and dominated-convergence mechanisms. For comparison, the primary
margin assumption of Audibert--Tsybakov~\cite{AT} controls mass near
a binary statistical decision threshold. That assumption explicitly
excludes equality in its strip and concerns excess risk, not the
unweighted disagreement/occupation considered here. In particular
positive equality mass cannot be discarded in the present theorem.
No statistical minimax rate, learning theorem or new general
continuity principle is being asserted.


\section{An actual viscous vortex and its axial pressure flux}
A further calibration has a genuine stationary Navier--Stokes velocity
and its constructed pressure, rather than only an algebraic pressure
candidate. It also has an indispensable spatial boundary limitation.
Write $s=x^2+y^2$, $f=e^{-s}$, and define the regular angular profile
and its pressure primitive by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:swirlprofile}
 g(s)=\frac12\int_0^1e^{-ts}\,dt,\qquad
 F(s)=\frac12\int_0^s g(u)^2\,du.
\end{equation}
Then $g(0)=1/2$, and for $s\ne0$,
$g(s)=(1-e^{-s})/(2s)$. Differentiating the parameter integral any
number of times is justified locally uniformly by
$|(-t)^k e^{-ts}|\le e^{|s_0|+1}$ for $0\le t\le1$ and
$|s-s_0|<1$. Thus $g$ and $F$ are smooth even at the axis, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:swirlidentities}
 2g+2sg'=e^{-s},\qquad4g'+2sg''=-e^{-s}.
\end{equation}
The first identity follows by integrating the derivative of
$te^{-ts}$ over $[0,1]$; differentiating it gives the second.

For viscosity $\nu>0$ and circulation amplitude $\beta\in\mathbb R$,
set
\begin{align}
 u&=(-2\nu x-\beta yg(s),\,-2\nu y+\beta xg(s),\,4\nu z),
 \label{eq:burgersvelocity}\\
 p&=\beta^2F(s)-2\nu^2s-8\nu^2z^2.\label{eq:burgerspressure}
\end{align}
The fluid density is normalized to one.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:burgersactual}
The displayed fields are smooth and satisfy pointwise
\[
 \nabla\cdot u=0,\qquad
 (u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p=\nu\Delta u,\qquad
 \omega:=\nabla\times u=(0,0,\beta e^{-s}).
\]
If $\beta\ne0$, then with $H=\nabla^2p$ and $B=\nabla\omega$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:burgerscontraction}
 \operatorname{tr}(B^2)=0,\qquad
 \omega\cdot H\omega=-16\nu^2\beta^2e^{-2s}<0.
\end{equation}
For any axial interval $[a,b]$, this nonzero contraction has the exact
boundary identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:burgersflux}
 \int_a^b\omega\cdot H\omega\,dz
 =[\omega_z^2\partial_zp]_{z=a}^{z=b}
 =-16\nu^2\beta^2(b-a)e^{-2s}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The parameter integral proves smoothness. Put
\[
 v=g(s)(-y,x,0),\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(-2\nu,-2\nu,4\nu),
\]
so $u=D(x,y,z)+\beta v$. Direct differentiation gives
$\nabla\cdot v=0$ and
$\nabla\times v=(0,0,2g+2sg')=(0,0,f)$. The linear part has zero
trace and zero curl. Moreover
\[
 (v\cdot\nabla)v=-g^2(x,y,0),\qquad
 \Delta v=-2f(-y,x,0).
\]
For the Laplacian, its scalar coefficient is
$8g'+4sg''=-2f$, by \eqref{eq:swirlidentities}.
The two cross accelerations satisfy
\[
 (Dq\cdot\nabla)v+(v\cdot\nabla)Dq
 =-4\nu(g+sg')(-y,x,0)=-2\nu f(-y,x,0).
\]
The purely linear acceleration is
$(4\nu^2x,4\nu^2y,16\nu^2z)$. Finally
\[
 \nabla p=(\beta^2xg^2-4\nu^2x,\,
            \beta^2yg^2-4\nu^2y,\,-16\nu^2z),
\]
since $F'=g^2/2$. The linear and radial swirl accelerations cancel
these pressure terms; the remaining cross acceleration equals
$\nu\beta\Delta v$. This proves the actual momentum balance.

The vorticity has only its third component nonzero, is independent of
$z$, and has transverse derivatives $-2\beta xf,-2\beta yf$.
Thus $B$ has possible nonzero entries only in its third row and first
two columns, so $B^2=0$. On the other hand $H_{zz}=-16\nu^2$, which
gives the second formula in \eqref{eq:burgerscontraction}. The axial
flux is $\omega_z^2p_z=-16\nu^2\beta^2zf^2$. Ordinary integration
in $z$ gives \eqref{eq:burgersflux} and its strict sign when $a<b$.
\end{proof}

There is no cancellation identity obtained by discarding this boundary
term. For example, at fixed $x,y$ the unit-slab integral of the full
contraction is negative while
$\int_0^1p\operatorname{tr}(B^2)dz=0$.
Integrating also over the transverse plane gives the elementary
consequence $-8\pi\nu^2\beta^2(b-a)$, since
$\int_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-2s}dxdy=\pi/2$. This integrated consequence is
labelled a human deduction; the source proves the axial identity
pointwise in the transverse variables.

The axial velocity $4\nu z$ is unbounded and not periodic. Its square
alone has infinite whole-space integral, so the full flow is not a
finite-energy three-dimensional example. The transverse vorticity
localization does not remove the axial flux. These observations delimit
application of the diagnostic to a decaying or periodic global identity.
The vortex is classical: Gallay--Maekawa's primary formulas
\cite{GM}, equations (1.3), (1.8)--(1.9), give the background strain
and Gaussian circulation profile in their normalization. Here the
strain rate is $4\nu$, the core length is one and the total
transverse circulation is $\pi\beta$. No new stationary solution or
stability theorem is asserted.

\section{A scalar Gaussian observation is not the full Hessian contraction}\label{sec:scalargaussian}
Let $\mu$ be the product Gaussian law with coordinate variance $1/4$.
It has density $(2/\pi)e^{-2s}dxdy$. Define
\[
 r(x,y)=2s-1,\qquad
 \mathcal R(P)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}rP\,d\mu.
\]
Here $\E_\mu r=0$ and $\|r\|_{L^2(\mu)}=1$: each coordinate has
second moment $1/4$ and fourth moment $3/16$, so
$\E s=1/2$ and $\E s^2=1/2$. Consequently $\mathcal R$ is a
bounded scalar $L^2$ observation of norm one and ignores constants.
It is not the tensor-valued pressure Hessian.

For smooth functions with polynomial growth of the function and its
first two derivatives, Gaussian integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radialresponse}
 \mathcal R(P)=\frac18\E_\mu\Delta P
             =\frac12\E_\mu(xP_x+yP_y).
\end{equation}
To check the constants, the one-coordinate Gaussian density satisfies
$\phi'=-4x\phi$. Integration of its derivative gives
$\E P_{xx}=4\E(xP_x)$, and integration of $(xP\phi)'$ gives
$\E[(x^2-1/4)P]=\frac14\E(xP_x)=\frac1{16}\E P_{xx}$.
Summing the two coordinate identities and multiplying by two proves
\eqref{eq:radialresponse}. Polynomial growth makes every boundary term
vanish and all integrals absolutely convergent. The formal source
works on a larger explicitly integrable derivative domain, not only
polynomials; the constructed pressure below belongs to that domain.

For $s\ge0$, the defining parameter integral gives
$0<g(s)\le1/2$ and $|g'(s)|\le1/4$.
Thus $0\le F(s)\le s/8$, $\nabla F(s)$ grows at most linearly,
and its coordinate second derivatives grow at most quadratically.
Indeed $\partial_xF(s)=xg(s)^2$ and
$\partial_{xx}F(s)=g(s)^2+4x^2g(s)g'(s)$.
These bounds justify the preceding integration by parts for $F(s)$
and for the full pressure \eqref{eq:burgerspressure} at each fixed $z$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:burgersresponse}
Writing $\ell=\log(9/8)>0$, the exact observations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exactpressure}
 \mathcal R(F)=\ell/4,\qquad
 \mathcal R(p(\cdot,\cdot,z))=\beta^2\ell/4-\nu^2,
\end{equation}
independently of $z$. For $\beta=1$ this scalar observation is zero
at $\nu_* =\sqrt{\ell/4}$ and positive at $\nu_*/2$, although the
full contraction in \eqref{eq:burgerscontraction} remains strictly
negative everywhere at both positive viscosities.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:radialresponse} gives
$\mathcal R(F)=\frac12\E_\mu[s g(s)^2]$.
Squaring the parameter integral in \eqref{eq:swirlprofile} gives
\[
 g(s)^2=\frac14\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 e^{-(t+u)s}\,dtdu.
\]
The nonnegative integrands permit Tonelli; alternatively $sg(s)^2$
is bounded by $s/4$ and has an integrable Gaussian dominator.
Polar integration, whose radial density is $2e^{-2s}ds$, gives
\[
 \E_\mu[s e^{-cs}]=\frac{2}{(2+c)^2}\quad(c\ge0).
\]
Therefore
\[
 \mathcal R(F)=\frac14\int_0^1\!\int_0^1
       \frac{dtdu}{(2+t+u)^2}.
\]
Integrating first in $u$ yields $(2+t)^{-1}-(3+t)^{-1}$.
Integrating in $t$ yields
$(\log3-\log2)-(\log4-\log3)=\log(9/8)$.
Also $\mathcal R(s)=2\E s^2-\E s=1/2$ and
$\mathcal R(1)=0$. Linearity applied to
\eqref{eq:burgerspressure} proves the second expression.
The assertions at $\nu_*,\nu_*/2$ follow by substitution and
$\ell>0$. The strict tensor sign is already
Theorem~\ref{thm:burgersactual} with $\beta=1$.
\end{proof}

This is an exact mismatch of observables, not a contradiction in the
Navier--Stokes pressure. It does not refute a criterion retaining the
full Hessian, its proper reference measure or the axial boundary terms.

\section{Solenoidal repair and a prescribed base-pressure pairing}
A related proposed helical vorticity needs a first-order repair before
it can be used even as a divergence-free field. With $f=e^{-s}$, set
\[
 \widetilde\omega_\varepsilon
   =(\varepsilon f\cos z,\,\varepsilon f\sin z,\,f),\qquad
 b=x\sin z-y\cos z.
\]
Its actual divergence is
$-2\varepsilon f(x\cos z+y\sin z)$, nonzero at $(1,0,0)$ for
$\varepsilon\ne0$. The corrected smooth field
\begin{equation}\label{eq:repairedomega}
 \omega_\varepsilon
 =(\varepsilon f\cos z,\,\varepsilon f\sin z,\,
   f+2\varepsilon fb)
\end{equation}
has zero divergence, since the $z$ derivative of its added term is
$2\varepsilon f(x\cos z+y\sin z)$. The axial correction is of the
same order as the transverse perturbation and cannot be dropped.

Let $B_\varepsilon=\nabla\omega_\varepsilon$. Differentiating the three
coordinates gives its first two rows
\[
 f(-2\varepsilon x\cos z,-2\varepsilon y\cos z,-\varepsilon\sin z),
 \qquad
 f(-2\varepsilon x\sin z,-2\varepsilon y\sin z,\varepsilon\cos z),
\]
and its third row
\[
 f(-2x+2\varepsilon(\sin z-2xb),\,
   -2y+2\varepsilon(-\cos z-2yb),\,
   2\varepsilon(x\cos z+y\sin z)).
\]
Multiplying these actual matrices and using
$(x\cos z+y\sin z)^2+b^2=s$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:repairedtrace}
 \operatorname{tr}(B_\varepsilon^2)
 =4\varepsilon f^2b+4\varepsilon^2 f^2(2s-1).
\end{equation}
In particular its phase integral is
$8\pi\varepsilon^2 f^2(2s-1)$, a sign-changing radial quantity.
Solenoidality has changed the quadratic trace from that of the
uncorrected ansatz; the change is not only the removal of its divergence.

For the next calculation, keep the actual \emph{base} pressure
$p_{\nu,\beta}$ from \eqref{eq:burgerspressure}, but pair it with the
prescribed unit-core field \eqref{eq:repairedomega}. We do not assert
that this pressure is induced by a velocity whose curl is
$\omega_\varepsilon$, or that the latter evolves by Navier--Stokes.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:basepair}
At every fixed phase $z$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:basecrosssection}
 \int_{\mathbb R^2}p_{\nu,\beta}(x,y,z)
            \operatorname{tr}(B_\varepsilon^2)\,dxdy
 =\frac\pi2\varepsilon^2(\beta^2\ell-4\nu^2).
\end{equation}
Thus integration on any finite axial interval $[a,b]$ multiplies this
value by $b-a$; on $[0,2\pi]$ the result is
$\pi^2\varepsilon^2(\beta^2\ell-4\nu^2)$.
These are finite-slab base-pressure pairings, not periodic full-flow
identities or full pressure-Hessian contractions.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The pressure is even under $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$, while $b$ is odd.
Their linear term in \eqref{eq:repairedtrace} integrates to zero.
All terms are absolutely integrable, since at fixed $z$ the pressure
has at most quadratic transverse growth and the trace is a polynomial
in $x,y$ times $e^{-2s}$. By the physical density of $\mu$, the remaining
quadratic contribution equals
\[
 4\varepsilon^2\int e^{-2s}(2s-1)p_{\nu,\beta}\,dxdy
 =2\pi\varepsilon^2\mathcal R(p_{\nu,\beta}).
\]
Insert \eqref{eq:exactpressure} to obtain
\eqref{eq:basecrosssection}. On a bounded axial interval the same
Gaussian polynomial dominator can be chosen uniformly in $z$, so
Fubini and the constant cross-section value justify the axial integral.
The quadratic $z$-dependence of the base pressure does not make the
flow periodic merely because this particular observation is independent
of $z$.
\end{proof}

The exact response, solenoidal correction and finite-slab constants
are calibration mathematics. The stationary Gaussian vortex and
Gaussian integration by parts are classical. Priority for these
assembled diagnostics is unresolved; no new vortex-stability theorem,
induced helical pressure, finite-energy singularity or unrestricted
regularity conclusion is claimed.


\section{The full repaired pairing and its missing slab flux}
Keep exactly the prescribed base pressure and repaired unit-core
vorticity of the preceding section. Their full contraction can also
be evaluated, without identifying this pressure as induced by the
repaired field. Let $H=\nabla^2p_{\nu,\beta}$ and write
$c=\cos z$, $d=\sin z$, $b=xd-yc$. Direct differentiation gives
\[
 H=\begin{pmatrix}
 D_{xx}&4\beta^2xygg'&0\\
 4\beta^2xygg'&D_{yy}&0\\
 0&0&-16\nu^2
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad
 D_{xx}=\beta^2(g^2+4x^2gg')-4\nu^2,
\]
with $D_{yy}=\beta^2(g^2+4y^2gg')-4\nu^2$; all profile functions
are evaluated at $s=x^2+y^2$. This includes the mixed derivatives,
not only the diagonal Gaussian observation.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:fullrepaired}
For every fixed $z$,
\[
 \int_{\R^2}\omega_\varepsilon\cdot H\omega_\varepsilon\,dxdy
 =\frac\pi2\left[\varepsilon^2(\beta^2\ell-20\nu^2)-16\nu^2\right].
\]
Both orders of integration on $\R^2\times[0,2\pi]$ agree, and
the full slab integral is
\[
 \pi^2\varepsilon^2(\beta^2\ell-20\nu^2)-16\pi^2\nu^2.
\]
Its difference from the pressure--trace integral in
Proposition~\ref{prop:basepair} is exactly
$-16\pi^2\nu^2(1+\varepsilon^2)$. For $\nu\ne0$ a boundary-free
identification of these two integrals is therefore false on this slab.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication by the actual corrected vector gives
\[
 \omega_\varepsilon\cdot H\omega_\varepsilon
 =f^2\left[\varepsilon^2c^2D_{xx}
 +8\varepsilon^2cd\beta^2xygg'
 +\varepsilon^2d^2D_{yy}
 -16\nu^2(1+2\varepsilon b)^2\right].
\]
All bracket terms have at most polynomial transverse growth, since
$|g|\leq1/2$ and $|g'|\leq1/4$. On any finite axial interval
their absolute values have a common polynomial Gaussian dominator
after multiplication by $f^2=e^{-2s}$. Thus the plane and product
integrals below are genuine absolutely convergent integrals.

Use the probability law $d\mu=(2/\pi)f^2dxdy$ from Section~\ref{sec:scalargaussian}.
Reflection of one coordinate makes $\mathbb E_\mu[xygg']=0$.
Coordinate interchange gives $\mathbb E_\mu D_{xx}
=\mathbb E_\mu D_{yy}$, while \eqref{eq:radialresponse} and
\eqref{eq:exactpressure} give
\[
 \mathbb E_\mu D_{xx}=\mathbb E_\mu D_{yy}
 =4\mathcal R(p)=\beta^2\ell-4\nu^2.
\]
The independent centered quarter-variance coordinates give
$\mathbb E_\mu b=0$ and
$\mathbb E_\mu b^2=(d^2+c^2)/4=1/4$.
Consequently $\mathbb E_\mu(1+2\varepsilon b)^2=1+\varepsilon^2$.
Taking the expectation of the bracket and using $c^2+d^2=1$
gives $\varepsilon^2(\beta^2\ell-4\nu^2)
-16\nu^2(1+\varepsilon^2)$, proving the plane formula.
Fubini and independence of the answer from $z$ give the slab formula.
Subtract the trace-pairing value from
Proposition~\ref{prop:basepair} to obtain the stated discrepancy.
\end{proof}

We can identify that discrepancy as an actual boundary term.
For any smooth solenoidal vector $w$ and smooth scalar $p$, expansion
of derivatives gives the pointwise identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slabdivergence}
 \nabla\cdot\left[w(w\cdot\nabla p)-p(w\cdot\nabla)w\right]
 =w\cdot(\nabla^2p)w-p\operatorname{tr}((\nabla w)^2).
\end{equation}
Indeed the two first-derivative terms in $p$ cancel after exchanging
their dummy indices, $\nabla\cdot w=0$ removes the terms containing
that divergence, and its derivative removes the remaining
second-derivative term in $w$. This derivation uses no evolution law.

For $w=\omega_\varepsilon$, put
$A=1+2\varepsilon b$ and $v=xc+yd$.
The derivatives of its axial component are
\[
 \partial_x w_3=f(-2xA+2\varepsilon d),\quad
 \partial_y w_3=f(-2yA-2\varepsilon c),\quad
 \partial_z w_3=2\varepsilon fv.
\]
Multiplying by $w=f(\varepsilon c,\varepsilon d,A)$ proves
$(w\cdot\nabla)w_3=0$ identically. Thus the axial flux in
\eqref{eq:slabdivergence} is simply
$\mathcal F_z=w_3(w\cdot\nabla p)$.
At $z=0$ and $z=2\pi$ the vector $w$ agrees and
$w_3=f(1-2\varepsilon y)$. The transverse pressure gradient also
agrees, but $p_z=-16\nu^2z$. Therefore
\[
 \mathcal F_z(x,y,2\pi)-\mathcal F_z(x,y,0)
 =-32\pi\nu^2 f^2(1-2\varepsilon y)^2.
\]
Its plane integral is
\[
 -32\pi\nu^2\frac\pi2
   \mathbb E_\mu(1-2\varepsilon y)^2
 =-16\pi^2\nu^2(1+\varepsilon^2),
\]
exactly the discrepancy above.
To apply the divergence theorem, first integrate
\eqref{eq:slabdivergence} over a finite cylinder and then let its
radius tend to infinity. On the lateral cylinder every flux term
is a polynomially bounded function times $e^{-2s}$, uniformly on
the finite axial interval; its surface integral tends to zero.
Absolute Gaussian domination permits the limit in the volume and
end-face integrals. Thus the computed end flux proves the full slab
identity directly, in agreement with the independent moment calculation.

The repaired vector is periodic in $z$; the base pressure and its
axial gradient are not. Replacing this slab by a boundary-free period
would discard a strictly nonzero end contribution. These tests do not
contradict a pressure identity proved under valid periodic or
vanishing-flux hypotheses, and do not supply an induced repaired
Navier--Stokes pressure.
