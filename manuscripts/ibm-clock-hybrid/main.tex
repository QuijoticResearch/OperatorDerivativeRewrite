\begin{abstract}
Let $Z_t=X_{|Y_t|}$ for independent Brownian motions $X,Y$.
We reconstruct the closed-operator argument that combines static recovery in
the scenery variable with a predictable integral in the hidden Brownian clock.
For absolutely summable multitime Fourier observations, the two channels
recover the entire centered observable through the original joint intrinsic
divergence. A stronger frequency summability condition puts the conditional
source mean in the original clock derivative domain and identifies the clock
integrand by predictable projection. Fold and collision cusps are treated by
strong graph approximation, including their zero levels. Complete proofs
separate ambient adjoints, observable projection, hidden-clock predictability
and observed-process causality. Original-domain multipliers and an exact
conditional-L1 criterion for the native adjoint are proved, retaining the
full observation projection and all bounded-test hypotheses. A sine observation has zero source mean but
positive variance, so clock-mean representation alone cannot reconstruct IBM.
This is an exposition of verified results and classical Gaussian mechanisms,
with no claim of completed physical-time IBM calculus or external novelty.
\end{abstract}

\begin{lemma}[Projected ambient adjoints]\label{lem:project}
If $u\in\Dom\delta_X$ and $v\in\Dom\delta_Y$, then
$(u,v)\in\Dom\delta_{\Hh}$ and
\[
 \delta_{\Hh}(u,v)=P(\delta_Xu+\delta_Yv).
\]
This is an inclusion of domains; no equality with the full intrinsic
adjoint domain is asserted.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For every $f\in\Dom D_{\Hh}$, ambient duality and $Pf=f$ give
\[
 \langle (u,v),D_{\Hh}f\rangle
 =\langle\delta_Xu+\delta_Yv,f\rangle
 =\langle P(\delta_Xu+\delta_Yv),f\rangle.
\]
The last expression is bounded in $\|f\|_2$. This is exactly adjoint-domain
membership, and density makes its representing vector unique.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Bounded graph approximation and bounded separating tests}
We first record the exact approximation facts needed for domain claims.
The closed Gaussian chain rule gives
\[
 D\phi(v)=\phi'(v)Dv\qquad(v\in\Dom D)
\]
when $\phi$ is bounded $C^1$ with continuous bounded derivative.
It applies in each ambient Gaussian factor. The composed scalar remains
$\mathcal O$-measurable, so the two graph identities belong to the
original restricted joint domain. Here is the strong-graph mechanism:
approximate in the original Gaussian graph by smooth cylindrical
functions, apply the finite chain rule, and select an almost-everywhere
convergent subsequence of the values. Bounded derivatives control the
product with the converging gradients; dominated convergence controls
$(\phi'(v_n)-\phi'(v))Dv$. Boundedness of $\phi'$ also gives convergence
of the values. Closedness identifies the limit in each graph. Smooth
approximations of $\phi$ on finite-dimensional cylinders give the same
argument for $C^1$ functions. This is the usual Gaussian Sobolev chain
mechanism, not an IBM physical-time chain rule.

Take $\chi_n(x)=n\tanh(x/n)$, $n\geq1$. These are bounded smooth
functions with $|\chi_n(x)|\leq|x|$, $|\chi_n'(x)|\leq1$,
$\chi_n(x)\to x$ and $\chi_n'(x)\to1$. Thus, for every $v\in\Dom D$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeboundedgraph}
 v_n=\chi_n(v)\longrightarrow v\text{ in }L^2,\qquad
 Dv_n=\chi_n'(v)Dv\longrightarrow Dv\text{ in }\mathcal V.
\end{equation}
Both convergences follow by dominated convergence, using $|v|^2$ and
$\|Dv\|^2$ respectively. In particular bounded domain elements form a
strong graph core. The displayed hyperbolic truncations are a human
choice of approximation; the source proves the same graph-core property
with its own bounded smooth approximants.

A different fact is needed to separate $L^1$ densities. If $F\in\Hh$
and $|F|\leq1$, density of $\Dom D$ gives $g_n\to F$ in $L^2$.
Let $\chi(x)=\max(-1,\min(1,x))$. Convolve it with a nonnegative
smooth unit-mass mollifier supported in $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$.
The result $\chi_\varepsilon$ is smooth, takes values in $[-1,1]$,
has derivative bounded by one and satisfies
$\sup_x|\chi_\varepsilon(x)-\chi(x)|\leq\varepsilon$.
The chain rule puts $u_n=\chi_{1/n}(g_n)$ in $\Dom D$, and
\[
 |u_n|\leq1,\qquad
 \|u_n-F\|_2\leq n^{-1}+\|g_n-F\|_2\longrightarrow0.
\]
We used that clipping is nonexpansive and fixes $F$.
The formal source uses the exact clipping identity
$\chi(x)=(|x+1|-|x-1|)/2$ and its proved absolute-value chain rule;
the smooth construction here supplies the same value approximation.
No convergence of the derivatives of $u_n$ is claimed in this statement.

Consequently an $\mathcal O$-measurable $a\in L^1$ satisfying
$\E[va]=0$ for every $v\in\Dom D$ with $|v|\leq1$ must vanish.
Apply the approximation to $F=\operatorname{sgn}(a)$, with sign zero
at zero. An $L^2$-convergent sequence has an almost-everywhere convergent
subsequence. Along it, $|u_na|\leq|a|$, so dominated convergence gives
$\E|a|=\lim_n\E[u_na]=0$. This proves $a=0$ almost surely.
The distinction between graph approximation and uniformly bounded value
approximation is essential to the following proof.

\subsection*{An exact $L^1$-to-adjoint criterion}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:conditionaladjoint}
Suppose $U\in\mathcal V$ and $q\in L^1$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeweakpair}
 \langle U,Dv\rangle_{\mathcal V}=\E[vq]
 \quad\text{for every bounded }v\in\Dom D.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 U\in\Dom\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Cq\in L^2.
\]
When these conditions hold, $\delta U=Cq$ as an actual intrinsic $L^2$
class. For unbounded domain tests the valid full identity is
$\langle U,Dv\rangle=\langle Cq,v\rangle_{L^2}$; integrability of
$vq$ is not asserted for arbitrary $v\in\Dom D$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For every bounded observable $v$, conditional expectation gives
$\E[vq]=\E[vCq]$, with both products integrable.
If $U\in\Dom\delta$ and $y=\delta U$, adjoint duality and
\eqref{eq:nativeweakpair} imply $\E[v(Cq-y)]=0$ for all bounded
domain tests. The difference $Cq-y$ is observable and integrable,
since the probability space has mass one and $y\in L^2$.
The separating-test argument just proved gives $Cq=y$ almost surely,
and therefore $Cq\in L^2$.

Conversely suppose $Cq\in L^2$. The bounded-test identity becomes
$\langle U,Dv\rangle=\langle Cq,v\rangle$. For an arbitrary domain
element use \eqref{eq:nativeboundedgraph}; convergence on both sides
extends the identity to it. Thus
$|\langle U,Dv\rangle|\leq\|Cq\|_2\|v\|_2$ on the whole domain,
which is exactly membership in the Hilbert adjoint graph. Density of
the original domain makes its output uniquely $Cq$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Bounded products and the native divergence rule}
If $f,v\in\Dom D$ are bounded, their product belongs to $\Dom D$ and
\[
 D(fv)=fDv+vDf.
\]
To see this on the original graphs, choose a bounded smooth function
agreeing with $x^2$ on a neighborhood of the common bounded ranges of
$f$, $v$ and $f+v$. The closed chain rule and
$2fv=(f+v)^2-f^2-v^2$ prove the formula in each component.
All terms are square integrable because both scalar factors are bounded.

Now let $f\in\Dom D$ be bounded and $U\in\Dom\delta$, without yet
assuming an essentially bounded derivative of $f$. The actual density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativedensity}
 q=f\delta U-\langle Df,U\rangle_{H_X\oplus H_Y}
\end{equation}
belongs to $L^1$: the first product is in $L^2$, and Cauchy--Schwarz
makes the field contraction integrable. Also $fU\in\mathcal V$.
For a bounded domain test $v$, apply adjoint duality to $fv$:
\[
 \langle fU,Dv\rangle
 =\langle U,D(fv)\rangle-\E[v\langle Df,U\rangle]
 =\E[vq].
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:conditionaladjoint} therefore gives the exact rule
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeweightedcriterion}
 fU\in\Dom\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 C\!\left(f\delta U-\langle Df,U\rangle\right)\in L^2.
\end{equation}
When it holds, the displayed conditional density is the adjoint value.
Boundedness of $f$ alone does not establish this remaining $L^2$ condition.
One need not demand that the unprojected $L^1$ density itself be in $L^2$;
the criterion concerns its actual conditional expectation.

Indeed use the bounded graph sequence $v_n$ above. The bounded product

\section{Static Gaussian recovery before predictability}
We recall a Gaussian fact in a form that fixes all normalizations. Let $D$
be the closed Gaussian derivative on one factor, $\delta=D^*$, and
$L^2=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}\mathcal C_n$ its orthogonal Hermite-chaos expansion.
For $f_n\in\mathcal C_n$, the original derivative has
\[
 \|Df_n\|_2^2=n\|f_n\|_2^2,\qquad \delta Df_n=nf_n.
\]
The identities follow first for finite products of normalized Hermite
polynomials, by one-variable Gaussian integration by parts and orthogonality,
and then by closure on each chaos. Derivative images from different chaos
orders are orthogonal as direction fields.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded original recovery]\label{prop:recovery}
For $f=\sum_{n\geq0}f_n\in L^2$, define
\[
 Rf=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac1n Df_n.
\]
Then $Rf\in\Dom\delta$, $\delta Rf=f-\E f$, and
\[
 \|Rf\|_2^2=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac1n\|f_n\|_2^2\leq\|f\|_2^2.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Orthogonality and the displayed derivative norm show that the field series
converges and give its norm formula. For finite chaos sums, Gaussian
integration by parts gives $\delta\sum_{n=1}^Nn^{-1}Df_n=\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n$.
The fields and outputs both converge strongly. Since a Hilbert adjoint is
closed, the limiting pair belongs to the original adjoint graph. Completeness
of Hermite polynomials identifies the scalar limit as $f-\E f$.
\end{proof}
This proof constructs a static Gaussian field. It supplies no time ordering
or predictable representative.

Apply $R$ fiberwise in the scenery coordinate, writing the bounded map as
$R_X$. Fubini and the norm bound give a bounded map on the product $L^2$
space. Approximate a product-space input by finite sums of tensors, use the
one-factor adjoint identity, and pass to the original closed partial adjoint
graph. Consequently, for every $F\in\mathcal K$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source}
 R_XF\in\Dom\delta_X,\qquad
 \delta_XR_XF=F-M,\qquad M=\E_XF,\qquad
 \|R_XF\|_2\leq\|F\|_2.
\end{equation}
Here and below a clock-only variable is lifted to the product space when
needed. Tensor approximation is legitimate because conditional expectation,
the recovery map and the tensor transports are bounded. It does not assert
that conditional expectation preserves an arbitrary derivative domain.

\begin{proposition}[Static centered surjectivity]\label{prop:static}
Every centered $F\in\Hh$ is an output of $\delta_{\Hh}$. A bounded choice of
preimage is $(R_XF,R_YM)$, and its squared norm is at most $2\|F\|_2^2$.
Conversely, every output of $\delta_{\Hh}$ is centered.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:source} and Proposition~\ref{prop:recovery} give ambient
outputs $F-M$ and $M-\E F$. Their sum is $F-\E F\in\Hh$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:project} gives the asserted intrinsic output. Conditional
expectation is a contraction, and hence the sum of the two field norms
squared is at most $\|F\|_2^2+\|M\|_2^2\leq2\|F\|_2^2$.
Finally $1\in\Dom D_{\Hh}$ and $D_{\Hh}1=0$. Testing any adjoint output
against $1$ gives expectation zero.
\end{proof}

from predictable representation in physical IBM time.

\section{Actual multitime Fourier observations}
Fix a finite index set $I$, times $r_i\geq0$, real frequencies $a_{ki}$,
phases $\beta_k$, and real weights $w_k$, where $k\geq0$. Assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:valuecost}
 \sum_{k\geq0}|w_k|<\infty.
\end{equation}
The uniformly absolutely convergent series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F}
 F=\sum_{k\geq0}w_k\cos\left(\beta_k+\sum_{i\in I}a_{ki}Z_{r_i}\right)
\end{equation}
defines a bounded intrinsic random variable. For $b\in\R^I$ set
\[
 A(b)=\sum_i|b_i|,\qquad
 Q_b(x)=\sum_{i,j}b_i b_j\min(|x_i|,|x_j|).
\]
The nonnegativity of this expression is essential: signed frequencies
cannot be handled by pretending that its individual summands are positive.

\begin{lemma}[Folded Gram identity]\label{lem:gram}
For every $x\in\R^I$,
\[
 Q_b(x)=\left\|\sum_i b_i1_{(0,|x_i|]}\right\|_{L^2(\R_+)}^2\geq0.
\]
Moreover the actual conditional source mean is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M(Y)=\E_XF
 =\sum_k w_k\cos\beta_k\exp\left(-\tfrac12 Q_{a_k}((Y_{r_i})_i)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The inner product of $1_{(0,s]}$ and $1_{(0,t]}$ is $\min(s,t)$;
expanding the norm proves the first claim. For a fixed clock realization,
$\sum_i b_iX_{|Y_{r_i}|}$ is a centered real Gaussian with variance
$Q_b((Y_{r_i})_i)$. Its characteristic function at frequency one is
$\exp(-Q_b/2)$. Taking the real part after multiplication by $e^{i\beta_k}$
gives the expectation of each cosine. The summable constant $\sum_k|w_k|$
dominates the series, so Fubini or dominated convergence interchanges the
source integral with the sum. This also proves $\E_YM=\E F$.
\end{proof}

\section{Strong graph rules at folds and collisions}
We use the original closed Gaussian derivative $D$ on the clock factor.
Its bounded $C^1$ chain rule says that bounded $\phi$ with continuous bounded
derivative satisfies $D\phi(f)=\phi'(f)Df$ for $f\in\Dom D$. This is the
closed extension of cylindrical differentiation: take a core approximation
to $f$, use bounded slopes for scalar convergence, and dominated multiplier
convergence for the derivative. For the latter, split the derivative error
as $\phi'(f_n)(Df_n-Df)+(\phi'(f_n)-\phi'(f))Df$; convergence in probability
with a uniform bound gives convergence in $L^2$ against the fixed field.
Closedness then gives the original graph identity.

\begin{theorem}[Absolute values and minima]\label{thm:cusp}
If $f,h\in\Dom D$, then $|f|,\min(f,h)\in\Dom D$ and
\begin{align*}
 D|f|&=\sgn(f)Df,\\
 D\min(f,h)&=\tfrac12\{Df+Dh-\sgn(f-h)(Df-Dh)\}.
\end{align*}
Here $\sgn(0)=0$. The respective norm bounds are $\|Df\|_2$ and
$\|Df\|_2+\|Dh\|_2$. No null-level-set hypothesis is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $\varepsilon_n=(n+1)^{-1}$, $r_\varepsilon(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\varepsilon^2}$,
and
\[
 \phi_n(x)=\frac{\sin(\varepsilon_n r_{\varepsilon_n}(x))}{\varepsilon_n}.
\]
For each fixed $n$ the function is bounded, continuously differentiable and
\[
 \phi_n'(x)=\cos(\varepsilon_n r_{\varepsilon_n}(x))
                  \frac{x}{r_{\varepsilon_n}(x)},\quad
 |\phi_n'|\leq1,\quad |\phi_n(x)|\leq |x|+1.
\]
Pointwise, $\phi_n(x)\to|x|$ and $\phi_n'(x)\to\sgn(x)$, including $x=0$.
The chain rule applies separately for every $n$. Dominated convergence gives
$\phi_n(f)\to|f|$ in $L^2$, since $f\in L^2$ on a probability space, and
$\phi_n'(f)Df\to\sgn(f)Df$ in the field $L^2$ norm. Closedness proves the
absolute-value identity and its norm bound. Now use
$\min(f,h)=(f+h-|f-h|)/2$ and linearity of the closed derivative.
For its norm, apply the triangle inequality and $|\sgn|\leq1$ to obtain
$\frac12(\|Df\|+\|Dh\|+\|Df-Dh\|)\leq\|Df\|+\|Dh\|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Reflected damping]\label{lem:damping}
For $c\geq0$ and $f\in\Dom D$,
\[
 D e^{-c|f|}=-c e^{-c|f|}\sgn(f)Df,
 \qquad \|D e^{-c|f|}\|_2\leq c\|Df\|_2.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use $\psi_n(x)=\exp(-c r_{\varepsilon_n}(x))$. These are bounded by one and
have continuous derivatives
$-c\psi_n(x)x/r_{\varepsilon_n}(x)$ bounded in modulus by $c$.
The derivatives converge pointwise to $-ce^{-c|x|}\sgn(x)$.
Apply the bounded chain rule, scalar and field dominated convergence,
and closedness, just as in Theorem~\ref{thm:cusp}.
\end{proof}

\section{The source mean in the original clock domain}
Let $e_r=1_{(0,r]}$ be the clock Gaussian direction, so
$DY_r=e_r$ and $\|e_r\|=\sqrt r$. Introduce the actual random direction fields
\begin{align*}
 v_r&=\sgn(Y_r)e_r,\\
 H_{rs}&=\tfrac12\{v_r+v_s-\sgn(|Y_r|-|Y_s|)(v_r-v_s)\},\\
 V_b&=\sum_{i,j}b_i b_j H_{r_i r_j},\qquad

\[
 D|Y_r|=v_r,\quad D\min(|Y_r|,|Y_s|)=H_{rs},\quad DQ_b(Y)=V_b.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cost}
 \|V_b\|_2\leq C_b
 \leq2A(b)^2\sum_i\sqrt{r_i}.

For the last inequality, the exact double sum equals
$2A(b)\sum_i|b_i|\sqrt{r_i}$, which is at most the stated bound.
All sums over $I$ are finite, so these operations are legitimate in the

\begin{theorem}[Completed mean graph]\label{thm:meangraph}
In addition to \eqref{eq:valuecost}, suppose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:curvaturecost}
 \sum_k |w_k|A(a_k)^2<\infty.
\end{equation}
Then the source mean in \eqref{eq:M} belongs to $\Dom D_Y$ and
\[
 D_YM=G:=\sum_k w_k\cos\beta_k\,g_{a_k},\qquad
 \|G\|_2\leq
 \left(\sum_k|w_k|A(a_k)^2\right)\sum_i\sqrt{r_i}.
\]
Both the scalar series defining $M$ and this vector series are norm summable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The scalar summand is bounded by $|w_k|$. Equations~\eqref{eq:cost}
and \eqref{eq:dampinggraph} bound the derivative summand by
$|w_k|A(a_k)^2\sum_i\sqrt{r_i}$. Thus the partial sums converge in both
components of the original graph. Closedness proves membership and the
output formula; the triangle inequality gives the bound. If representatives
are desired, $L^1$ summability of the pointwise field norms follows from the
$L^2$ estimates on a probability space. Tonelli then gives almost-everywhere
absolute convergence to a representative of the same $L^2$ field.
\end{proof}
\lean{IBMFourierClockGraph}{multitime_clock_graph; multitime_clock_domain}
The conclusion concerns $M$, not $F$. A smoothing effect of source averaging
does not transfer original clock-domain membership back to the observation.
The summability condition is sufficient, not claimed necessary or sharp.

\section{Predictable representation of the hidden clock mean}
Let $\mathcal F_t^Y=\sigma(Y_s:0\leq s\leq t)$, using the raw Brownian past
and its $L^2$ equivalence classes. Let $\mathcal P_Y$ be the closed predictable
energy space in $L^2(ds\,d\mathbf P_Y)$, obtained by completing adapted step
fields. Its original It\^o integral
\[
 J:\mathcal P_Y\longrightarrow L^2(\Omega_Y)
\]
is an isometry with centered outputs. Let $\mathcal B_Y$ be the closed
$L^2$ space generated by the Brownian path, and let $P_{\rm pred}$ be
orthogonal projection of direction fields onto $\mathcal P_Y$.

\begin{theorem}[Brownian range and uniqueness]\label{thm:prp}
The range of $J$ is exactly the centered subspace of $\mathcal B_Y$.
For every $M\in\mathcal B_Y$, its unique predictable coefficient is
$U=J^*M$, and
\[
 JU=M-\E M,\qquad \|U\|_2=\|M-\E M\|_2\leq\|M\|_2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The isometry has closed range. It remains to prove that a dense collection
of centered Brownian functionals lies in that range. For deterministic step
$h$ of compact support, put
\[
 E_t(h)=\exp\left(\int_0^t h_s\,dY_s-\frac12\int_0^t h_s^2\,ds\right).
\]
The elementary Brownian It\^o formula gives
$E_T(h)-1=J(1_{[0,T]}h E_\cdot(h))$. Its field has finite energy because
$\E E_s(h)^2=\exp(\int_0^s h^2)$. These exponentials span a dense subspace
of $\mathcal B_Y$: if a variable is orthogonal to all of them, condition
on any finite vector of independent Brownian increments. In finite Gaussian
dimension its Laplace transform after multiplication by the Gaussian density
vanishes at every real vector. Cauchy--Schwarz with Gaussian exponential
moments makes this transform entire, so successive one-variable uniqueness
gives a vanishing Fourier transform and hence the zero conditional variable.
An increasing collection of rational-time vectors generates the continuous
Brownian path; convergence of conditional expectations then makes the
original variable zero. This proves density.

Centering is continuous, so centered exponentials have dense span in the
centered Brownian space. Closed range now proves surjectivity onto that
space. For any isometry $J$, $J^*J=1$ and $JJ^*$ is orthogonal projection
onto its range. The orthogonal projection of $M\in\mathcal B_Y$ onto the
centered subspace is $M-\E M$. This proves the formula. Isometry proves
uniqueness and the norm equality.
\end{proof}
\lean{GaussianBrownianPRP}{integralRange_eq_centeredBrownianL2; brownian_representation_exists_unique}
The range claim is deliberately restricted to $\mathcal B_Y$. It would be
false for unrelated Gaussian coordinates in a larger ambient realization.
For the source mean \eqref{eq:M}, membership in $\mathcal B_Y$ was established
by its actual finite clock-vector formula.

\begin{proposition}[Support and stability]\label{prop:support}
If $M$ is measurable for $\mathcal F_T^Y$, its coefficient $U$ vanishes
after $T$. For any two Brownian-generated inputs,
$\|J^*M-J^*M'\|_2\leq\|M-M'\|_2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For predictable step fields, independence of future Brownian increments and
conditional expectation give
$\E(Ju\mid\mathcal F_T^Y)=J(1_{[0,T]}u)$. Extend by the integral isometry
and contraction of conditional expectation. Apply this identity to $U$.
Since $JU=M-\E M$ is past measurable, $J(1_{(T,\infty)}U)=0$; the isometry
forces the corresponding field to vanish. Finally the adjoint of an
isometry has operator norm at most one, proving stability.
\end{proof}
\lean{IBMFourierClockClarkOcone}{coefficient_supported; finite_reconstruction; coefficient_stability}

\section{Identifying the coefficient by the original derivative}
The representation theorem requires only $M\in\mathcal B_Y$. The next
identity requires membership in the original closed derivative domain.

\begin{theorem}[Projected derivative]\label{thm:co}
If $M\in\Dom D_Y\cap\mathcal B_Y$, then $J^*M=P_{\rm pred}D_YM$.
Consequently, under \eqref{eq:valuecost} and \eqref{eq:curvaturecost}, the
actual Fourier source mean has predictable coefficient $P_{\rm pred}G$,
where $G$ is the vector series in Theorem~\ref{thm:meangraph}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
First take a smooth bounded past cylinder $H$ measurable at time $a$ and
the step field $u_s=H1_{(a,b]}(s)$. Its integral is $H(Y_b-Y_a)$.
Gaussian integration by parts in the future increment direction gives
\[
 \E\{M H(Y_b-Y_a)\}
 =\E\left\{H\int_a^b D_sM\,ds\right\}.
\]
The additional product-rule term involving $D_sH$ is zero for $s\in(a,b]$,
because every direction generating $H$ is supported in $[0,a]$.
This calculation is first for cylindrical $M$. Approximation in its original
derivative graph extends it to all $M\in\Dom D_Y$: the integral side is
continuous in scalar $L^2$, and the right side is continuous in the field
$L^2$ norm, for each fixed bounded step field.

Bounded smooth past cylinders are dense in the past $L^2$ space, by finite
Gaussian conditional approximation. Their predictable step fields are dense
in $\mathcal P_Y$ by its definition. The integral isometry and
Cauchy--Schwarz therefore extend the pairing identity to every
$u\in\mathcal P_Y$. It reads
$\langle J^*M,u\rangle=\langle D_YM,u\rangle$.
Since $J^*M$ lies in $\mathcal P_Y$, this is precisely the orthogonal
projection characterization. Substitute Theorem~\ref{thm:meangraph} for
the Fourier case.
\end{proof}
\lean{GaussianBrownianPRP}{clark_ocone; clark_ocone_representer_unique}
\lean{IBMFourierClockClarkOcone}{projected_actual_derivative; stochastic_reconstruction}
For adapted step fields the same calculation proves
$\delta_Yu=Ju$ in the original Gaussian adjoint graph. Approximation by
steps, isometry and closedness extend this equality to every predictable
energy field. This domain assertion will be used in the hybrid theorem.

\section{Hybrid reconstruction through the original intrinsic adjoint}
For a clock-only predictable field $U$, write $C(U)$ for its lift to the
product space, constant in the scenery coordinate. This is an isometry.
Testing on product cylinders and using density gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lift}
 C(U)\in\Dom\delta_Y,\qquad \delta_Y C(U)=JU.
\end{equation}
Indeed the clock-only adjoint identity integrates over each scenery
coordinate, and finite tensor approximation extends it to the full partial
derivative domain. Equivalently, take a clock adjoint graph approximation
and transport its constant-scenery tensor through the closed partial graph.
No observable or predictable projection is commuted with a derivative.

\begin{theorem}[Actual Fourier hybrid identity]\label{thm:hybrid}
Assume only \eqref{eq:valuecost}, and let $F$ be \eqref{eq:F}, $M=\E_XF$,
and $U=J^*M$. Then
\[
 (R_XF,C(U))\in\Dom\delta_{\Hh},\qquad
 \delta_{\Hh}(R_XF,C(U))=F-\E F.
\]
For every $f\in\Dom D_{\Hh}$ one has the exact duality
\[
 \E\{(F-\E F)f\}
 =\langle R_XF,D_Xf\rangle+\langle C(U),D_Yf\rangle.
\]
The squared norm of the pair is at most $2\|F\|_2^2$.
If all $r_i\leq T$, the clock field is supported in $[0,T]$.
Under \eqref{eq:curvaturecost} it is the lift of $P_{\rm pred}G$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The actual source-mean formula makes $M$ Brownian generated.
Equation~\eqref{eq:source}, Theorem~\ref{thm:prp} and \eqref{eq:lift} give
the original ambient adjoint outputs
\[
 \delta_XR_XF=F-M,\qquad
 \delta_Y C(U)=M-\E_YM=M-\E F.
\]
Their sum is the intrinsic variable $F-\E F$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:project} removes the final projection on this \emph{sum}.
Its proof also gives the duality against every original joint-domain test.
The squared field norm is at most
$\|F\|_2^2+\|M-\E M\|_2^2\leq2\|F\|_2^2$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:support} gives time support. Only the last projected
derivative identification uses Theorem~\ref{thm:meangraph} and therefore
the stronger summability hypothesis.
\end{proof}
\lean{IBMFourierHybridReconstruction}{partial_graph; clock_mem; clock_value; reconstruction; duality}
The norm estimate here is a direct human deduction from the mapped original
recovery bound and Brownian isometry. It is not a new formal declaration or
a claim that this joint preimage is unique. A joint divergence may have a
kernel even though its clock-only predictable coefficient is unique.

\section{A nonzero observable with zero clock mean}
Take $T>0$ and $F=\sin Z_T$, which is a one-term Fourier observation after
a phase change. Conditional on $Y$, the scenery value $Z_T$ is centered
Gaussian of variance $|Y_T|$. Symmetry gives
\[
 M=\E_X\sin Z_T=0,\qquad U=J^*M=0.
\]
Nevertheless the double-angle identity and the Gaussian characteristic
function give
\[
 \E F=0,\qquad
 \E F^2=\tfrac12\left(1-\E e^{-2|Y_T|}\right)>0.
\]
The strict inequality follows because $Y_T$ is nondegenerate, so
$|Y_T|>0$ almost surely and $e^{-2|Y_T|}<1$ almost surely. In particular,
the entire predictable hidden-clock channel vanishes while $F$ is nonzero.
The hybrid theorem instead reads $\delta_{\Hh}(R_XF,0)=F$.
\lean{IBMFourierClockClarkOcone}{coefficient_zero_of_cos_zero}
\lean{IBMActualSineDomainObstruction}{sinePoint_not_constant; sinePoint_not_joint_domain}
The verified source additionally proves that this positive-time sine
observation is outside the original joint derivative domain. That separate
domain obstruction is not needed in the variance proof above and is not
reproved here. It is consistent with our theorem: membership of an
\emph{output} in the range of an adjoint does not require it to be in the
domain of the operator being adjointed. The paper's claim of complete
reconstruction is therefore a claim about an original adjoint output, not
point-observation differentiability.

\section{Classical context and precise remaining obligations}
The Gaussian recovery $DN^{-1}$ on centered chaoses, Brownian predictable
representation and the projected-derivative mechanism are classical.
Ocone's primary institutional publication record \cite{ocone} describes
the extension of Clark's formula to weakly $H$-differentiable functionals.
This supplies relevant historical context. Our bounded search inspected
that record and abstract, not the complete article; it does not establish
priority for any specific IBM formulation or match all analytic hypotheses.
The paper claims neither a new Clark--Ocone theorem nor external novelty.
Its contribution as a formalization companion is the explicit alignment of
actual folded covariance, original cusp graph, completed Fourier mean,
predictable clock coefficient and projected intrinsic adjoint.

Three qualifications are mathematical requirements, rather than choices of
terminology. First, $\mathcal F_t^Y$ is the hidden clock's Brownian past.
The observed past $\sigma(Z_s:s\leq t)$ is a different sigma algebra.
Nothing above identifies $U$ as predictable for that observed past.
Second, $R_XF$ is a static Gaussian source field; it is not constructed as
a physical-time predictable IBM integrand. Third, even the full observable
projection $P$ used in the adjoint identity is not a causal projection.
Dropping any of these distinctions would change the theorem.

To obtain an observed-filtration physical-time reconstruction, one would
still need a specified observed predictable integral space, an original
closed integral realizing the desired physical prescription, admissibility
of the proposed coefficients in that space, and a proof that its centered
residual vanishes in the stated topology. The sine test shows why success
for conditional source means cannot discharge those obligations. Existing
Simpson identities and native-grid weak-limit obstructions retain their own
domains and topologies; no transfer to arbitrary partitions, nonsmooth
physical-time formulas or a predictable representation property is asserted.
