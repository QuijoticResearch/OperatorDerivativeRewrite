\begin{abstract}
Let $Z_t=X_{|Y_t|}$ for independent Brownian motions $X,Y$.
We reconstruct the closed-operator argument that combines static recovery in
the scenery variable with a predictable integral in the hidden Brownian clock.
For absolutely summable multitime Fourier observations, the two channels
recover the entire centered observable through the original joint intrinsic
divergence. Static contraction gives a constant-one native Poincar\'e
bound and an actual centered Poisson solution with minimum-energy field.
A stronger frequency summability condition puts the conditional
source mean in the original clock derivative domain and identifies the clock
integrand by predictable projection. Fold and collision cusps are treated by
strong graph approximation, including their zero levels. Complete proofs
separate ambient adjoints, observable projection, hidden-clock predictability
and observed-process causality. Original-domain multipliers and an exact
conditional-L1 criterion for the native adjoint are proved, retaining the
full observation projection and all bounded-test hypotheses. A complete
projected-adjoint graph-core theorem retains independent observable-domain
density, with explicit distinctions between closure, initial domains and
separately admissible joint pairs. A sine observation has zero source mean but
positive variance, so clock-mean representation alone cannot reconstruct IBM.
A bounded native coefficient with bounded gradient also acts continuously on the full intrinsic adjoint graph dual, giving a Leibniz rule with an ordinary L2 correction for every rough observable. This is an exposition of verified results and classical Gaussian mechanisms,
with no claim of completed physical-time IBM calculus or external novelty.
\end{abstract}

If $u\in\Dom\delta_X$ and $v\in\Dom\delta_Y$, then

\]
The last expression is bounded in $\|f\|_2$. This is exactly adjoint-domain
membership, and density makes its representing vector unique.

\subsection*{Bounded graph approximation and bounded separating tests}
We first record the exact approximation facts needed for domain claims.
The closed Gaussian chain rule gives
\[
 D\phi(v)=\phi'(v)Dv\qquad(v\in\Dom D)
\]
when $\phi$ is bounded $C^1$ with continuous bounded derivative.
It applies in each ambient Gaussian factor. The composed scalar remains
$\mathcal O$-measurable, so the two graph identities belong to the
original restricted joint domain. Here is the strong-graph mechanism:
approximate in the original Gaussian graph by smooth cylindrical
functions, apply the finite chain rule, and select an almost-everywhere
convergent subsequence of the values. Bounded derivatives control the
product with the converging gradients; dominated convergence controls
$(\phi'(v_n)-\phi'(v))Dv$. Boundedness of $\phi'$ also gives convergence
of the values. Closedness identifies the limit in each graph. Smooth
approximations of $\phi$ on finite-dimensional cylinders give the same
argument for $C^1$ functions. This is the usual Gaussian Sobolev chain
mechanism, not an IBM physical-time chain rule.

Take $\chi_n(x)=n\tanh(x/n)$, $n\geq1$. These are bounded smooth
functions with $|\chi_n(x)|\leq|x|$, $|\chi_n'(x)|\leq1$,
$\chi_n(x)\to x$ and $\chi_n'(x)\to1$. Thus, for every $v\in\Dom D$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeboundedgraph}
 v_n=\chi_n(v)\longrightarrow v\text{ in }L^2,\qquad
 Dv_n=\chi_n'(v)Dv\longrightarrow Dv\text{ in }\mathcal V.
\end{equation}
Both convergences follow by dominated convergence, using $|v|^2$ and
$\|Dv\|^2$ respectively. In particular bounded domain elements form a
strong graph core. The displayed hyperbolic truncations are a human
choice of approximation; the source proves the same graph-core property
with its own bounded smooth approximants.

A different fact is needed to separate $L^1$ densities. If $F\in\Hh$
and $|F|\leq1$, density of $\Dom D$ gives $g_n\to F$ in $L^2$.
Let $\chi(x)=\max(-1,\min(1,x))$. Convolve it with a nonnegative
smooth unit-mass mollifier supported in $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$.
The result $\chi_\varepsilon$ is smooth, takes values in $[-1,1]$,
has derivative bounded by one and satisfies
$\sup_x|\chi_\varepsilon(x)-\chi(x)|\leq\varepsilon$.
The chain rule puts $u_n=\chi_{1/n}(g_n)$ in $\Dom D$, and
\[
 |u_n|\leq1,\qquad
 \|u_n-F\|_2\leq n^{-1}+\|g_n-F\|_2\longrightarrow0.
\]
We used that clipping is nonexpansive and fixes $F$.
The formal source uses the exact clipping identity
$\chi(x)=(|x+1|-|x-1|)/2$ and its proved absolute-value chain rule;
the smooth construction here supplies the same value approximation.
No convergence of the derivatives of $u_n$ is claimed in this statement.

Consequently an $\mathcal O$-measurable $a\in L^1$ satisfying
$\E[va]=0$ for every $v\in\Dom D$ with $|v|\leq1$ must vanish.
Apply the approximation to $F=\operatorname{sgn}(a)$, with sign zero
at zero. An $L^2$-convergent sequence has an almost-everywhere convergent
subsequence. Along it, $|u_na|\leq|a|$, so dominated convergence gives
$\E|a|=\lim_n\E[u_na]=0$. This proves $a=0$ almost surely.
The distinction between graph approximation and uniformly bounded value
approximation is essential to the following proof.

\subsection*{An exact $L^1$-to-adjoint criterion}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:conditionaladjoint}
Suppose $U\in\mathcal V$ and $q\in L^1$ satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeweakpair}
 \langle U,Dv\rangle_{\mathcal V}=\E[vq]
 \quad\text{for every bounded }v\in\Dom D.
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 U\in\Dom\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Cq\in L^2.
\]
When these conditions hold, $\delta U=Cq$ as an actual intrinsic $L^2$
class. For unbounded domain tests the valid full identity is
$\langle U,Dv\rangle=\langle Cq,v\rangle_{L^2}$; integrability of
$vq$ is not asserted for arbitrary $v\in\Dom D$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For every bounded observable $v$, conditional expectation gives
$\E[vq]=\E[vCq]$, with both products integrable.
If $U\in\Dom\delta$ and $y=\delta U$, adjoint duality and
\eqref{eq:nativeweakpair} imply $\E[v(Cq-y)]=0$ for all bounded
domain tests. The difference $Cq-y$ is observable and integrable,
since the probability space has mass one and $y\in L^2$.
The separating-test argument just proved gives $Cq=y$ almost surely,
and therefore $Cq\in L^2$.

Conversely suppose $Cq\in L^2$. The bounded-test identity becomes
$\langle U,Dv\rangle=\langle Cq,v\rangle$. For an arbitrary domain
element use \eqref{eq:nativeboundedgraph}; convergence on both sides
extends the identity to it. Thus
$|\langle U,Dv\rangle|\leq\|Cq\|_2\|v\|_2$ on the whole domain,
which is exactly membership in the Hilbert adjoint graph. Density of
the original domain makes its output uniquely $Cq$.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Bounded products and the native divergence rule}
If $f,v\in\Dom D$ are bounded, their product belongs to $\Dom D$ and
\[
 D(fv)=fDv+vDf.
\]
To see this on the original graphs, choose a bounded smooth function
agreeing with $x^2$ on a neighborhood of the common bounded ranges of
$f$, $v$ and $f+v$. The closed chain rule and
$2fv=(f+v)^2-f^2-v^2$ prove the formula in each component.
All terms are square integrable because both scalar factors are bounded.

Now let $f\in\Dom D$ be bounded and $U\in\Dom\delta$, without yet
assuming an essentially bounded derivative of $f$. The actual density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativedensity}
 q=f\delta U-\langle Df,U\rangle_{H_X\oplus H_Y}
\end{equation}
belongs to $L^1$: the first product is in $L^2$, and Cauchy--Schwarz
makes the field contraction integrable. Also $fU\in\mathcal V$.
For a bounded domain test $v$, apply adjoint duality to $fv$:
\[
 \langle fU,Dv\rangle
 =\langle U,D(fv)\rangle-\E[v\langle Df,U\rangle]
 =\E[vq].
\]
Theorem~\ref{thm:conditionaladjoint} therefore gives the exact rule
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeweightedcriterion}
 fU\in\Dom\delta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 C\!\left(f\delta U-\langle Df,U\rangle\right)\in L^2.
\end{equation}
When it holds, the displayed conditional density is the adjoint value.
Boundedness of $f$ alone does not establish this remaining $L^2$ condition.
One need not demand that the unprojected $L^1$ density itself be in $L^2$;
the criterion concerns its actual conditional expectation.

\section{Static Gaussian recovery before predictability}
We recall a Gaussian fact in a form that fixes all normalizations. Let $D$
be the closed Gaussian derivative on one factor, $\delta=D^*$, and
$L^2=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}\mathcal C_n$ its orthogonal Hermite-chaos expansion.
For $f_n\in\mathcal C_n$, the original derivative has
\[
 \|Df_n\|_2^2=n\|f_n\|_2^2,\qquad \delta Df_n=nf_n.
\]
The identities follow first for finite products of normalized Hermite
polynomials, by one-variable Gaussian integration by parts and orthogonality,
and then by closure on each chaos. Derivative images from different chaos
orders are orthogonal as direction fields.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded original recovery]\label{prop:recovery}
For $f=\sum_{n\geq0}f_n\in L^2$, define
\[
 Rf=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac1n Df_n.
\]
Then $Rf\in\Dom\delta$, $\delta Rf=f-\E f$, and
\[
 \|Rf\|_2^2=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac1n\|f_n\|_2^2\leq\|f\|_2^2.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Orthogonality and the displayed derivative norm show that the field series
converges and give its norm formula. For finite chaos sums, Gaussian
integration by parts gives $\delta\sum_{n=1}^Nn^{-1}Df_n=\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n$.
The fields and outputs both converge strongly. Since a Hilbert adjoint is
closed, the limiting pair belongs to the original adjoint graph. Completeness
of Hermite polynomials identifies the scalar limit as $f-\E f$.
\end{proof}
This proof constructs a static Gaussian field. It supplies no time ordering
or predictable representative.

Apply $R$ fiberwise in the scenery coordinate, writing the bounded map as
$R_X$. Fubini and the norm bound give a bounded map on the product $L^2$
space. Approximate a product-space input by finite sums of tensors, use the
one-factor adjoint identity, and pass to the original closed partial adjoint
graph. Consequently, for every $F\in\mathcal K$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source}
 R_XF\in\Dom\delta_X,\qquad
 \delta_XR_XF=F-M,\qquad M=\E_XF,\qquad
 \|R_XF\|_2\leq\|F\|_2.
\end{equation}
Here and below a clock-only variable is lifted to the product space when
needed. Tensor approximation is legitimate because conditional expectation,
the recovery map and the tensor transports are bounded. It does not assert
that conditional expectation preserves an arbitrary derivative domain.

\begin{proposition}[Static centered surjectivity]\label{prop:static}
Every centered $F\in\Hh$ is an output of $\delta_{\Hh}$. A bounded choice of
preimage is $(R_XF,R_YM)$, and its squared norm is at most $2\|F\|_2^2$.
Conversely, every output of $\delta_{\Hh}$ is centered.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:source} and Proposition~\ref{prop:recovery} give ambient
outputs $F-M$ and $M-\E F$. Their sum is $F-\E F\in\Hh$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:project} gives the asserted intrinsic output. Conditional
expectation is a contraction, and hence the sum of the two field norms
squared is at most $\|F\|_2^2+\|M\|_2^2\leq2\|F\|_2^2$.
Finally $1\in\Dom D_{\Hh}$ and $D_{\Hh}1=0$. Testing any adjoint output
against $1$ gives expectation zero.
\end{proof}

\section{Native Poincar\'e control and centered Poisson solutions}

Thus one can choose a linear static preimage $\mathcal RF$ of every
centered $F\in\Hh$ with $\|\mathcal RF\|\leq\|F\|_2$.

\begin{proposition}[Constant-one native Poincar\'e bound]\label{prop:nativepoincare}
For every $f\in\Dom D$,
\[
 \|f-\E f\|_2\leq\|Df\|_2.
\]
The kernel of $D$ consists exactly of the constants in its domain.
No optimality of the numerical constant is asserted.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $g=f-\E f$. Constants lie in $\Dom D$ with derivative zero, so
$g\in\Dom D$ and $Dg=Df$. Static recovery and original adjointness give
\[
 \|g\|_2^2=\langle\delta\mathcal Rg,g\rangle
           =\langle\mathcal Rg,Dg\rangle
           \leq\|g\|_2\|Df\|_2.
\]
If $g=0$ the inequality is immediate; otherwise divide by $\|g\|_2$.
Zero derivative therefore forces $g=0$, and constants have zero derivative.
\end{proof}

To solve a centered native Poisson equation, first construct its actual
resolvent without assuming a differential expression for a generator.
The graph of the original closed $D$ is a closed subspace of the Hilbert
product $\Hh\oplus\mathcal V$, where $\mathcal V$ is the original field

Projection and coordinate extraction are bounded linear. Orthogonality

\begin{equation}\label{eq:nativeweakQ}
 \langle Qx,v\rangle+\langle DQx,Dv\rangle=\langle x,v\rangle
                             \quad(v\in\Dom D).
\end{equation}
Consequently $DQx\in\Dom\delta$ and $\delta DQx=x-Qx$, exactly the
adjoint definition. Testing with $1$ shows $\E Qx=\E x$.
Testing with $v=Qx$ gives
\[
 \|Qx\|_2^2+\|DQx\|_2^2=\langle x,Qx\rangle.
\]
If $x$ is centered, then $Qx$ is centered and Poincar\'e bounds its first
norm by the second. Thus $2\|Qx\|_2^2\leq\|x\|_2\|Qx\|_2$,
and $\|Qx\|_2\leq\|x\|_2/2$, including the zero case.
This is a contraction statement on the centered subspace, not on constants.

\begin{theorem}[Actual native centered Poisson solution]\label{thm:nativepoisson}
For each centered $x\in\Hh$, there is a unique centered $u\in\Dom D$
such that $Du\in\Dom\delta$ and $\delta Du=x$. It satisfies
\[
 \|u\|_2\leq\|Du\|_2\leq\|x\|_2.
\]
Among all original fields $V\in\Dom\delta$ with $\delta V=x$, the field
$Du$ is the unique minimum-norm one, with exact gap
\[
 \|V\|_2^2=\|Du\|_2^2+\|V-Du\|_2^2.
\]
The solution depends linearly and continuously on the centered input.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The mean-zero subspace $\Hh_0$ is closed and complete, because expectation
is a bounded linear functional. For fixed $x\in\Hh_0$, the map
$v\mapsto Q(x+v)$ preserves $\Hh_0$ and has Lipschitz constant at most
$1/2$, by the just-proved centered bound and linearity of $Q$.
Its unique fixed point $u$ satisfies $u=Q(x+u)$, so it belongs to the
original domain of $D$. The actual adjoint identity for $Q$ gives
$\delta Du=x+u-u=x$ and the required product-domain membership.
Testing this equation against $u$ yields
$\|Du\|_2^2=\langle x,u\rangle\leq\|x\|_2\|u\|_2
\leq\|x\|_2\|Du\|_2$. Division when nonzero proves the bounds.

If two centered solutions existed, their difference $w$ would satisfy
$\delta Dw=0$. Original adjointness gives $\|Dw\|_2^2=0$, and
Poincar\'e then forces $w=0$. This also proves linearity: linear
combinations of centered solutions solve the corresponding combined
input and must equal its unique solution. The norm bound gives continuity.

For any competing field $V$, the difference $V-Du$ belongs to the
original adjoint domain and has divergence zero. Thus
$\langle V-Du,Du\rangle=\langle\delta(V-Du),u\rangle=0$.
Expanding the square gives the exact gap; equality forces $V=Du$.
This is minimization in the whole original field space, without a
predictability constraint.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{A weak chain identity on the whole native energy domain}
Write $A=\delta D$ with its actual product domain. For $u\in\Dom A$,
let $y=Au\in\Hh$ and let
\[
 e_u(\omega)=\|D_Xu(\omega)\|^2+\|D_Yu(\omega)\|^2.
\]
This is a well-defined almost-everywhere nonnegative $L^1$ density,
with $\E e_u=\|Du\|_2^2$. Its square integrability or full-observation
measurability is not assumed. Let $\phi\in C^2(\R)$ have bounded first
and second derivatives. The original closed Gaussian chain rule,
restricted to observable inputs, gives
\[
 \phi(u),\ \phi'(u)\in\Dom D,\qquad
 D\phi(u)=\phi'(u)Du,\quad D\phi'(u)=\phi''(u)Du.
\]
Indeed $|\phi(u)|\leq|\phi(0)|+\|\phi'\|_\infty|u|$ ensures its $L^2$

\section{Actual multitime Fourier observations}
Fix a finite index set $I$, times $r_i\geq0$, real frequencies $a_{ki}$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:valuecost}
 \sum_{k\geq0}|w_k|<\infty.
\end{equation}
The uniformly absolutely convergent series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F}
 F=\sum_{k\geq0}w_k\cos\left(\beta_k+\sum_{i\in I}a_{ki}Z_{r_i}\right)
\end{equation}
defines a bounded intrinsic random variable. For $b\in\R^I$ set
\[
 A(b)=\sum_i|b_i|,\qquad

\]
The nonnegativity of this expression is essential: signed frequencies
cannot be handled by pretending that its individual summands are positive.

\begin{lemma}[Folded Gram identity]\label{lem:gram}
For every $x\in\R^I$,
\[
 Q_b(x)=\left\|\sum_i b_i1_{(0,|x_i|]}\right\|_{L^2(\R_+)}^2\geq0.
\]
Moreover the actual conditional source mean is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
 M(Y)=\E_XF
 =\sum_k w_k\cos\beta_k\exp\left(-\tfrac12 Q_{a_k}((Y_{r_i})_i)\right).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The inner product of $1_{(0,s]}$ and $1_{(0,t]}$ is $\min(s,t)$;
expanding the norm proves the first claim. For a fixed clock realization,
$\sum_i b_iX_{|Y_{r_i}|}$ is a centered real Gaussian with variance
$Q_b((Y_{r_i})_i)$. Its characteristic function at frequency one is
$\exp(-Q_b/2)$. Taking the real part after multiplication by $e^{i\beta_k}$
gives the expectation of each cosine. The summable constant $\sum_k|w_k|$
dominates the series, so Fubini or dominated convergence interchanges the
source integral with the sum. This also proves $\E_YM=\E F$.
\end{proof}

\section{Strong graph rules at folds and collisions}
We use the original closed Gaussian derivative $D$ on the clock factor.
Its bounded $C^1$ chain rule says that bounded $\phi$ with continuous bounded
derivative satisfies $D\phi(f)=\phi'(f)Df$ for $f\in\Dom D$. This is the
closed extension of cylindrical differentiation: take a core approximation
to $f$, use bounded slopes for scalar convergence, and dominated multiplier
convergence for the derivative. For the latter, split the derivative error
as $\phi'(f_n)(Df_n-Df)+(\phi'(f_n)-\phi'(f))Df$; convergence in probability
with a uniform bound gives convergence in $L^2$ against the fixed field.
Closedness then gives the original graph identity.

\begin{theorem}[Absolute values and minima]\label{thm:cusp}
If $f,h\in\Dom D$, then $|f|,\min(f,h)\in\Dom D$ and
\begin{align*}
 D|f|&=\sgn(f)Df,\\
 D\min(f,h)&=\tfrac12\{Df+Dh-\sgn(f-h)(Df-Dh)\}.
\end{align*}
Here $\sgn(0)=0$. The respective norm bounds are $\|Df\|_2$ and
$\|Df\|_2+\|Dh\|_2$. No null-level-set hypothesis is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $\varepsilon_n=(n+1)^{-1}$, $r_\varepsilon(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\varepsilon^2}$,
and
\[
 \phi_n(x)=\frac{\sin(\varepsilon_n r_{\varepsilon_n}(x))}{\varepsilon_n}.
\]
For each fixed $n$ the function is bounded, continuously differentiable and
\[
 \phi_n'(x)=\cos(\varepsilon_n r_{\varepsilon_n}(x))
                  \frac{x}{r_{\varepsilon_n}(x)},\quad
 |\phi_n'|\leq1,\quad |\phi_n(x)|\leq |x|+1.
\]
Pointwise, $\phi_n(x)\to|x|$ and $\phi_n'(x)\to\sgn(x)$, including $x=0$.
The chain rule applies separately for every $n$. Dominated convergence gives
$\phi_n(f)\to|f|$ in $L^2$, since $f\in L^2$ on a probability space, and
$\phi_n'(f)Df\to\sgn(f)Df$ in the field $L^2$ norm. Closedness proves the
absolute-value identity and its norm bound. Now use
$\min(f,h)=(f+h-|f-h|)/2$ and linearity of the closed derivative.
For its norm, apply the triangle inequality and $|\sgn|\leq1$ to obtain
$\frac12(\|Df\|+\|Dh\|+\|Df-Dh\|)\leq\|Df\|+\|Dh\|$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Reflected damping]\label{lem:damping}
For $c\geq0$ and $f\in\Dom D$,
\[
 D e^{-c|f|}=-c e^{-c|f|}\sgn(f)Df,
 \qquad \|D e^{-c|f|}\|_2\leq c\|Df\|_2.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use $\psi_n(x)=\exp(-c r_{\varepsilon_n}(x))$. These are bounded by one and
have continuous derivatives
$-c\psi_n(x)x/r_{\varepsilon_n}(x)$ bounded in modulus by $c$.
The derivatives converge pointwise to $-ce^{-c|x|}\sgn(x)$.
Apply the bounded chain rule, scalar and field dominated convergence,
and closedness, just as in Theorem~\ref{thm:cusp}.
\end{proof}

\section{Predictable representation of the hidden clock mean}
Let $\mathcal F_t^Y=\sigma(Y_s:0\leq s\leq t)$, using the raw Brownian past
and its $L^2$ equivalence classes. Let $\mathcal P_Y$ be the closed predictable
energy space in $L^2(ds\,d\mathbf P_Y)$, obtained by completing adapted step
fields. Its original It\^o integral
\[
 J:\mathcal P_Y\longrightarrow L^2(\Omega_Y)
\]
is an isometry with centered outputs. Let $\mathcal B_Y$ be the closed
$L^2$ space generated by the Brownian path, and let $P_{\rm pred}$ be
orthogonal projection of direction fields onto $\mathcal P_Y$.

\begin{theorem}[Brownian range and uniqueness]\label{thm:prp}
The range of $J$ is exactly the centered subspace of $\mathcal B_Y$.
For every $M\in\mathcal B_Y$, its unique predictable coefficient is
$U=J^*M$, and
\[
 JU=M-\E M,\qquad \|U\|_2=\|M-\E M\|_2\leq\|M\|_2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The isometry has closed range. It remains to prove that a dense collection
of centered Brownian functionals lies in that range. For deterministic step
$h$ of compact support, put
\[
 E_t(h)=\exp\left(\int_0^t h_s\,dY_s-\frac12\int_0^t h_s^2\,ds\right).
\]
The elementary Brownian It\^o formula gives
$E_T(h)-1=J(1_{[0,T]}h E_\cdot(h))$. Its field has finite energy because
$\E E_s(h)^2=\exp(\int_0^s h^2)$. These exponentials span a dense subspace
of $\mathcal B_Y$: if a variable is orthogonal to all of them, condition
on any finite vector of independent Brownian increments. In finite Gaussian
dimension its Laplace transform after multiplication by the Gaussian density
vanishes at every real vector. Cauchy--Schwarz with Gaussian exponential
moments makes this transform entire, so successive one-variable uniqueness
gives a vanishing Fourier transform and hence the zero conditional variable.
An increasing collection of rational-time vectors generates the continuous
Brownian path; convergence of conditional expectations then makes the
original variable zero. This proves density.

Centering is continuous, so centered exponentials have dense span in the
centered Brownian space. Closed range now proves surjectivity onto that
space. For any isometry $J$, $J^*J=1$ and $JJ^*$ is orthogonal projection
onto its range. The orthogonal projection of $M\in\mathcal B_Y$ onto the
centered subspace is $M-\E M$. This proves the formula. Isometry proves
uniqueness and the norm equality.
\end{proof}

The range claim is deliberately restricted to $\mathcal B_Y$. It would be
false for unrelated Gaussian coordinates in a larger ambient realization.
For the source mean \eqref{eq:M}, membership in $\mathcal B_Y$ was established
by its actual finite clock-vector formula.

\begin{proposition}[Support and stability]\label{prop:support}
If $M$ is measurable for $\mathcal F_T^Y$, its coefficient $U$ vanishes
after $T$. For any two Brownian-generated inputs,
$\|J^*M-J^*M'\|_2\leq\|M-M'\|_2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For predictable step fields, independence of future Brownian increments and
conditional expectation give
$\E(Ju\mid\mathcal F_T^Y)=J(1_{[0,T]}u)$. Extend by the integral isometry
and contraction of conditional expectation. Apply this identity to $U$.
Since $JU=M-\E M$ is past measurable, $J(1_{(T,\infty)}U)=0$; the isometry
forces the corresponding field to vanish. Finally the adjoint of an
isometry has operator norm at most one, proving stability.
\end{proof}

\section{When projected source adjoints form a full graph core}
\label{sec:observablecore}
The projected-adjoint inclusion in Section 1 has a precise general
closure theorem. This is classical Hilbert operator theory, and its
density hypotheses must be checked separately for an observable
restriction. We give its proof to distinguish graph closure from
equality of the initially assigned domains.

Let $H,E$ be real Hilbert spaces, let $A:\Dom A\subset H\to E$
be densely defined and closed, and let $V\subset H$ be a closed
linear subspace with orthogonal projection $P_V:H\to V$.
Define the actual pullback restriction and projected source adjoint by
\[
 S:\Dom S=V\cap\Dom A\longrightarrow E,\quad Sf=Af,
 \qquad B:\Dom B=\Dom A^*\longrightarrow V,\quad Bu=P_VA^*u.
\]
The norm and topology on $V$ are inherited from $H$. The graph
of $S$ is closed: its image under $(f,g)\mapsto(f,g)\in H\oplus E$
is the intersection of the graph of $A$ with $V\oplus E$.
Closedness alone says nothing about density of $\Dom S$ in $V$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:observablecore}
The source-adjoint domain $\Dom A^*$ is dense in $E$, and
$B^*=S$. If in addition $\Dom S$ is dense in $V$, then $B$ is
closable and
\[
 \overline B=S^*.
\]
Equivalently, for every $u\in\Dom S^*$ there are
$u_n\in\Dom A^*$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projectedcorelimit}
 u_n\longrightarrow u\text{ in }E,\qquad
 P_VA^*u_n\longrightarrow S^*u\text{ in }V.
\end{equation}
No convergence of the unprojected $A^*u_n$ is required or concluded.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We first supply the Hilbert graph facts used in the argument.
For any densely defined $T:H\to E$, direct evaluation of the
product inner product gives
\[
 \operatorname{graph}(T)^\perp
   =\{(-T^*y,y):y\in\Dom T^*\}.
\]
Indeed $(x,y)$ belongs to the left side exactly when
$\langle x,f\rangle+\langle y,Tf\rangle=0$ for all
$f\in\Dom T$, which is the adjoint-domain condition
$y\in\Dom T^*$ and $x=-T^*y$.
If $T$ is closable and $y\perp\Dom T^*$, this identity implies
$(0,y)\in\operatorname{graph}(T)^{\perp\perp}
=\overline{\operatorname{graph}(T)}$. The graph closure has no
nonzero vertical vector by closability, so $y=0$. Thus $\Dom T^*$
is dense. Conversely, if that domain is dense, the same graph
identity excludes every nonzero vertical vector from the graph
closure, making it the graph of a closed operator. Applying the
identity twice then shows
$\operatorname{graph}(T^{**})=\overline{\operatorname{graph}(T)}$:
a pair $(f,g)$ is in this closure exactly when
$\langle g,y\rangle=\langle f,T^*y\rangle$ for all
$y\in\Dom T^*$, the defining condition for $T^{**}f=g$.
This also proves $A^{**}=A$ and density of $\Dom A^*$, since
$A$ is closed and densely defined. Adjoint graphs themselves are
closed by passing their defining inner-product identities to limits.

Hence $B$ is densely defined. If $f\in\Dom B^*\subset V$ with
$B^*f=g\in E$, then for every $u\in\Dom A^*$,
\[
 \langle g,u\rangle_E=\langle f,P_VA^*u\rangle_V
                       =\langle f,A^*u\rangle_H.
\]
The last identity uses $f\in V$. It puts $f$ in $\Dom A^{**}$
with $A^{**}f=g$. Therefore $f\in V\cap\Dom A=\Dom S$ and
$Sf=g$. Conversely any $f\in\Dom S$ satisfies these pairings
by the source adjoint identity, so it belongs to $\Dom B^*$ and
$B^*f=Sf$. This proves $B^*=S$ as actual partial operators.

If $\Dom S$ is dense, so is $\Dom B^*$; the graph facts just
proved make $B$ closable and give
$\overline B=B^{**}=S^*$. Finally closure in the metric product
$E\oplus V$ is sequential. For each graph point $(u,S^*u)$,
choose a point of $\operatorname{graph}B$ at distance less than
$1/n$, $n\geq1$. Its two coordinates give
\eqref{eq:projectedcorelimit}. Conversely any such limit is in the
closed graph of $S^*$, so this is precisely its whole graph.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{Density is an independent hypothesis}
For a given $V$, density is equivalent to the elementary exact test
\[
 g\in V,\quad\langle g,f\rangle=0\ (f\in V\cap\Dom A)
       \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g=0.
\]
This is the orthogonal-annihilator characterization of closure of a
linear subspace. A sufficient condition is
$P_V(\Dom A)\subset\Dom A$: approximating any $v\in V$ by
$x_n\in\Dom A$ then gives $P_Vx_n\to v$ in the restricted domain.
It is an additional condition, not a consequence of source density.
If $v\notin\Dom A$, then
$\operatorname{span}\{v\}\cap\Dom A=\{0\}$, because any
nonzero scalar multiple in the domain could be divided by that
scalar. Thus a proper dense source domain can have a zero,+nondense restriction to a nonzero closed line.
\lean{ObservableDensityCriterion}{derivative_dense_iff_annihilator_zero}
\lean{ObservableCoreDensity}{derivative_dense_of_projection_invariant}
\lean{ObservableDensityObstruction}{restricted_line_domain_bot; restricted_line_not_dense}

Here are two explicit human Hilbert illustrations. On real $\ell^2$
indexed by $n\geq1$, take
\[
 (Ax)_n=nx_n,\qquad \Dom A=\{x:\sum_{n\geq1}n^2x_n^2<\infty\}.
\]
Finite sequences prove density. If $x^{(j)}\to x$ and
$Ax^{(j)}\to y$ in $\ell^2$, coordinate continuity gives
$y_n=nx_n$, proving closedness. Testing an adjoint representer
against each coordinate vector gives $A^*=A$ on exactly this
domain; conversely Cauchy--Schwarz proves the required pairings
for every member of it. The vector $v_n=1/n$ lies in $\ell^2$
and outside $\Dom A$, so its line realizes the nondense case above.

Instead let $V=\operatorname{span}\{e_1\}$. Now $S$ is the identity
inclusion of this line into $\ell^2$, and $S^*u=u_1e_1$ is
defined on all of $\ell^2$. The initially projected source adjoint
has the identical formula $Bu=u_1e_1$, but only on $\Dom A$.
This is a strictly smaller domain. Coordinate truncations converge
to every $u\in\ell^2$ and have projected outputs $u_1e_1$,
so its graph closure is exactly the stated whole-domain $S^*$.
These examples are direct human coordinate consequences; no
additional named formal example or new spectral result is claimed.

\subsection*{Application boundaries for the IBM operators}
The theorem applies separately to an original closed densely defined
source derivative and a closed observable subspace whose restricted
domain has been proved dense. In the notation of this paper, taking
the scenery source or clock source and the full IBM observable
subspace gives the corresponding one-channel projected-adjoint core,
using the separate intrinsic density results. This is a human
specialization of the generic theorem, and is not a statement that
conditioning preserves an arbitrary source derivative domain.

For the joint operator of Section 1 there is a further distinction.
The generic source adjoint of $f\mapsto(D_Xf,D_Yf)$ is tested against
the intersection of the two source domains. This definition does
not identify its entire domain with
$\Dom\delta_X\times\Dom\delta_Y$. Therefore the generic closure
theorem alone does not prove that the separately admissible pairs
in Lemma~\ref{lem:project} form a core for the full joint intrinsic
adjoint. Neither dropping its observable projection nor replacing
its fields by observed-past predictable fields is justified by
\eqref{eq:projectedcorelimit}.

These are classical adjoint and graph-closure mechanisms. Richard's
primary lecture notes give the adjoint definition and complete
closedness proof in Definition 2.1.8/Lemma 2.1.10~\cite{richard}.
The full graph-orthogonal-complement argument needed here is supplied
above; no new general adjoint principle is claimed.

\section{Multiplication on the full intrinsic adjoint graph dual}
The static product rules also identify a precise class of coefficients
that can multiply a generalized derivative \emph{after} a graph-dual
limit. This is stronger than forming each product before the limit,
but its bounded native-gradient hypothesis is an additional requirement.
Retain the original closed densely defined
$D:\Dom D\subset\Hh\to\mathcal V$ and $\delta=D^*$ from Section 2.

\subsection*{The actual full test graph}
Use the Hilbert test space
\[
 \mathcal T=\{(V,\delta V):V\in\Dom\delta\},\qquad
 \|(V,\delta V)\|_{\mathcal T}^2
            =\|V\|_{\mathcal V}^2+\|\delta V\|_{\Hh}^2.
\]
It is complete because $\delta$ is closed. The field projection
$(V,\delta V)\mapsto V$ is injective, and every member of
$\mathcal T$ is a test from the entire original adjoint domain.
Define continuous linear maps into its norm dual by
\begin{align}
 (dg)(V,\delta V)&=\langle g,\delta V\rangle_{\Hh},
                         &&g\in\Hh,\label{eq:fullweakd}\\
 (\iota U)(V,\delta V)&=\langle U,V\rangle_{\mathcal V},
                         &&U\in\mathcal V.\label{eq:fullembed}
\end{align}
Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\|dg\|_{\mathcal T^*}\leq\|g\|_2$
and $\|\iota U\|_{\mathcal T^*}\leq\|U\|_2$. Adjoint duality
shows $\iota Dg=dg$ for $g\in\Dom D$. In particular, if
$g_n\in\Dom D$ and $g_n\to g$ in $\Hh$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fullweakerror}
 \|\iota Dg_n-dg\|_{\mathcal T^*}\leq\|g_n-g\|_2.
\end{equation}
Closedness of $D$ and the double-adjoint theorem give the exact
representability boundary
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fullrepresent}
 dg=\iota U\text{ for some }U\in\mathcal V
                \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g\in\Dom D.
\end{equation}
Indeed equality tested on every $V\in\Dom\delta$ says precisely
that $g\in\Dom\delta^*$ with $\delta^*g=U$; and $\delta^*=D$.
Conversely the original graph gives the representation.
The embedding $\iota$ is injective because $\Dom\delta$ is
dense in $\mathcal V$. This density is a consequence of the
closed, densely defined original $D$, not a density assumption
on a smaller selected set of fields.

\lean{IBMIntrinsicGraphDual}{embed_injective; representable_iff_domain}

\subsection*{One bounded native multiplier}
Fix $f\in\Dom D$ and finite $A,B\geq0$ such that, in the
original joint coordinates,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fullmultclass}
 |f(\omega)|\leq A,\qquad
 \|Df(\omega)\|_{H_X\oplus H_Y}\leq B
 \quad\text{almost surely}.
\end{equation}
The formal hypotheses give an essential bound on each of the
two native coordinates; taking the square root of the sum of
their squared bounds gives the displayed combined $B$.
Both coordinates here use the original Fubini and source-swap
isometries into the same joint sample order. Without that
identification, a pointwise product in the scenery channel
could put $f$ in the wrong source order.

Section 2 already proves, for every original $V\in\Dom\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fullweighted}
 fV\in\Dom\delta,\qquad
 \delta(fV)=f\delta V-P\langle Df,V\rangle_{H_X\oplus H_Y}.
\end{equation}
The contraction is an actual $L^2$ scalar, since its pointwise
absolute value is at most $B\|V(\omega)\|$. The projection $P$
cannot be omitted: $Df$ and $V$ are ambient fields. The formula
is proved first using bounded native scalar tests and the
original strong product rule. Bounded graph approximation of
every native scalar test passes its two inner products to the
limit, yielding the equality on the entire adjoint domain.

Define the graph multiplier
\[
 M_f(V,\delta V)=(fV,\delta(fV)).
\]
It is well-defined by \eqref{eq:fullweighted}. It is linear,
since multiplication and the original adjoint are linear.
The formal source proves its boundedness by the closed graph
theorem on the actual complete test graph. There is also the
following direct HUMAN bound. Projection contractivity gives
\[
 \|\delta(fV)\|_2\leq A\|\delta V\|_2+B\|V\|_2,
 \qquad \|fV\|_2\leq A\|V\|_2.
\]
Minkowski's inequality in the two-component Euclidean norm
therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphmultbound}
 \|M_f(V,\delta V)\|_{\mathcal T}
 \leq A\sqrt{\|V\|_2^2+\|\delta V\|_2^2}+B\|V\|_2
 \leq(A+B)\|(V,\delta V)\|_{\mathcal T}.
\end{equation}
This explicit numerical estimate is a printed consequence of
the actual weighted formula, rather than an additional named
formal operator-norm theorem.

Precomposition now defines a bounded action on the \emph{whole}
dual:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dualaction}
 f\mathbin{\cdot}F:=F\circ M_f,\qquad F\in\mathcal T^*,
 \qquad \|f\mathbin{\cdot}F\|\leq\|M_f\|\|F\|.
\end{equation}
It agrees with original field multiplication. In fact,
\[
 (f\mathbin{\cdot}\iota U)(V,\delta V)
       =\langle U,fV\rangle=\langle fU,V\rangle,
 \qquad f\mathbin{\cdot}\iota U=\iota(fU).
\]
Thus it is a continuous extension of the genuine native product,
not a product assigned only to one approximation sequence.
The definition uses the entire graph; no choice of a projected
source-adjoint core is needed.

\begin{theorem}[Full-dual intrinsic Leibniz rule]\label{thm:fulldualproduct}
Under \eqref{eq:fullmultclass}, every $g\in\Hh$, including
unbounded $g$ outside $\Dom D$, satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fulldualproduct}
 d(fg)=f\mathbin{\cdot}dg+\iota(gDf)
                  \quad\text{in }\mathcal T^*.
\end{equation}
The correction is an actual ordinary field in $\mathcal V$,
with $\|gDf\|_2\leq B\|g\|_2$.
For every $g_n\in\Dom D$ converging to $g$ in $\Hh$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dualproductlimit}
 \iota(fDg_n)\longrightarrow f\mathbin{\cdot}dg
                      \quad\text{in }\mathcal T^*,
 \qquad
 \|\iota(fDg_n)-f\mathbin{\cdot}dg\|
       \leq\|M_f\|\|g_n-g\|_2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The product $fg$ belongs to $\Hh$ because it is observable
and square integrable. The pointwise product $gDf$ belongs
to the ordinary field space by the bound in the statement.
Its native channel realization is obtained by transporting
each actual joint product back through the original channel
isometry. For a full test $(V,\delta V)$, equations
\eqref{eq:fullweakd}, \eqref{eq:fullweighted} and
\eqref{eq:dualaction} yield
\begin{align*}
 d(fg)(V,\delta V)-(f\mathbin{\cdot}dg)(V,\delta V)
 &=\langle fg,\delta V\rangle
                        -\langle g,\delta(fV)\rangle\\
 &=\langle g,P\langle Df,V\rangle\rangle.
\end{align*}
Since $g$ is observable, this last pairing equals
$\E[g\langle Df,V\rangle]=\langle gDf,V\rangle$.
All products are integrable by $L^2$ Cauchy--Schwarz; no
boundedness of $g$ was used. This proves the full-dual
identity. Apply the continuous action \eqref{eq:dualaction}
to \eqref{eq:fullweakerror} and use its agreement with
field multiplication to obtain \eqref{eq:dualproductlimit}.
\end{proof}

For example $g=Z_t$ is an actual unbounded member of $\Hh$.
The theorem gives the precise static equality
\[
 d(fZ_t)=f\mathbin{\cdot}dZ_t+\iota(Z_tDf).
\]
On a test $(V,\delta V)$ its correction is the actual joint
integral $\E[Z_t\langle Df,V\rangle]$. Its absolute integral
is at most $B\|Z_t\|_2\|V\|_2$.
The ordinary correction field $Z_tDf$ depends continuously
on $t$ in $\mathcal V$, by the original strong $L^2$
continuity of $Z_t$ and the fixed bounded field $Df$.
These observations do not assign a finite-energy original
gradient to $Z_t$ or assert that $fZ_t$ is nonnative:
the separate native-domain question for that product is
exactly the representability criterion
\eqref{eq:fullrepresent}.

In particular this theorem does not license multiplying $dg$
by an arbitrary bounded random coefficient, or by
$\phi'(g)$ when $g$ is rough. Such a coefficient must itself
satisfy the original native domain and bounded-gradient
requirements used to construct $M_f$. Nor does a full
observable graph-dual product become a causal physical-time
IBM integral. This is a static extension of the classical
weak product mechanism. The previously compared Gaussian
divergence/chain rules in Nualart's notes remain its
antecedents; no new general product principle or priority
claim is made.

\section{Classical context and precise remaining obligations}
The Gaussian recovery $DN^{-1}$ on centered chaoses, Brownian predictable
representation and the projected-derivative mechanism are classical.
Ocone's primary institutional publication record \cite{ocone} describes
the extension of Clark's formula to weakly $H$-differentiable functionals.
This supplies relevant historical context. Our bounded search inspected
that record and abstract, not the complete article; it does not establish
priority for any specific IBM formulation or match all analytic hypotheses.
The paper claims neither a new Clark--Ocone theorem nor external novelty.
Its contribution as a formalization companion is the explicit alignment of
actual folded covariance, original cusp graph, completed Fourier mean,
predictable clock coefficient and projected intrinsic adjoint.

Three qualifications are mathematical requirements, rather than choices of
terminology. First, $\mathcal F_t^Y$ is the hidden clock's Brownian past.
The observed past $\sigma(Z_s:s\leq t)$ is a different sigma algebra.
Nothing above identifies $U$ as predictable for that observed past.
Second, $R_XF$ is a static Gaussian source field; it is not constructed as
a physical-time predictable IBM integrand. Third, even the full observable
projection $P$ used in the adjoint identity is not a causal projection.
Dropping any of these distinctions would change the theorem.

To obtain an observed-filtration physical-time reconstruction, one would
still need a specified observed predictable integral space, an original
closed integral realizing the desired physical prescription, admissibility
of the proposed coefficients in that space, and a proof that its centered
residual vanishes in the stated topology. The sine test shows why success
for conditional source means cannot discharge those obligations. Existing
Simpson identities and native-grid weak-limit obstructions retain their own
domains and topologies; no transfer to arbitrary partitions, nonsmooth
physical-time formulas or a predictable representation property is asserted.
