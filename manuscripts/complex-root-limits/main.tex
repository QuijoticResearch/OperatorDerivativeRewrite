\begin{abstract}
Uniform complex fourth-root steps yield genuine positive probability laws
whose holomorphic exponential transforms approach the biharmonic heat
symbol. Their absolute second moments diverge, and exact fourth moments
give a uniform positive mass outside each compact set, eventually along
every refinement. Thus the transform limit is compatible with the absence
of any weak probability-law limit. Poissonization preserves both features.
Normalized complex exponential tilts have mass one and convergent
holomorphic transforms, but their total variations diverge. We exhibit a
single bounded continuous test, independent of the approximation index,
whose integral equals the total variation exactly. The paper reconstructs
verified fourth-order results with elementary proofs and explicit
constants. The analytic-versus-probabilistic distinction is established
in prior work; this is a formalization companion, and no priority claim
is made.
The actual mark source separately has a positive Hermitian three-channel predictable calculus, full original-source $L^2$ reconstruction and a distinct Poisson clock channel. Complete proofs retain the difference between this source calculus and its analytic scaling target. A compact-frequency domain supports genuine integrable transitions, derivative limits and finite-process backward martingales. \end{abstract}

\section{Scope and main conclusions}
Let $\varepsilon_j,\delta_j$ be mutually independent symmetric signs and set
\[
 \zeta_j=\varepsilon_j+i\delta_j,\qquad
 S_{n,a}=a\sum_{j=1}^n\zeta_j,\qquad
 a_n(T)=(6T/n)^{1/4}\quad(n\geq1).
\]
The four values of $\zeta_j$ are the fourth roots of $-4$.
Write $\mu_n^T$ for the law of $S_{n,a_n(T)}$ on $\C$ with its usual
Euclidean topology. For $r\geq0$, let $N_r$ be Poisson with mean $r$,
independent of the signs, and let $\mu_{r,a}$ be the law of
\[
 C_{r,a}=a\sum_{j=1}^{N_r}\zeta_j.
\]
All these laws are ordinary positive probability measures.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:positive}
For $T>0$ and each real $\eta$,
\[
 \int e^{i\eta z}\,d\mu_n^T(z)\longrightarrow e^{-T\eta^4},\qquad
 \int e^{i\eta z}\,d\mu_{n,a_n(T)}(z)\longrightarrow e^{-T\eta^4}.
\]
Nevertheless, for every compact $K\subset\C$, eventually in $n$,
\[
 \mu_n^T(K^c)\geq\tfrac18,\qquad
 \mu_{n,a_n(T)}(K^c)\geq\tfrac1{12}.
\]
Neither family is tight. Neither has a weak probability-law limit along
any reindexing $n=\varphi(k)$ with $\varphi(k)\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}

The transforms in this statement test $e^{i\eta z}$, which is unbounded on
$\C$ when $\eta\ne0$. They are not the characteristic functions
$e^{i(u\operatorname{Re}z+v\operatorname{Im}z)}$ used in weak convergence
on $\R^2$. This domain distinction is essential.

For the tilted result use the second, rate-scaled normalization
$b_n=a_n(1)$, $r_n=nT$. Define real functions
\[
 F(x)=\cos x\cosh x,\qquad \psi_a(\xi)=F(a\xi)-1,
\]
and the finite complex measure
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tilt}
 d\nu_n(z)=e^{-r_n\psi_{b_n}(\xi)}e^{i\xi z}\,d\mu_{r_n,b_n}(z).
\end{equation}
Here and below total variation means the mass $|\nu|(\C)$, with no factor
$1/2$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:tilt}
Fix $T>0$ and $\xi\in\R\setminus\{0\}$. Each $\nu_n$ has mass one, and
for every real $\eta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tiltlimit}
 \int e^{i\eta z}\,d\nu_n(z)
 \longrightarrow e^{-T((\eta+\xi)^4-\xi^4)}.
\end{equation}
The fixed function $f_\xi(z)=e^{-i\xi\operatorname{Re}z}$ is continuous
and has modulus one, and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed}
 \int f_\xi\,d\nu_n=|\nu_n|(\C)
 =\exp\!\left(r_n\cosh(b_n\xi)(1-\cos(b_n\xi))\right)
 \longrightarrow+\infty.
\end{equation}
Consequently these complex measures do not converge against all bounded
continuous tests to any finite complex measure, even along a divergent
reindexing.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Relationship with prior work.}
Bonaccorsi and Mazzucchi~\cite{BM} construct higher-order analytic limits
from complex root walks. Their Theorem 2 treats exponential transforms;
their Remark 1 discusses escape from bounded regions, and their discussion
before Theorem 7 distinguishes the analytic limit from an ordinary limit
process. Our rotated fourth roots and normalization are a fourth-order
instance of that construction. We do not assert that their analytic
framework supplies an ordinary weak limit. The contribution of this
companion is a complete reconstruction of the frozen formal results:
finite moment identities, explicit escape bounds, Poissonized statements,
and a fixed bounded test for actual complex coordinate measures. These
are meaningful precise distinctions, but their independent novelty has
not been established. The later analytic It\^o framework~\cite{BCM} is
also separate from weak convergence of the present probability laws.

\section{Transforms and integrability}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:step}
For real $a,\eta$,
\begin{align}
 \E e^{i\eta a\zeta_1}&=F(a\eta),\label{eq:step}\\
 \E\left|e^{i\eta a\zeta_1}\right|&=\cosh(a\eta).
 \label{eq:stepnorm}
\end{align}
Consequently
\begin{align}
 \E e^{i\eta S_{n,a}}&=F(a\eta)^n,\label{eq:walktransform}\\
 \E e^{i\eta C_{r,a}}&=\exp(r(F(a\eta)-1)),\label{eq:compound}\\
 \E\left|e^{i\eta C_{r,a}}\right|&=\exp(r(\cosh(a\eta)-1)).
 \label{eq:compoundnorm}
\end{align}
All integrals in these formulas are absolutely integrable.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Independence of the two signs gives
$\E e^{ix(\varepsilon+i\delta)}=(\E e^{ix\varepsilon})
(\E e^{-x\delta})=\cos x\cosh x$. The modulus is $e^{-x\delta}$,
which gives \eqref{eq:stepnorm}. Independence over $j$ gives products
for the walk, including $\E|e^{i\eta S_{n,a}}|=\cosh(a\eta)^n$.
Conditioning on $N_r=m$ and summing the Poisson series gives
\[
 e^{-r}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{r^m}{m!}F(a\eta)^m
 =e^{r(F(a\eta)-1)}.
\]
Absolute convergence follows either from the same series with
$|F(a\eta)|$ or from the nonnegative modulus series
$e^{-r}\sum_m r^m\cosh(a\eta)^m/m!$.
This also proves \eqref{eq:compoundnorm} and justifies conditioning for
the complex integrals.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:limit}
For $T\geq0$ and real $\eta$,
\begin{align*}
 F(a_n(T)\eta)^n&\longrightarrow e^{-T\eta^4},\\
 e^{n(F(a_n(T)\eta)-1)}&\longrightarrow e^{-T\eta^4},\\
 e^{nT(F(b_n\eta)-1)}&\longrightarrow e^{-T\eta^4}.
\end{align*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication of the convergent cosine and hyperbolic cosine series
gives $F(x)=1-x^4/6+O(x^8)$ as $x\to0$; the degree-two and degree-six
terms cancel. Thus
\[
 n(F(a_n(T)\eta)-1)=-T\eta^4+O(n^{-1}).
\]
For the walk, $F(a_n(T)\eta)>0$ eventually, and
$\log(1+y)=y+O(y^2)$ shows that
$n\log F(a_n(T)\eta)\to-T\eta^4$. Exponentiation proves the first
two limits. Replacing $a_n(T)$ by $b_n$ gives
$n(F(b_n\eta)-1)\to-\eta^4$; multiplication by $T$ proves the third.
For $T=0$, the first two sequences are identically one, as is the third.
\end{proof}

These statements establish only the stated exponential-test limits.
Passing a general entire function through the limit needs a separate
integrability and domination argument. The compact-frequency domain below supplies a justified restricted extension.

\section{Exact positive moments and compact escape}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:moments}
For $n\geq0$, real $a$, and $r\geq0$,
\begin{align}
 \E|S_{n,a}|^2&=2na^2,&
 \E|S_{n,a}|^4&=(8n^2-4n)a^4,\label{eq:walkmoments}\\
 \E|C_{r,a}|^2&=2ra^2,&
 \E|C_{r,a}|^4&=(8r^2+4r)a^4.\label{eq:poissonmoments}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $R_n=\sum_{j=1}^n\varepsilon_j$ and
$I_n=\sum_{j=1}^n\delta_j$. These variables are independent, with
$\E R_n^2=\E I_n^2=n$. In the fourth power of $R_n$, only four equal
indices and two equal pairs survive sign averaging. Therefore
\[
 \E R_n^4=n+6\binom n2=3n^2-2n.
\]
Since $|S_{n,a}|^2=a^2(R_n^2+I_n^2)$, expansion gives
\[
 \E|S_{n,a}|^4=a^4\bigl(2(3n^2-2n)+2n^2\bigr),
\]
proving \eqref{eq:walkmoments}. The defining Poisson series gives
$\E N_r=r$ and $\E[N_r(N_r-1)]=r^2$ by shifting the summation index.
Hence $\E N_r^2=r^2+r$. Conditioning \eqref{eq:walkmoments} on
$N_r$ proves \eqref{eq:poissonmoments}. All these nonnegative
moments are finite by the same Poisson moment sums.
\end{proof}

The positive second moment in this lemma must be distinguished from
$\E S_{n,a}^2=0$. The latter is a holomorphic cancellation and is not
a variance in the complex Hilbert-space sense.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:tail}
If $X\geq0$, $0<m=\E X<\infty$, and $\E X^2\leq A m^2$, then
for $0\leq b\leq m/2$,
\[
 \Pp(X>b)\geq\frac1{4A}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Split the expectation at $b$ and apply Cauchy--Schwarz:
\[
 m\leq b+\E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>b\}}]
 \leq b+(\E X^2)^{1/2}\Pp(X>b)^{1/2}.
\]
Thus $m/2\leq \sqrt A m\Pp(X>b)^{1/2}$. Divide by $m$ and square.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:positive}]
The transforms follow from Lemma~\ref{lem:step} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:limit}. At $a=a_n(T)$, the common second moment
of the walk and the rate-$n$ compound law is
\[
 m_n=2\sqrt{6Tn}\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
For the walk the fourth moment is $(48n-24)T\leq2m_n^2$;
for the compound law it is $(48n+24)T\leq3m_n^2$, since $n\geq1$.
Given compact $K$, choose $R>0$ with $K\subset\{|z|\leq R\}$.
Eventually $m_n\geq2R^2$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:tail} to $X=|S|^2$
or $|C|^2$, with $b=R^2$ and $A=2$ or $3$ respectively.
The event $\{X>R^2\}$ lies in $K^c$, giving the two constants.

This contradicts tightness with any error smaller than $1/12$.
The same eventual inequalities hold after any $\varphi(k)\to\infty$.
To rule out a weak limit directly, let $\rho$ be a proposed probability
limit. The disks $\{|z|\leq R\}$ increase to $\C$, so choose $R$
with $\rho(\{|z|\leq R\})>1-1/48$. Let $\chi$ be a continuous
function equal to one on that disk, zero outside the disk of radius
$R+1$, and between zero and one. Then
$\int\chi\,d\rho>1-1/48$, while every sufficiently large approximating
law has $\int\chi\leq1-1/12$. This contradicts convergence against
the fixed bounded continuous $\chi$, and proves the assertion for both
families and every divergent reindexing.
\end{proof}

The constants are elementary uniform lower bounds, not claimed optimal.
In particular this proof does not claim that the escaping mass tends to
one; the verified conclusion is already enough to exclude weak limits.

\section{Path-space consequences with an explicit topology}
For $n\geq1$, join the successive partial sums
$a\sum_{j=1}^k\zeta_j$ linearly at times $k/n$ to obtain a continuous
path $\Gamma_{n,a}:[0,1]\to\C$. Give $C([0,1];\C)$ the uniform norm.
Its endpoint is $S_{n,a}$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:path}
For $T>0$, the laws of $\Gamma_{n,a_n(T)}$ are not tight in the uniform
path topology. More generally, any family of probability measures on a
topological path space admitting a continuous coordinate map with
coordinate laws $\mu_n^T$ or $\mu_{n,a_n(T)}$ is not tight.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
A continuous image of a compact set is compact. If a family of path laws
were tight, for every $\epsilon>0$ one could choose a compact path set
carrying mass at least $1-\epsilon$ under every member. Its coordinate
image would be a compact subset of $\C$ carrying at least that mass under
every coordinate law. This contradicts Theorem~\ref{thm:positive}.
Endpoint evaluation is continuous in the uniform norm, which proves the
first assertion.
\end{proof}

This implication specifies its topology. The formal Poisson trajectory
corollary uses a continuous coordinate on a countable product space.
It is not silently a statement about every Skorokhod topology: evaluation
at a jump time requires its own continuity hypotheses there. No path-law
limit is constructed from the holomorphic transform alone.

\section{Actual complex tilts and the fixed test}
We prove Theorem~\ref{thm:tilt} through formulas valid at every finite
parameter. Fix $a,\xi\in\R$, $r\geq0$, and put
\[
 h_{r,a,\xi}(z)=e^{-r\psi_a(\xi)}e^{i\xi z},\qquad
 d\nu_{r,a,\xi}=h_{r,a,\xi}\,d\mu_{r,a}.
\]
The normalizing factor is a positive real number because $\psi_a(\xi)$
is real, although $\psi_a(\xi)$ need not be nonnegative.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:finite}
The density is absolutely integrable, $\nu_{r,a,\xi}(\C)=1$, and
\begin{align}
 \int e^{i\eta z}\,d\nu_{r,a,\xi}(z)
 &=\exp\bigl(r(\psi_a(\eta+\xi)-\psi_a(\xi))\bigr),\label{eq:shift}\\
 |\nu_{r,a,\xi}|(\C)
 &=\exp\bigl(r\cosh(a\xi)(1-\cos(a\xi))\bigr).
 \label{eq:variation}
\end{align}
The exponential test in \eqref{eq:shift} is integrable for the total
variation measure. Furthermore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phasecancel}
 f_\xi(z)h_{r,a,\xi}(z)=|h_{r,a,\xi}(z)|\qquad(z\in\C),
\end{equation}
so $\int f_\xi\,d\nu_{r,a,\xi}=|\nu_{r,a,\xi}|(\C)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Formula \eqref{eq:compoundnorm} gives density integrability. Multiplying
the density by $e^{i\eta z}$ leaves the same positive normalizer times
$e^{i(\eta+\xi)z}$, whose modulus is integrable by that formula again.
The compound transform \eqref{eq:compound} now proves
\eqref{eq:shift}; taking $\eta=0$ proves mass one.

For completeness, the variation of a finite complex density $h$ relative
to a positive measure $\mu$ is $|h|\mu$. The upper bound follows from
$|\int_A h\,d\mu|\leq\int_A|h|\,d\mu$ on every measurable partition.
For the reverse bound, approximate the measurable phase $h/|h|$ on
$\{h\ne0\}$ uniformly by finitely many phases on a partition of the
unit circle into arcs of arbitrarily small diameter. On each resulting
measurable piece the integral differs from its constant-phase integral
by at most that diameter times $\int|h|$. Summing and letting the diameter
vanish proves equality for every measurable set. In particular,
\begin{align*}
 |\nu_{r,a,\xi}|(\C)
 &=e^{-r(F(a\xi)-1)}\E|e^{i\xi C_{r,a}}|\\
 &=e^{-r(F(a\xi)-1)+r(\cosh(a\xi)-1)}\\
 &=e^{r\cosh(a\xi)(1-\cos(a\xi))}.
\end{align*}
Finally, writing $z=x+iy$, one has
$e^{-i\xi x}e^{i\xi z}=e^{-\xi y}=|e^{i\xi z}|$.
The normalizer is positive real, so the same identity holds for the
density. This is \eqref{eq:phasecancel}. Integrating it proves the last
claim. The test depends on $\xi$ alone, not on $a$ or $r$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:tilt}]
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} with $a=b_n$, $r=nT$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:limit} gives
$nT\psi_{b_n}(u)\to-Tu^4$ for every real $u$. Subtraction at
$u=\eta+\xi$ and $u=\xi$ followed by exponentiation proves
\eqref{eq:tiltlimit}.

To prove divergence using a quantitative lower bound, observe that
$|b_n\xi|\to0$. For $|x|\leq\pi$, concavity of sine on $[0,\pi/2]$
gives $\sin(|x|/2)\geq |x|/\pi$. Consequently
\[
 1-\cos x=2\sin^2(x/2)\geq2x^2/\pi^2.
\]
Since $\cosh x\geq1$, eventually
\begin{equation}\label{eq:growth}
 nT\cosh(b_n\xi)(1-\cos(b_n\xi))
 \geq\frac{2T\xi^2}{\pi^2}\,n b_n^2
 =\frac{2T\xi^2\sqrt6}{\pi^2}\sqrt n\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{equation}
The coefficient is positive by $T>0$ and $\xi\ne0$.
Equation \eqref{eq:variation} and the phase cancellation give
\eqref{eq:fixed}. A finite complex measure has a finite integral against
the modulus-one function $f_\xi$. Divergence of this one integral excludes
convergence against all bounded continuous tests. The same lower bound
holds after every divergent reindexing.
\end{proof}

The exclusions $T>0$, $\xi\ne0$ are indispensable. If $T=0$ the law is
$\delta_0$. If $\xi=0$ the density is one and the variation is one,
although positive-law compact escape can still occur at positive $T$.
Nor is the fixed-test conclusion merely a restatement that some sequence
of test functions detects growing variation: a single explicitly given
test works for the entire sequence.


\section{Cyclic mark channels and positive Hermitian energy}
The probability sources behind the preceding approximations admit an
exact calculus before any scaling limit. Its positive Hermitian energy
must be distinguished from its vanishing holomorphic moments and from
the signed higher-heat target. We first record the finite mechanism.

Let $K$ be uniform on $\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z$, $m\ge1$, and put
$\chi_j(k)=e^{2\pi i jk/m}$. The elementary character sum is
\[
 \frac1m\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\chi_j(k)=\mathbf1_{j=0}.
\]
For $j\ne0$, multiply the geometric sum by $1-e^{2\pi i j/m}$ to
prove it is zero; at $j=0$ it is one after normalization. Consequently
$\E[\chi_j(K)\overline{\chi_l(K)}]=\mathbf1_{j=l}$.
For any function $g$ on the finite mark space, define
\[
 \widehat g_j=\frac1m\sum_k g(k)\overline{\chi_j(k)}.
\]
Orthogonality and the dimension $m$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclic}
 g(k)-\E g(K)=\sum_{j\ne0}\widehat g_j\chi_j(k),\qquad
 \E|g(K)-\E g(K)|^2=\sum_{j\ne0}|\widehat g_j|^2.
\end{equation}
Here $j$ ranges over the residue classes; the case $m=1$ has empty
centered sum. Fourier sign conventions are fixed by the displayed
conjugate; the source uses its matching discrete Fourier transform.
If $\zeta=c\chi_1(K)$, then
\[
 \E\zeta^r=\mathbf1_{m\mid r}c^r,\qquad
 \E|\zeta|^2=|c|^2.
\]
The first identity is the character sum applied to $r$; the second
uses the pointwise modulus. Thus holomorphic cancellation is not a
zero variance in the Hilbert sense.

For the four marks used in this paper, $\xi=a(1+i)i^K$ in their cyclic
order. Assume $a\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$, and set
\[
 w_r=(2a^2)^r\quad(1\le r\le3),\qquad q=-4a^4.
\]
Then $\xi^4=q$ pointwise, $\E\xi^r=0$ for $1\le r\le3$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rootgram}
 \E[\xi^r\overline{\xi^s}]=\mathbf1_{r=s}w_r.
\end{equation}
Indeed $\xi^r=a^r(1+i)^r\chi_r(K)$ and its squared modulus is $w_r$.
These are all three nonconstant characters with nonzero normalizations.
For every $g$ on the four marks, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rootcoefficient}
 c_r(g)=\frac{\E[g\overline{\xi^r}]}{w_r}
       =\frac{\E[g\xi^{4-r}]}q.
\end{equation}
The equality follows pointwise from
$q\overline{\xi^r}=w_r\xi^{4-r}$, using the constant modulus and
$\xi^4=q$. Thus \eqref{eq:cyclic} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rootrepresent}
 g-\E g=\sum_{r=1}^3c_r(g)\xi^r,\qquad
 \E\left|\sum_{r=1}^3u_r\xi^r\right|^2
   =\sum_{r=1}^3w_r|u_r|^2.
\end{equation}
Pairing with each conjugate power proves uniqueness. A single first
channel cannot represent the centered square $\xi^2$: if
$\xi^2=b\xi$, multiply by $\xi^2$ and integrate to obtain
$q=b\E\xi^3=0$, a contradiction. This obstruction is on a genuine
four-point probability space.

\section{Predictable sums and finite reconstruction on the mark source}
Work on the actual infinite product $\{0,1,2,3\}^{\mathbb N}$ with
uniform independent coordinates $K_n$, and write $\xi_n=a(1+i)i^{K_n}$.
Let $\mathcal H_n=\sigma(K_0,\ldots,K_{n-1})$. A predictable coefficient
$H_{r,n}$ is a complex function of this prefix. Every such function
has all finite moments at each fixed $n$, because the prefix space is
finite; no uniform bound in $n$ is assumed. Define
\[
 S_N(H)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{r=1}^3H_{r,n}\xi_n^r,
 \qquad e_n(H)=\sum_{r=1}^3w_r|H_{r,n}|^2.
\]
Fresh-mark independence and \eqref{eq:rootgram} give the actual
conditional identities
\begin{align}
 \E[\Delta S_n\mid\mathcal H_n]&=0,\label{eq:condcenter}\\
 \E[|C+\Delta S_n|^2\mid\mathcal H_n]
 &=|C|^2+e_n(H)\label{eq:condenergy}
\end{align}
for every complex prefix-measurable $C$. In the second identity, expand
the square into its Hermitian cross terms; the linear terms vanish and
the quadratic terms use \eqref{eq:rootgram}. In particular $S_N$ is a
martingale and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rootisometry}
 \|S_N(H)\|_2^2=\sum_{n<N}\E e_n(H).
\end{equation}
Iteration of \eqref{eq:condenergy} with $C=S_n$ proves this formula;
it is not an isometry for the unconjugated square $S_N^2$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:finiteclark}
For every function $F$ of the first $N$ marks, there are predictable
coefficients with
\[
 F-\E F=S_N(H).
\]
They are unique almost surely for every channel and time $n<N$.
More generally, every integrable martingale in this prefix filtration
has a coherent three-channel representation of all its increments.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For the terminal value, set $M_n=\E[F\mid\mathcal H_n]$, $n\le N$.
Write $M_{n+1}=f_{n+1}(K_0,\ldots,K_n)$ and, for a fixed prefix $p$,
apply \eqref{eq:rootrepresent} to $g_p(k)=f_{n+1}(p,k)$.
The mean of $g_p$ is $M_n(p)$ by conditioning on the fresh uniform mark.
Thus $M_{n+1}-M_n=\sum_r c_r(g_p)\xi_n^r$. These coefficients depend
only on the prefix. Summing telescopes to $M_N-M_0=F-\E F$.
For a general martingale use its prefix functions at each successive
time in the same construction. This gives one array for all finite
horizons, rather than a separately chosen array at each horizon.

If two arrays have the same terminal sum, subtract them and apply
\eqref{eq:rootisometry}. Its nonnegative summands have sum zero.
For every $n<N$, each positive $w_r$ therefore multiplies a coefficient
of zero $L^2$ norm. This is the stated almost sure uniqueness. It makes
no assertion about coefficients beyond the specified terminal horizon.
\end{proof}

The same conditional argument with the $m-1$ nonzero characters gives
finite-horizon cyclic predictable integration and its unit-weight
isometry for any $m\ge1$. The source formally supplies this general
finite-energy calculation; the completed operator constructed next is
mapped specifically to the actual four-mark source.

\section{Infinite limits and the exact completed integration operator}
A coefficient array has finite energy if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finiteenergy}
 \mathcal E(H)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\E e_n(H)<\infty.
\end{equation}
Cross-time martingale orthogonality gives, for $M\ge N$,
\[
 \|S_M(H)-S_N(H)\|_2^2=\sum_{n=N}^{M-1}\E e_n(H).
\]
Consequently the sums are Cauchy in the actual complex $L^2$ space.
Let $JH$ denote their limit. Passing to norms, means and bounded
conditional projections gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infiniteenergy}
 \|JH\|_2^2=\mathcal E(H),\quad \E JH=0,\quad
 \E[JH\mid\mathcal H_N]=S_N(H).
\end{equation}
Linearity follows by taking limits of the finite sums. Conversely,
$L^2$ convergence of those sums bounds their norms; the nonnegative
energy partial sums in \eqref{eq:rootisometry} are then bounded, so
\eqref{eq:finiteenergy} holds. This supplies both directions of the
finite-energy convergence criterion.

We give the needed prefix-density argument explicitly. The collection
of sets approximable in probability by finite-coordinate cylinder sets
is a sigma algebra: it is closed under complements; finite unions
follow by the union bound; for a countable union first truncate the
union using continuity of a finite measure and then approximate the
finite union. It contains all cylinders and hence the product sigma
algebra. Indicators of finite-measure sets can therefore be approximated
in $L^p$, for every finite $p$, by cylinder indicators. Truncation and
simple-function approximation extend this density to $L^1$ and $L^2$
functions, including measurable representatives modulo null sets.
Conditional expectation is a contraction in either norm. If $G$ is a
cylinder function measurable at time $m$, then
$\E[G\mid\mathcal H_N]=G$ for $N\ge m$. Thus
\[
 \|\E[F\mid\mathcal H_N]-F\|_p\le2\|F-G\|_p\longrightarrow0
 \quad(p=1\text{ or }2)
\]
by first choosing a good cylinder approximation and then taking $N\ge m$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:infiniteclark}
Every centered $F\in L^2$ on the actual infinite four-mark product
has a coherent predictable array $H$ with
\[
 S_N(H)=\E[F\mid\mathcal H_N],\qquad
 S_N(H)\longrightarrow F\text{ in }L^2,\qquad
 \mathcal E(H)=\|F\|_2^2.
\]
The integration range is exactly the closed centered $L^2$ subspace.
For general $F\in L^1$, the coherent representation of
$\E[F\mid\mathcal H_N]-\E F$ converges in $L^1$ and almost surely,
without an assertion of finite square energy.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply the martingale construction of Theorem~\ref{thm:finiteclark} to
$M_N=\E[F\mid\mathcal H_N]$ for every $N$ with its single coherent
array. Prefix density proves the claimed norm convergence. In the
$L^2$ case, \eqref{eq:rootisometry} and norm continuity identify the
sum of energies with $\|F\|_2^2$. Conversely every finite-energy integral
is centered by \eqref{eq:infiniteenergy}. Centered functions form the
kernel of the continuous mean functional, hence a closed subspace.

Here is an elementary justification of the almost sure conclusion in
the $L^1$ case. For a complex integrable $V$, conditional Jensen makes
$|\E[V\mid\mathcal H_n]|$ a nonnegative submartingale, and the finite
maximal inequality gives
\[
 P\{\sup_n|\E[V\mid\mathcal H_n]|>\lambda\}
 \le\lambda^{-1}\E|V|.
\]
For a finite maximum, stop at the first crossing and use conditional
Jensen against the final conditional value to bound $\lambda$ times
the crossing probability by its final mean. Increasing the finite
horizon gives the displayed inequality. Choose cylinders $G_j$ with
$\E|F-G_j|\le2^{-3j}$, and choose $m_j$ so $G_j$ is measurable at
$\mathcal H_{m_j}$. For $n\ge m_j$, the difference
$\E[F\mid\mathcal H_n]-F$ is bounded in modulus by
$|\E[F-G_j\mid\mathcal H_n]|+|F-G_j|$.
The maximal inequality and Markov's inequality bound the probability
that its supremum exceeds $2^{1-j}$ by $2^{1-2j}$. These probabilities
are summable. The union bound on their tails, or the first
Borel--Cantelli lemma, proves that eventually each supremum is small.
This proves almost sure convergence. Subtracting the constant mean
and using the coherent identity transfers it to the stochastic sums.
\end{proof}

For precision, the formal Hilbert integrand space is the vector space
of finite-energy coefficient arrays modulo the kernel of $J$, with
norm induced by its realization in the actual integration range.
Equation~\eqref{eq:infiniteenergy} identifies this norm with the printed
energy. The range theorem identifies it isometrically with centered
$L^2$, so this quotient is complete; no completion is postulated to
make surjectivity true. Write $\mathcal I$ for that Hilbert space and
still write $J:\mathcal I\to L^2$ for its isometric inclusion onto the
centered range. Its bounded Hilbert adjoint $D=J^*$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rootadjoint}
 DJ=I_{\mathcal I},\qquad JD F=F-\E F.
\end{equation}
Indeed $\langle Ju,Jv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle$ gives $J^*J=I$;
$JJ^*$ is the orthogonal projection onto the closed range, which is
exactly centering. Thus $D$ is a bounded predictable reconstruction
operator. The notation does not identify it with an unbounded
anticipating Malliavin gradient on a different integrand space.

\section{Poissonizing the source does not remove the clock channel}
Now use an independent sequence of unit exponential holding times
$E_1,E_2,\ldots$, arrival times $\tau_n=E_1+\cdots+E_n$, and
$N_t=\max\{n:\tau_n\le t\}$. Positivity holds simultaneously almost
surely. Moreover the events $E_j\ge1$ are independent with probability
$e^{-1}$. For each fixed index $k$, the probability that none occur
after $k$ is the limit of $(1-e^{-1})^l$, namely zero. A countable
union shows infinitely many such events occur, so $\tau_n\to\infty$.
Thus $N_t$ is finite at every finite $t$ on one common event, and the
marked path
\[
 X_t=\sum_{j=0}^{N_t-1}\xi_j
\]
is an actual right-continuous finite-jump path, with its pre-jump
positions and jumps given by the original partial sums and marks.

The exponential renewal calculation gives
$P(N_t=n)=e^{-t}t^n/n!$. To see this directly, convolution gives the
arrival density $e^{-u}u^{n-1}/(n-1)!$ for $n\ge1$; integrating it on
$0<u<t$ against the survival $e^{-(t-u)}$ of the next holding time
gives the formula. For $n=0$ it is just exponential survival.
Memorylessness $P(E>s+t\mid E>s)=e^{-t}$, together with independence
of unused holdings and marks, supplies stationary independent marked
future increments at deterministic wall-clock times. Here is the source-level conditioning argument. On $\{N_s=n\}$,
condition first on completed holdings $E_1,\ldots,E_n$ with
$\tau_n\le s$ and the first $n$ marks. The only condition on the next
holding is $E_{n+1}>u=s-\tau_n$. In its density integral, the substitution
$E_{n+1}=u+r$ gives $e^{-u-r}dr=e^{-u}e^{-r}dr$.
The factor $e^{-u}$ is precisely the survival weight for this prefix;
the residual $r$ therefore has the unit exponential law independently
of the prefix. Unused holdings and marks retain their independent
product laws. Testing against bounded functions of the prefix and
fresh source, and summing over $n$, proves factorization on this
partition. Every coordinate of the observed past is a measurable
function of the count and completed prefixes. A monotone-class
extension from their cylinder tests therefore proves independence
from the entire observed past. Reconstructing the future from the
residual holding and unused source gives the claimed increment laws.
In particular $N_t-t$ is a genuine count-filtration martingale,
since a future count increment has independent Poisson mean $t-s$.
This is a separate holding/count source from the mark-only product.

For bounded continuous $f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$, let
\[
 Pf(z)=\E f(z+\xi),\qquad Af=Pf-f,\qquad
 C_rf(z)=c_r(k\mapsto f(z+\xi(k))).
\]
These are bounded operators in the uniform norm:
$\|P\|\le1$, $\|A\|\le2$ and
$\|C_r\|\le w_r^{-1/2}$. The last bound follows from
\eqref{eq:rootcoefficient} and the constant modulus of $\xi$.
Continuity follows from finite sums of translates. Formula
\eqref{eq:rootrepresent} gives the exact jump identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jumpchannels}
 f(z+\xi)-f(z)=Af(z)+\sum_{r=1}^3C_rf(z)\xi^r.
\end{equation}
It holds for every mark, without a Taylor remainder or analyticity
hypothesis on $f$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:clockdecomp}
With $X_{\tau_{j+1}-}$ denoting the actual pre-jump value, define
\begin{align*}
 K_t(f)&=\sum_{j<N_t}Af(X_{\tau_{j+1}-})
              -\int_0^t Af(X_s)\,ds,\\
 L_{r,t}(f)&=\sum_{j<N_t}C_rf(X_{\tau_{j+1}-})\xi_j^r.
\end{align*}
Then the exact actual path formula is
\[
 f(X_t)-f(0)-\int_0^tAf(X_s)\,ds
 =K_t(f)+\sum_{r=1}^3L_{r,t}(f).
\]
The combined process is a martingale in the actual observed natural
filtration of $X$. The centered mark span alone cannot represent the
unit jump of the count process for every mark.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Summing \eqref{eq:jumpchannels} at the finitely many jumps telescopes
the left observable. Nonexplosion and the exact partial-sum path
identify each pre-jump value, and subtracting the displayed time
integral gives the identity.

For the martingale assertion, the actual transition semigroup is
\[
 T_t=e^{-t}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{t^n}{n!}P^n=e^{tA}.
\]
The series converges in bounded-operator norm; differentiating on
bounded time intervals gives
$T_u f-f=\int_0^u T_v Af\,dv$. The fresh-future construction above
therefore gives, conditionally on the observed past at $s$,
\[
 \E[f(X_t)-f(X_s)\mid\mathcal F_s]
 =(T_{t-s}f-f)(X_s)
 =\int_0^{t-s}T_vAf(X_s)\,dv.
\]
Boundedness of $Af$ permits conditional Fubini; the same expression
is the conditional mean of $\int_s^t Af(X_r)dr$. Their difference
has zero conditional mean. All observable and drift terms are
integrable, the latter bounded by $2t\|f\|_\infty$, and the actual
finite-jump paths make the drift adapted. This proves the martingale
claim and agrees with the mapped native Dynkin theorem.

Finally every sum $\sum_{r=1}^3u_r\xi^r$ has mark mean zero, whereas
the constant one has mean one. No choice of pre-jump coefficients
can equate them for every fresh mark. The count increases by exactly
one at each arrival, and its continuous compensator has no jumps,
so its compensated jump has this same missing constant component.
\end{proof}

The last obstruction is the explicit source statement about centered
mark channels. It is not a proof that no representation exists after
adding a clock channel, nor does the displayed decomposition establish
a full wall-clock predictable representation theorem for arbitrary
terminal functions of holdings and marks. We assert the combined
martingale just proved; separate operator-domain and isometry claims
for its four continuous-time components are not imported from the
mark-index Hilbert space. Likewise no isometry is transferred to the
signed heat target or to a nonexistent ordinary weak limit.

\section{A genuine compact-frequency analytic domain}
The failure of weak convergence does not prevent convergence on a proved
class of unbounded complex tests. Fix $R\geq0$ and
$\varphi\in L^1([-R,R];\C)$, and put
\[
 f_\varphi(z)=\int_{-R}^R\varphi(\eta)e^{i\eta z}\,d\eta.
\]
Lebesgue measure is used here, with no $2\pi$ normalization. At $R=0$
this domain contains only the zero realization. For all $z\in\C$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bandbound}
 |f_\varphi(z)|\leq\|\varphi\|_1 e^{R|z|},\qquad
 f_\varphi^{(k)}(z)=\int_{-R}^R(i\eta)^k\varphi(\eta)e^{i\eta z}\,d\eta.
\end{equation}
Indeed $|e^{i\eta z}|=e^{-\eta\operatorname{Im}z}\leq e^{R|z|}$;
on $|z-z_0|\leq1$ each differentiated integrand is dominated by
$R^k|\varphi(\eta)|e^{R(|z_0|+1)}$. Dominated differentiation proves
the formula inductively and proves that $f_\varphi$ is entire. Its
real restriction is bounded, whereas its complex extension can grow.

Let $C_a(t)$ denote the actual unit-rate marked Poisson process of the
preceding sections. The finite-rate compound law of $C_a(r)$ is
$\mu_{r,a}$. Conditional on $N_r=j$, its norm is at most $2|a|j$, hence
for every $B\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radialband}
 \E e^{B|C_a(r)|}\leq
 \exp\{r(e^{2B|a|}-1)\}<\infty.
\end{equation}
Thus $f_\varphi$, every derivative, and every shifted function
$f_\varphi(z+\cdot)$ are genuinely integrable against each finite law.
More precisely, the absolute double integral of the defining frequency
integrand is at most
\[
 \|\varphi\|_1 e^{R|z|}\E e^{R|C_a(r)|}<\infty.
\]
This is the Fubini justification; it is not inferred from cancellation
in a holomorphic transform. With $\psi_a(\eta)=F(a\eta)-1$, it gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bandexpect}
 \E f_\varphi(z+C_a(r))
 =\int_{-R}^R\varphi(\eta)e^{i\eta z}e^{r\psi_a(\eta)}\,d\eta.
\end{equation}
For a real tilt $\kappa$, the density defining the finite complex law
has the modulus bound
\[
 e^{-r\psi_a(\kappa)}e^{|\kappa||w|}.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:radialband} with $B=R+|\kappa|$ therefore also
proves variation-integrability before Fubini, and yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bandtilt}
 \int f_\varphi(w)\,d\nu_{r,a,\kappa}(w)
 =\int_{-R}^R\varphi(\eta)
 e^{r(\psi_a(\eta+\kappa)-\psi_a(\kappa))}\,d\eta.
\end{equation}
Here $d\nu_{r,a,\kappa}=e^{-r\psi_a(\kappa)}e^{i\kappa w}d\mu_{r,a}$;
the notation includes exactly the rate-scaled tilts used earlier.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:bandlimit}
For $T\geq0$, $z\in\C$ and every fixed integer $k\geq0$,
\begin{align}
 \E f_\varphi^{(k)}(z+C_{b_n}(nT))&\longrightarrow
 \int_{-R}^R(i\eta)^k\varphi(\eta)e^{i\eta z-T\eta^4}\,d\eta,
 \label{eq:bandlimit}\\
 \int f_\varphi(w)\,d\nu_{nT,b_n,\kappa}(w)&\longrightarrow
 \int_{-R}^R\varphi(\eta)e^{-T((\eta+\kappa)^4-\kappa^4)}\,d\eta.
 \label{eq:bandtiltlimit}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The expansion $F(x)=1-x^4/6+O(x^8)$ implies $F(x)\leq1$
on a sufficiently small real neighborhood of zero. Since $b_n\to0$,
for every fixed frequency radius $S$ and all sufficiently large $n$,
\[
 0<e^{nT\psi_{b_n}(\eta)}\leq1\quad(|\eta|\leq S).
\]
The pointwise limit is $e^{-T\eta^4}$. In
\eqref{eq:bandexpect}, with $(i\eta)^k\varphi$ in place of $\varphi$,
an eventual integrable majorant is
$R^k|\varphi(\eta)|e^{R|z|}$. Dominated convergence proves the first
limit, including $k=0$ with its evident bound.
For the tilt write its multiplier as
\[
 \frac{e^{nT\psi_{b_n}(\eta+\kappa)}}
 {e^{nT\psi_{b_n}(\kappa)}}.
\]
The numerator has modulus at most one uniformly for $|\eta|\leq R$
eventually, by choosing $S=R+|\kappa|$. The denominator converges to
$e^{-T\kappa^4}>0$, so its reciprocal is bounded independently of $n$
eventually. This supplies $C|\varphi|$ as an integrable frequency
majorant. The pointwise ratio limit proves \eqref{eq:bandtiltlimit}.
No uniform bound on the variation of $\nu_{nT,b_n,\kappa}$ has been used;
that variation diverges when $T>0$ and $\kappa\ne0$.
\end{proof}

The limit is a classical solution on this fixed spectral domain. Define
\[
 u(t,z)=\int_{-R}^R\varphi(\eta)e^{i\eta z-t\eta^4}\,d\eta
 \quad(t\in\R,z\in\C).
\]
On bounded time and complex-coordinate sets, all time and space
derivatives have integrable bounds by constants times $|\varphi|$.
Consequently $u(0,z)=f_\varphi(z)$ and
\[
 \partial_tu(t,z)=-\partial_z^4u(t,z).
\]
Negative times are allowed here because the frequencies are bounded;
this statement is not backward solvability on arbitrary initial data.
In the library's $2\pi$ Fourier convention, the multiplier
$e^{-T\eta^4}$ is the higher-heat multiplier at frequency $\eta/(2\pi)$.

\subsection*{An entire real-Schwartz test outside the finite-clock domain}
Entire extension and rapid decay on the real line are insufficient for
the preceding genuine process integrals. Take
\[
 g(z)=e^{-z^2}.
\]
Its real restriction is Schwartz: every derivative is a polynomial
times $e^{-x^2}$, and every polynomial multiple is bounded and decays
at infinity. Yet, for every $r>0$ and $a\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gaussiannonintegrable}
 \int_{\C}|g(w)|\,d\mu_{r,a}(w)=+\infty.
\end{equation}
Thus even a finite compound-root process time has no ordinary complex
expectation of this test.

Here is the complete rare-path proof. Conditional on exactly $2m$
marks, prescribe the first $m$ roots to be $1+i$ and the next $m$
to be $-1+i$. This particular word has probability $4^{-2m}$ and
endpoint $2mai$. At that endpoint
\[
 |g(2mai)|=e^{4a^2m^2}.
\]
The nonnegative integral over the disjoint count events therefore
dominates the sum, over $m\geq0$, of
\[
 u_m=e^{-r}\frac{(r/4)^{2m}}{(2m)!}e^{4a^2m^2}>0.
\]
For fixed nonzero $a$ and positive $r$ its consecutive ratio is
\[
 \frac{u_{m+1}}{u_m}
 =\frac{(r/4)^2 e^{4a^2(2m+1)}}{(2m+2)(2m+1)}\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
For an elementary justification of the last divergence, use
$e^x\geq x^3/6$ for $x\geq0$: the resulting lower bound grows linearly
in $m$. In particular $u_{m+1}\geq2u_m$ eventually, so the terms do
not tend to zero and their sum diverges. Tonelli for the nonnegative
count mixture proves \eqref{eq:gaussiannonintegrable}. The original
process has exactly this law, so its test is nonintegrable as well.
The exclusions are sharp for the degenerate cases $r=0$ or $a=0$,
where the process value is identically zero and $g$ equals one there.

For a deterministic finite walk every test value is integrable, since
its sample space is finite. Even there, absolute control under the heat
scaling can fail spectacularly. At $n=2m$ and amplitude $a_{2m}(T)$,
the same word gives, for fixed $T>0$,
\[
 \E|g(S_{2m,a_{2m}(T)})|
 \geq\exp\{4\sqrt{3T}\,m^{3/2}-2m\log4\}
 \longrightarrow+\infty.
\]
This scaled specialization is an elementary human deduction from the
mapped finite-word lower bound, not an additional named formal limit.
It concerns absolute expectation; it does not by itself determine a
limit or divergence of the complex expectations with cancellation.

In particular, for every $n\geq1$ and $T>0$, the Poissonized test
$g(C_{b_n}(nT))$ is already nonintegrable. No exchange of a full
Gaussian frequency integral with these laws is justified by the earlier
compact-frequency theorem. This is a finite-law integrability boundary,
stronger than merely lacking a uniform bound in the approximation
index. The spectral and entire-function hypotheses of a claimed
representation must be checked before using its expectation notation.
The restricted Fourier-data framework of~\cite{BM} is not refuted by
this example.

\section{Actual conditional transitions and backward martingales}
Fix the finite process parameter $a\in\R$ and write
$\mathcal H_s^a=\sigma(C_a(v):0\leq v\leq s)$ for its natural past.
The renewal construction above supplies an increment independent of
this entire past, with law $\mu_{t-s,a}$, for $0\leq s\leq t$.
Define the frequency propagator
\[
 (P_r^a\varphi)(\eta)=\varphi(\eta)e^{r\psi_a(\eta)},\quad r\geq0.
\]
For fixed $a,r$, its multiplier is bounded on $[-R,R]$, so it preserves
$L^1([-R,R])$. Direct multiplication gives $P_0^a=I$ and
$P_r^aP_q^a=P_{r+q}^a$. It need not be a contraction on that domain
at arbitrary finite $a$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:bandconditional}
For $0\leq s\leq t$,
\[
 \E[f_\varphi(C_a(t))\mid\mathcal H_s^a]
 =f_{P_{t-s}^a\varphi}(C_a(s))\quad\hbox{almost surely}.
\]
For every fixed horizon $H\geq0$, the process
\[
 M_t=f_{P_{(H-t)_+}^a\varphi}(C_a(\min(t,H)))\quad(t\geq0)
\]
is an integrable complex martingale in this same natural filtration.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The function $(p,w)\mapsto f_\varphi(p(s)+w)$ is measurable on the
product of the coordinate past space and $\C$. Its value at the
actual past and increment is $f_\varphi(C_a(t))$, which is integrable
by \eqref{eq:bandbound}--\eqref{eq:radialband}. Independence therefore
gives its conditional expectation by integrating only the increment.
One may prove this conditional-integration rule first on product
indicators, then on simple functions and finally on integrable functions
by truncation and $L^1$ approximation. Formula~\eqref{eq:bandexpect}
identifies this increment integral for every starting coordinate and
proves the first assertion. This argument handles an unbounded test;
bounded-continuous transition formulas alone would not justify it.

For $t\leq H$, the first assertion says exactly
$M_t=\E[f_\varphi(C_a(H))\mid\mathcal H_t^a]$. For $t\geq H$,
$P_0^a=I$ and the terminal variable is already measurable in
$\mathcal H_t^a$, so the same identity holds. The displayed version is
adapted because the coordinate at $\min(t,H)$ is past-measurable and
each propagated extension is continuous. Each time is integrable by
the same finite-law bound. The tower property proves the martingale
identity for every pair of times.
\end{proof}

These martingales concern each actual finite positive compound-root
process. No limiting path law, limiting filtration or limiting
martingale representation is supplied by the expectation limits.
The compact-frequency Fourier mechanism has a direct antecedent:
Bonaccorsi--Mazzucchi~\cite{BM}, Hypothesis 10 and Theorem 11, already
represent admissible Fourier-measure data and the corresponding
higher-order equation. Compactly supported $L^1$ densities meet those
spectral integrability conditions. Our Poisson clock, finite conditional
identities and fully specified domination reconstruct the frozen
library's version; they do not establish a new analytic representation
principle or priority over that work.

\section{Classical finite-state context}
