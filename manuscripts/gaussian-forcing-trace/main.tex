\begin{abstract}
Countably many independent exponentially damped Gaussian modes define
an actual square-integrable Hilbert-valued field at positive time exactly
when the squared forcing amplitudes divided by the damping rates are
summable. We prove this criterion, the exact covariance and cutoff error,
and mean-square continuity up to time zero. The field is a closed linear
Gaussian field in the original source space; its full Gaussian divergence
exists, lies in second chaos, and has norm at most $\sqrt2$ times the
field norm. The proof reconstructs finite Gaussian integration by parts
and Hilbert--Schmidt completion, including the symmetric/antisymmetric
kernel distinction. In contrast, observation of all Brownian modes at
any positive time retains an infinite first-chaos isometry, preventing
compact divergence recovery. We also prove that the linear-rate deterministic synthesis is compact
but has infinite stochastic trace. Wick-centered observables can converge
when the corresponding raw sums fail; their exact Brownian prediction
and increment energies distinguish initial Hermite scaling from
positive-time roughness. Predictions from arbitrary Gaussian observations retain full projected Gram cross terms. Normalized Wick metric bounds retain the
positive-correlation hypothesis; fractional positive-time exponents and
the antipodal obstruction are proved explicitly. These conclusions concern distinct operators. The trace and Gaussian mechanisms are
classical; the paper consolidates frozen verified constructions and
explicit counterexamples without claiming new stochastic PDE theory.
\end{abstract}

\section{The actual modes and the trace criterion}
Let $\Hh$ be a real separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space with
an isonormal Gaussian map $X:\Hh\to L^2(P)$:
$\E X(h)=0$, $\E[X(h)X(k)]=\langle h,k\rangle$, and finite families
are jointly Gaussian. All scalar and field $L^2$ spaces below are over
the Gaussian sigma-algebra generated by $X(\Hh)$, modulo null sets;
no additional non-Gaussian random factor is included. Embed mutually
orthogonal copies of
$L^2(\R_+)$ isometrically into $\Hh$, with embeddings $\iota_n$,
$n\geq0$. Thus $B_n(t)=X(\iota_n\mathbf1_{(0,t]})$ are independent
Brownian coordinates at the level of their joint Gaussian laws.
Continuous versions give the usual Brownian realization if desired;
only the stated $L^2$ coordinates are used below.

Fix deterministic rates $\lambda_n\geq1$, amplitudes $c_n\in\R$,
and an orthonormal sequence $(e_n)$ in the same source coefficient
space $\Hh$. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profiles}
 k_{n,t}(s)=\mathbf1_{(0,t]}(s)e^{-\lambda_n(t-s)},\quad
 h_{n,t}=\iota_nk_{n,t},\quad Y_n(t)=X(h_{n,t}).
\end{equation}
The deterministic-integrand Brownian notation
$Y_n(t)=\int_0^te^{-\lambda_n(t-s)}\,dB_n(s)$ is equivalent to this
isonormal definition. No adaptedness of the coefficient vectors $e_n$
relative to the noise is needed. They are deterministic output
directions; they need not be orthogonal to all input directions.

For $N\geq1$ put
\[
 F_N(t)=\sum_{n<N}c_n e_nY_n(t)\in L^2(P;\Hh).
\]
This is a Hilbert-valued random field, not a scalar sum and not yet a
claimed solution of an arbitrary SPDE.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:trace}
For each $t>0$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $F_N(t)$ converges in $L^2(P;\Hh)$;
\item $\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq0}\frac{c_n^2}{\lambda_n}<\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
When these hold, write the limit as $F(t)$. Then
\begin{align}
 \|F(t)\|_2^2
 &=\sum_{n\geq0}c_n^2\frac{1-e^{-2\lambda_nt}}{2\lambda_n},
 \label{eq:energy}\\
 \|F(t)-F_N(t)\|_2^2
 &=\sum_{n\geq N}c_n^2\frac{1-e^{-2\lambda_nt}}{2\lambda_n}
 \leq\frac12\sum_{n\geq N}\frac{c_n^2}{\lambda_n}.
 \label{eq:tail}
\end{align}
Setting $F(0)=0$, the curve $t\mapsto F(t)$ is continuous in
$L^2(P;\Hh)$ on $[0,\infty)$.
\end{theorem}

The reciprocal-rate condition is a diagonal instance of the classical
stochastic-convolution Hilbert--Schmidt criterion; see Hairer's
primary lecture notes~\cite{Hairer}, Sections 4.4 and 6. The theorem
here fixes the actual source construction and distinguishes its
positive-time quantifier from the trivial zero-time field.

\section{Finite-mode identities and completion}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:profile}
For $\lambda>0$, let $k_{\lambda,t}(r)=\mathbf1_{(0,t]}(r)
e^{-\lambda(t-r)}$ and
$V_\lambda(t)=(1-e^{-2\lambda t})/(2\lambda)$. Then
\begin{align}
 \|k_{\lambda,t}\|_{L^2}^2&=V_\lambda(t),\label{eq:V}\\
 \langle k_{\lambda,t},k_{\lambda,s}\rangle
 &=e^{-\lambda|t-s|}V_\lambda(\min(s,t)),\label{eq:cov}\\
 \|k_{\lambda,t}-k_{\lambda,s}\|_{L^2}^2
 &=V_\lambda(t)+V_\lambda(s)
       -2e^{-\lambda|t-s|}V_\lambda(\min(s,t)).\label{eq:increment}
\end{align}
For $\lambda\geq1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bounds}
 \frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2\lambda}\leq V_\lambda(t)\leq\frac1{2\lambda},
 \qquad \|k_{\lambda,t}-k_{\lambda,s}\|^2\leq\frac2\lambda.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Integrating $e^{-2\lambda(t-r)}$ over $0<r\leq t$ proves
\eqref{eq:V}. If $s\leq t$, factor
$e^{-\lambda(t-s)}$ from the overlap integral on $(0,s]$ and use
\eqref{eq:V}; symmetry gives \eqref{eq:cov}. Expansion of a squared
norm proves \eqref{eq:increment}. The first bounds follow from
$0\leq e^{-2\lambda t}\leq e^{-2t}\leq1$. Finally
\[
 \|k_{\lambda,t}-k_{\lambda,s}\|^2
 \leq2\|k_{\lambda,t}\|^2+2\|k_{\lambda,s}\|^2\leq2/\lambda.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:trace}]
Orthonormality of the deterministic output vectors gives, for any
finite index set $A$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finiteenergy}
 \left\|\sum_{n\in A}c_ne_nY_n(t)\right\|_2^2
 =\sum_{n\in A}c_n^2\E Y_n(t)^2
 =\sum_{n\in A}c_n^2V_{\lambda_n}(t).
\end{equation}
In particular, disjoint finite index sets give orthogonal fields.
Thus the partial sums are Cauchy exactly when the nonnegative energy
series converges. At fixed $t>0$, the factor $1-e^{-2t}$ is positive,
so \eqref{eq:bounds} makes that convergence equivalent to the
reciprocal-rate series. Completeness gives $F(t)$.
Passing finite orthogonal sums to their limits gives \eqref{eq:energy}
and \eqref{eq:tail}, including the stated upper error bound.

For continuity fix $s\geq0$. Each right side in
\eqref{eq:increment} is continuous in $t$ and vanishes at $t=s$.
Consequently $k_{\lambda_n,t}\to k_{\lambda_n,s}$ in $L^2$ for each
$n$. Orthogonality gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fieldincrement}
 \|F(t)-F(s)\|_2^2
 =\sum_{n\geq0}c_n^2\|k_{\lambda_n,t}-k_{\lambda_n,s}\|_{L^2}^2.
\end{equation}
For example, this equality follows first for $F_N$, then by its
convergence at $t$ and $s$ and the dominated summability in
\eqref{eq:bounds}. The summable bound $2c_n^2/\lambda_n$ permits
dominated convergence of the series, so the squared increment norm
tends to zero. This also proves continuity at $s=0$.
\end{proof}

When $c_n=1$, the rates $(n+1)^2$ admit the field at all times, while
$\lambda_n=n+1$ do not admit the $L^2$ series at any positive time.
Indeed the reciprocal square series converges, for example by
comparison with the telescoping series $1/(n(n+1))$ for $n\geq1$;
the harmonic series diverges by grouping indices in dyadic blocks.
For an undamped unit Brownian series the cutoff energy is $Nt$.
Zero damping lies outside the hypothesis $\lambda_n\geq1$ and is
this separate elementary example, not a substitution into
\eqref{eq:energy} with a zero denominator.

These statements assert $L^2$ convergence and mean-square continuity.
They do not assert a samplewise uniform-in-time limit, a path-space
H\"older exponent, or a continuum nonlinear PDE solution.

\section{The original Gaussian divergence on linear fields}
Let $D$ be the original closed Gaussian gradient, obtained by closing
the polynomial cylindrical gradient in $L^2(P)\to L^2(P;\Hh)$.
Its maximal Hilbert adjoint is the Gaussian divergence $\delta=D^*$.
The relevant conventions agree with~\cite{SV}. Write
$\mathcal L$ for the closed linear span in $L^2(P;\Hh)$ of fields
$aX(b)$ with $a,b\in\Hh$. This is the space of linear Gaussian fields,
not the full random field space.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rankone}
For $a,b\in\Hh$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rankone}
 aX(b)\in D(\delta),\qquad
 \delta(aX(b))=X(a)X(b)-\langle a,b\rangle.
\end{equation}
For a finite-rank deterministic operator
$K=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\otimes b_j$, where
$(a\otimes b)h=a\langle b,h\rangle$, put
$F_K=\sum_j a_jX(b_j)$. Then
\begin{align}
 \|F_K\|_2^2&=\|K\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2,\label{eq:HS}\\
 \|\delta F_K\|_2^2
 &=\|K\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2+\langle K,K^*\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}
 =2\|\operatorname{sym}K\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
 \leq2\|F_K\|_2^2.\label{eq:quadratic}
\end{align}
Here $\operatorname{sym}K=(K+K^*)/2$. The divergence is in second
Gaussian chaos.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a polynomial cylindrical test $f$, Gaussian integration by parts
applied to the product $fX(b)$ gives
\[
 \E[X(a)fX(b)]=\E[\langle Df,a\rangle X(b)]
                       +\langle a,b\rangle\E f.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:rankone} on the core. Passing graph approximants
of $f$ through the pairing extends it to every $f\in D(D)$; the
right side of \eqref{eq:rankone} is in $L^2$ by Gaussian moments.
It therefore meets the defining maximal-adjoint bound.

The isonormal covariance gives
$\E\langle F_K,F_L\rangle=\langle K,L\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}$,
proving \eqref{eq:HS} and representation independence. For the
divergence, the centered Gaussian fourth-moment identity gives
\begin{align*}
 &\E[(X(a)X(b)-\langle a,b\rangle)
       (X(c)X(d)-\langle c,d\rangle)]\\
 &\hspace{2em}=\langle a,c\rangle\langle b,d\rangle
                       +\langle a,d\rangle\langle b,c\rangle.
\end{align*}
That identity follows by differentiating the finite joint Gaussian
exponential moment formula four times at zero. Summing proves the
first equality of \eqref{eq:quadratic}. Orthogonality of the symmetric
and skew-adjoint parts in Hilbert--Schmidt inner product proves the
second equality and the upper bound. The centered quadratic products
are degree-two Hermite polynomials or their polarizations, hence lie
in second chaos.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:complete}
Every field $F\in\mathcal L$ belongs to the original domain $D(\delta)$.
The restricted divergence is bounded, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sqrt2}
 \|\delta F\|_2\leq\sqrt2\|F\|_2.
\end{equation}
It maps into second chaos. For $F=F_K$ with $K$ Hilbert--Schmidt,
its kernel consists exactly of skew-adjoint $K$. Its canonical
gradient recovery is $F_{\operatorname{sym}K}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recovery}
 R(\delta F_K)=F_{\operatorname{sym}K},\qquad
 \|\delta F_K\|_2^2=2\|F_{\operatorname{sym}K}\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The finite-rank isometry \eqref{eq:HS} extends by Hilbert--Schmidt
completion onto $\mathcal L$. For finite-rank approximants $K_j\to K$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:rankone} shows that $\delta F_{K_j}$ is Cauchy, with
the bound \eqref{eq:sqrt2}. Closedness of the original adjoint
therefore puts $(F_K,\lim_j\delta F_{K_j})$ in its original graph.
Second chaos is closed, so the limit remains in it. The exact norm
formula \eqref{eq:quadratic} passes to the limit, proving the kernel
and energy assertions.

On centered Gaussian chaos define the canonical recovery as
$R=DN^{-1}$, where the number operator $N$ has eigenvalue $q$ on
chaos $q\geq1$, and set $R1=0$. On second chaos it is $D/2$.
Finite differentiation gives
\[
 D\delta F_K=F_{K+K^*}=2F_{\operatorname{sym}K}
\]
for finite-rank $K$. Passing to the original closed gradient graph
proves the identity for Hilbert--Schmidt $K$, since both sides
converge strongly. This gives \eqref{eq:recovery}.
\end{proof}

For clarity, the recovery used here is bounded on full scalar $L^2$:
if $f=\sum_{q\geq0}f_q$ is its orthogonal chaos decomposition, the
finite Gaussian identity
$\|Df_q\|_2^2=q\|f_q\|_2^2$ gives
\[
 \|Rf\|_2^2=\sum_{q\geq1}\frac{\|f_q\|_2^2}{q}
 \leq\|f\|_2^2.
\]
The finite identity follows from the one-coordinate Hermite derivative
and orthogonality, then tensorization. Different differentiated chaos
orders are orthogonal. Thus the series defines $R$ by norm completion.
In the frozen library it is constructed as the minimal-norm original
divergence recovery; its chaos identity identifies it with this $DN^{-1}$.
Only its proved action in \eqref{eq:recovery} and on first chaos is
needed in this paper.

The constant $\sqrt2$ is attained for a unit $a=b$ in
\eqref{eq:rankone}. Antisymmetric finite tensors instead give zero
divergence with a nonzero field. Thus no isometry on all of
$\mathcal L$ is asserted, and scalar divergence does not recover the
antisymmetric component of a field.

\section{Divergence and limits of the admitted forcing}
Under the trace criterion of Theorem~\ref{thm:trace}, define
\[
 K_t=\sum_{n\geq0}c_ne_n\otimes h_{n,t}.
\]
Orthonormality of $e_n$ makes this an actual Hilbert--Schmidt sum:
$\|K_t\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\sum_n c_n^2\|h_{n,t}\|^2$.
It follows that $F(t)=F_{K_t}\in\mathcal L$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:forcing}
For every admitted forcing profile, $F(t)\in D(\delta)$ at every
$t\geq0$, and
\[
 \delta F_N(t)\longrightarrow\delta F(t)\quad\hbox{in }L^2(P),
 \qquad \|\delta F(t)-\delta F_N(t)\|_2
          \leq\sqrt2\|F(t)-F_N(t)\|_2.
\]
The curve $t\mapsto\delta F(t)$ is mean-square continuous and has the
exact symmetric recovery \eqref{eq:recovery} at each time.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} to $F(t)$ and to the differences of
finite or limiting fields in $\mathcal L$. The norm estimate proves
the convergence. Applying the same estimate to $F(t)-F(s)$ and using
Theorem~\ref{thm:trace} proves continuity. Recovery follows from the
identity for $K_t$.
\end{proof}

This divergence is the original Gaussian source adjoint, not an It\^o
integral in the physical time of $F$. In particular the field's
deterministic output coefficient vectors may overlap its source
directions, and the centered quadratic correction in
\eqref{eq:rankone} must be retained. The trace criterion controls the
specific field; it does not make every observation or recovery map
compact.

\section{Infinitely many observed modes and noncompact recovery}
Let the complete modal past at time $t$ be
\[
 \mathcal A_t=\sigma(B_n(s):n\geq0,\ 0\leq s\leq t),\qquad
 Q_t=\E[\,\cdot\mid\mathcal A_t].
\]
These are the actual Gaussian observation spaces of all modes,
independent of whether a particular amplitude/rate profile is
trace-admitted.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:noncompact}
The bounded operator $RQ_t:L^2(P)\to L^2(P;\Hh)$ is compact if and
only if $t=0$. At every positive $t$, even recovery restricted to the
observed first chaos contains an infinite-dimensional isometry.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $t>0$ set $u_n=t^{-1/2}\iota_n\mathbf1_{(0,t]}$. These form an
orthonormal sequence in $\Hh$, and $X(u_n)$ form an orthonormal
sequence in $L^2(P)$. Each is observed at time $t$, so
$Q_tX(u_n)=X(u_n)$. On first chaos the number operator is one and
$DX(u_n)=u_n$, a deterministic coefficient vector. Therefore
\[
 RQ_tX(u_n)=u_n\quad\hbox{as a constant field in }L^2(P;\Hh).
\]
These outputs are pairwise at distance $\sqrt2$. A compact operator
would take this bounded input sequence to a sequence with a norm
convergent subsequence, a contradiction. Taking closed linear spans
also proves the asserted isometry. At $t=0$ all modal observations
are zero; $Q_0$ projects onto constants, which $R$ kills. Thus
$RQ_0=0$ is compact.
\end{proof}

The obstruction does not depend on the chosen canonical right inverse.
Indeed, if a bounded linear map $L$ satisfies
$\delta L(X(u))=X(u)$ for every $u$ in an infinite observed coefficient
subspace, adjointness against $X(v)$ gives
\[
 \langle\E L(X(u)),v\rangle
 =\E\langle L(X(u)),DX(v)\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle.
\]
Thus expectation, followed by projection to that subspace, is a
bounded left inverse to $u\mapsto L(X(u))$. Compactness of $L$ would
make the identity on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space compact,
contradicting its orthonormal sequence. This is the elementary
left-inverse mechanism behind the frozen general recovery obstruction.

At zero damping, unit cutoffs have divergent energy; under quadratic
damping, the particular field has finite energy and a valid divergence.
Nevertheless the underlying observation family still contains every
$B_n(t)$ and retains Theorem~\ref{thm:noncompact}. The two conclusions
concern different operators and do not contradict one another.

\section{Compact deterministic synthesis does not give a stochastic trace}
The failed linear-rate example has a useful additional property. At fixed
$t\geq0$, define a \emph{scalar} synthesis map on deterministic amplitudes,
\[
 T_t:\ell^2\longrightarrow L^2(P),\qquad
 T_tc=\sum_{n\geq0}c_nY_n(t),\qquad \lambda_n=n+1.
\]
This is different from the Hilbert-valued random field $F_N(t)$: its
output is a scalar Gaussian random variable, and its input is an arbitrary
square-summable deterministic sequence. The original modal source
isometry supplies $T_t$ on exactly this coefficient space.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:compact}
$T_t$ is a compact bounded operator for every $t\geq0$. For $t>0$ it
is not Hilbert--Schmidt and does not admit the unit-amplitude stochastic
field of Theorem~\ref{thm:trace}. If
$T_t^{(N)}c=\sum_{n<N}c_nY_n(t)$, then
\[
 \|T_t-T_t^{(N)}\|\leq[2(N+1)]^{-1/2}.
\]
The sharper constant here is a direct consequence of the energy formula;
the formal source records the sufficient bound $(N+1)^{-1/2}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The source directions $h_{n,t}$ are orthogonal across $n$, so
$\|\sum_{n\in A}c_nY_n(t)\|_2^2=\sum_{n\in A}c_n^2V_{n+1}(t)$.
Since $V_{n+1}(t)\leq1/[2(n+1)]\leq1/2$, this defines a bounded
operator on $\ell^2$ by completion. For its tail,
\[
 \|(T_t-T_t^{(N)})c\|_2^2
  =\sum_{n\geq N}c_n^2V_{n+1}(t)
  \leq\frac{\|c\|_{\ell^2}^2}{2(N+1)}.
\]
Each $T_t^{(N)}$ has finite-dimensional range. Their norm convergence
implies compactness: approximate the image of the unit ball to any
positive tolerance by a bounded subset of a finite-dimensional range,
which has a finite net; completeness turns total boundedness into
relative compactness. But for the standard coefficient basis $\epsilon_n$,
\[
 \sum_n\|T_t\epsilon_n\|_2^2
   =\sum_nV_{n+1}(t)
   \geq\frac{1-e^{-2t}}2\sum_n\frac1{n+1}=\infty
 \quad(t>0).
\]
Thus $T_t$ is not Hilbert--Schmidt. The same divergent sum is the
cutoff field energy for $c_n=1$, proving the last assertion.
At $t=0$, $T_0=0$ and both compactness and a zero trace are trivial.
\end{proof}
The constant sequence one is outside $\ell^2$. Evaluating $T_t$ there
is not an authorized extension of its deterministic domain. Compactness
controls a supremum of tail sizes; stochastic trace requires their sum.
These are the classical compact-versus-Hilbert--Schmidt alternatives,
now exhibited on the actual exponential modal construction.

\section{Wick centering and exact series energies}
For $h\in\Hh$, define the variance-dependent Wick polynomial
$\mathcal W_d(h)$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wickgen}
 e^{uX(h)-u^2\|h\|^2/2}
   =\sum_{d\geq0}\frac{u^d}{d!}\mathcal W_d(h).
\end{equation}
For example $\mathcal W_0(h)=1$, $\mathcal W_1(h)=X(h)$ and
$\mathcal W_2(h)=X(h)^2-\|h\|^2$. We use monic Hermite normalization,
so the factorial in this definition is essential.
Taking expectations of the product of two such exponentials gives
$e^{uv\langle h,k\rangle}$. Comparing coefficients yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wickcov}
 \E\mathcal W_d(h)=0\ (d>0),\qquad
 \E[\mathcal W_d(h)\mathcal W_e(k)]
  =\mathbf1_{d=e}\,d!\langle h,k\rangle^d.
\end{equation}
Coefficient comparison is justified by differentiating at zero: on
compact parameter sets, polynomial derivatives times exponentials of
a finite Gaussian vector are integrable. Thus this derivation concerns
actual $L^2(P)$ random variables, not formal power series alone.

\begin{theorem}[Exact Wick-series energy]\label{thm:wickseries}
Let $(g_n)$ be pairwise orthogonal directions in $\Hh$, let $d\geq1$
be an integer, and let $a_n\in\R$. Then
\[
 S=\sum_n a_n\mathcal W_d(g_n)\quad\hbox{converges in }L^2(P)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \sum_n a_n^2d!\|g_n\|^{2d}<\infty.
\]
In that case the squared norm is exactly the latter sum, and the
squared cutoff error is its corresponding tail. The limit is in
homogeneous chaos $d$, belongs to the original closed gradient domain,
and its canonical divergence recovery satisfies
\[
 \delta RS=S,\qquad \|RS\|_2^2=\frac1d\|S\|_2^2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equation \eqref{eq:wickcov} makes distinct terms orthogonal. Pythagoras
therefore gives the finite norm and every finite tail norm exactly.
The nonnegative energy series converges precisely when those tails
are Cauchy; completeness proves existence and necessity. The closed
$d$th chaos contains the limit.

For completeness the gradient assertion uses its original graph.
Differentiating \eqref{eq:wickgen} gives
$D\mathcal W_d(g)=d\,g\mathcal W_{d-1}(g)$.
For distinct orthogonal directions the resulting vector fields are
orthogonal as well, and for a single term their squared norm is
\[
 d^2\|g\|^2(d-1)!\|g\|^{2(d-1)}
       =d\,d!\|g\|^{2d}.
\]
Thus the finite partial sums and their gradients are Cauchy. Closedness
places $S$ in the original gradient domain with
$\|DS\|_2^2=d\|S\|_2^2$. On chaos $d$, the recovery already defined
above is $R=D/d$, giving its exact energy. The finite product rule
$\delta(gA)=X(g)A-\langle g,DA\rangle$, together with the recurrence for $d\geq2$
$\mathcal W_d(g)=X(g)\mathcal W_{d-1}(g)
 -(d-1)\|g\|^2\mathcal W_{d-2}(g)$, gives
$\delta DS_N=dS_N$ (the $d=1$ case uses $\delta g=X(g)$);
closedness of the original adjoint, applied to
$DS_N/d\to DS/d$ and $S_N\to S$, proves $\delta RS=S$.
\end{proof}

since $V_{n+1}(t)^d\leq[2(n+1)]^{-d}$. At $d=2$ the raw squares
$\sum_nY_n(t)^2$ again have divergent expectations for $t>0$.
This is a direct instantiation of Theorem~\ref{thm:wickseries}, rather
than a separately named positive-time theorem in the formal source.
It constructs a scalar centered observable. It does not create the
missing unit-amplitude Hilbert-valued first-chaos field or a nonlinear
stochastic PDE solution. Its covariance energy is a power of the
modal variance, which explains why its summability differs.

\subsection{Arbitrary observations retain a full projected Gram matrix}
Let $(o_\alpha)$ be any family in $\Hh$, let
$K=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{o_\alpha\}$, and let $P_K$ be its
orthogonal projection. Write $\mathcal A$ for the completed sigma
algebra generated by the actual Gaussian variables $X(o_\alpha)$.
The original pairwise orthogonality of $(g_n)$ need not survive
$P_K$. This changes the energy calculation, although it does not
prevent convergence of a predicted response.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fullwickprediction}
For every $g\in\Hh$ and integer $d\geq0$,
\[
 \E[\mathcal W_d(g)\mid\mathcal A]=\mathcal W_d(P_Kg).
\]
In particular the variance in the Wick polynomial on the right is
$\|P_Kg\|^2$, not $\|g\|^2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Put $v=P_Kg$ and $r=g-v$. Approximation of $v$ by finite linear
combinations of observations, followed by $L^2$ convergence and an
almost surely convergent subsequence, makes $X(v)$ measurable for
$\mathcal A$. The variable $X(r)$ has zero covariance with every
finite vector of observed coordinates. Joint Gaussianity gives
independence from each such vector; the cylinder-event monotone
class argument extends independence to their generated sigma
algebra and its completion. Also $\langle v,r\rangle=0$, so
$\|g\|^2=\|v\|^2+\|r\|^2$. Hence for real $z$,
\[
 \E[e^{zX(g)-z^2\|g\|^2/2}\mid\mathcal A]
       =e^{zX(v)-z^2\|v\|^2/2}.
\]
Both sides can be differentiated any fixed number of times at
zero as $L^2$ variables. On a compact $z$ interval each derivative
is bounded by a fixed polynomial in the Gaussian coordinates
times $e^{C(|X(v)|+|X(r)|)+C}$, whose square is integrable.
Conditional expectation is bounded on $L^2$, so differentiation
commutes with it. The generating function gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:projectedgram}
Assume the hypotheses and finite energy of
Theorem~\ref{thm:wickseries}. Set $v_n=P_Kg_n$. Then
\[
 \widehat S:=\E[S\mid\mathcal A]
       =\sum_n a_n\mathcal W_d(v_n)\quad\hbox{in }L^2(P).
\]
For every finite index set $F$, its partial-sum energy is exactly
\[
 G_F=d!\sum_{i,j\in F}a_i a_j\langle v_i,v_j\rangle^d,
 \qquad
 0\leq G_F\leq\sum_{i\in F}a_i^2d!\|g_i\|^{2d}.
\]
As finite sets increase to all indices, $G_F\to\|\widehat S\|_2^2$.
The canonical original divergence recovery obeys
\[
 \delta R\widehat S=\widehat S,\qquad
 \|R\widehat S\|_2^2=d^{-1}\|\widehat S\|_2^2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Apply the bounded conditional expectation to the convergent finite
partial sums of $S$ and use Lemma~\ref{lem:fullwickprediction} term
by term. This proves convergence over arbitrary finite cutoffs,
without an orthogonality assertion for $(v_n)$.
Expanding the finite squared norm and using \eqref{eq:wickcov}
gives $G_F$. Its lower bound is a squared norm, and its upper bound
is the contraction inequality for the conditional expectation of
the original orthogonal finite sum. Norm continuity proves its
limit. For any finite tail $F$ the same argument gives
\[
 \Big\|\sum_{i\in F}a_i\mathcal W_d(v_i)\Big\|_2^2
       \leq\sum_{i\in F}a_i^2d!\|g_i\|^{2d},
\]
so no absolute convergence of an infinite double sum is needed.
Every predicted finite sum lies in homogeneous chaos $d$, and that
chaos is closed. The inverse-number recovery energy is therefore
$d^{-1}\|\widehat S\|_2^2$. Its original adjoint graph follows
either from the previously proved recovery on centered $L^2$ or
by bounded recovery convergence and closedness of $\delta$ on the
finite-sum graph. The target is centered because $d\geq1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Initial scaling and positive-time roughness}
A final exact example separates chaos degree from temporal roughness.
For any square-summable $a=(a_n)$ and integer $d\geq1$, set
\[
 Z_d(t)=\sum_n a_n\mathcal W_d(\iota_n\mathbf1_{(0,t]}),
 \qquad C_d=d!\sum_na_n^2.
\]
This definition uses undamped Brownian directions and is separate from
the exponentially damped $S_d(t)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:wickseries} gives
$\|Z_d(t)\|_2^2=C_dt^d$ and $Z_d(0)=0$.

For $0\leq s\leq t$, independent Brownian increments give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wickpredict}
 \E[Z_d(t)\mid\mathcal A_s]=Z_d(s).
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal A_s$ is the full modal past defined earlier, not only
the present values. To verify this point explicitly, the Gaussian
increment $B_n(t)-B_n(s)$ has zero covariance with every
$B_m(r)$, $r\leq s$. Joint Gaussianity gives independence from each
finite past vector, and a monotone-class argument gives independence
from their entire generated sigma-algebra. Factoring the exponential
in \eqref{eq:wickgen} into its past and increment factors shows that
the conditional expectation of its increment factor is one.
Differentiation at zero, justified by Gaussian exponential domination,
proves the finite Wick identity. Conditional expectation is an
$L^2$ contraction, so passing the convergent series proves
\eqref{eq:wickpredict}.

Orthogonal projection onto the observed past now gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wickincrement}
 \E[Z_d(t)Z_d(s)]=C_ds^d,\qquad
 \|Z_d(t)-Z_d(s)\|_2^2=C_d(t^d-s^d),\quad s\leq t.
\end{equation}
Indeed $Z_d(s)$ is observed at $s$, so its pairing with $Z_d(t)$
equals its pairing with the conditional expectation in
\eqref{eq:wickpredict}. Expansion of the squared norm proves the
second identity. It also proves mean-square continuity on all
$[0,\infty)$ without a sample-path assertion.

\section{Covariance powers do not identify the process law}
The preceding Wick martingales give an actual realization of integer
entrywise covariance powers. If $X(h_t)$ is any Gaussian source
family, define
\[
 V_q(t)=\frac{\mathcal W_q(h_t)}{\sqrt{q!}},\qquad q\geq1.
\]
The proved Wick pairing identity gives
\[
 \E[V_q(s)V_q(t)]=\langle h_s,h_t\rangle^q.
\]
For any finite times and real coefficients $c_i$ it follows that
\[
 \sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\langle h_{t_i},h_{t_j}\rangle^q
 =\left\|\sum_i c_i V_q(t_i)\right\|_2^2\geq0.
\]
Thus the entrywise power is a genuine positive semidefinite Gram
matrix. For fractional Brownian directions the exact same identity
is the content of \code{FractionalWickCovariance}; it is a statement
about actual finite covariances and original Gaussian derivatives,
not a new fractional-time generator or a Hermite-process construction.

For Brownian $h_t=\mathbf1_{(0,t]}$, take independent actual modal
Brownian motions $B_0,B_1$ from the initial construction and set
\[
 V_q(t)=\frac{\mathcal W_q(\iota_0\mathbf1_{(0,t]})}{\sqrt{q!}},
 \qquad G_q(t)=B_1(t^q),\qquad t\geq0.
\]
Their paths are continuous, both are centered martingales for their
respective Brownian pasts, and both have covariance $\min(s,t)^q$.
For $V_q$ the martingale proof is the full-past Wick identity above;
for $G_q$ it is Brownian independence on the deterministic increasing
clock $t^q$. No equality of their laws follows.

Indeed, already at $q=2$,
\[
 V_2(t)=\frac{B_0(t)^2-t}{\sqrt2},\qquad
 \E V_2(t)^2=t^2,\qquad \E V_2(t)^4=15t^4,
\]
whereas $\E G_2(t)^4=3t^4$. To verify the former fourth moment,
expand $(B_0(t)^2-t)^4$ and use the Gaussian even moments
$1,3,15,105$ in degrees $2,4,6,8$; the numerator is $60t^4$,
and division by $(\sqrt2)^4=4$ gives $15t^4$.
For $s>0$, $h>0$, let $\Delta=B_0(s+h)-B_0(s)$.
The exact increment identity
\[
 V_2(s+h)-V_2(s)
 =\sqrt2 B_0(s)\Delta+\frac{\Delta^2-h}{\sqrt2}
\]
and independent centered normal $\Delta$ give
\[
 \E[(V_2(s+h)-V_2(s))^2\mid\mathcal F_s^{B_0}]
 =2h B_0(s)^2+h^2.
\]
The cross term vanishes by the third normal moment. Since
$B_0(s)^2=\sqrt2 V_2(s)+s$ is observed in the $V_2$ past,
the tower property gives this same nonconstant conditional second
moment on that smaller past. Thus its increments are not independent.
The corresponding Gaussian increment of $G_2$ is independent of its
past and has deterministic variance $(s+h)^2-s^2=2sh+h^2$.
Identical covariance and unconditional increment energies have
therefore concealed different conditional laws. These explicit
fourth-moment and conditional-variance comparisons are classical human
deductions, not additional named formal theorems.

\subsection{Exact finite innovations and generalized eigenvalues}
At the integer times $1,\ldots,n$, the common covariance matrix is
\[
 C_k(i,j)=\min(i+1,j+1)^k,\qquad0\leq i,j<n,\quad k\geq1.
\]
Write $L_{ij}=\mathbf1_{\{j\leq i\}}$ and
$d_k(j)=(j+1)^k-j^k>0$. Telescoping the powers gives the exact
factorization
\[
 C_k=L\,\operatorname{diag}(d_k(j))L^\top.
\]
It is a covariance factorization: the Gaussian process $G_k$ has
independent innovations with these variances. The non-Gaussian
process with the same covariance need not have independent
innovations, as the example just proved shows. In particular a
finite covariance calculation is not a probabilistic independence
argument.

The triangular matrix $L$ is invertible, its inverse taking adjacent
differences. Thus, for $k,l\geq1$, the exact generalized eigenvalues
of $(C_k,C_l)$ are
\[
 \rho_j=\frac{d_k(j)}{d_l(j)}.
\]
More explicitly, let $v_0=e_0$ and $v_j=e_j-e_{j-1}$ for $j>0$.
Then $L^\top v_j=e_j$, so
$C_kv_j=d_k(j)Le_j=\rho_j C_lv_j$.
These $n$ nonzero vectors form a basis. Congruence by $L^{-1}$
also proves the sharp quadratic-form comparisons
\[
 \left(\min_j\rho_j\right)C_l\preceq C_k
 \preceq\left(\max_j\rho_j\right)C_l.
\]
Here $A\preceq B$ means that $B-A$ has a nonnegative quadratic
form. Indeed the diagonal congruence of $C_k-cC_l$ has entries
$d_k(j)-c d_l(j)$; these are all nonnegative exactly when
$c\leq\min_j\rho_j$. The upper comparison is identical with
the roles reversed, and the vectors $v_j$ attain the endpoints.
These sharp comparison statements are direct classical deductions
from the verified factorization and generalized eigenpairs.

For $k=3$, $l=2$, $j=1$, the comparison value is $7/3$, not the
single innovation variance $7$. Nor is $C_k$ the ordinary matrix
power of $C_1$. At $n=2$,
\[
 C_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\quad
 C_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix},\quad
 C_1^2=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Entrywise covariance powers, ordinary matrix powers, independent
Gaussian innovation variances and generalized comparison ratios
are four different quantities. None of these finite identities
identifies a physical-time generator, curvature operator or
scale-independent estimate. The underlying Wick and Gaussian
mechanisms are classical, with \cite{Baudoin} providing the
previously credited chaos context.


\section{Wick metric transfer and its antipodal boundary}\label{sec:wickmetric}
Covariance powers also give an exact metric calculation. Retain the
monic Wick convention of \eqref{eq:wickgen} and put
$V_q(h)=\mathcal W_q(h)/\sqrt{q!}$, for an integer $q\geq1$.
The mixed Wick moment identity already proved gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wickdistance}
 \|V_q(h)-V_q(k)\|_2^2
 =\|h\|^{2q}+\|k\|^{2q}-2\langle h,k\rangle^q.
\end{equation}
This identity follows by expanding the squared norm and applying the
same pairing formula to each of the three terms; it holds for all
source directions, with no equal-variance assumption.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:wickmetric}
If $\|h\|=\|k\|=1$ and $0\leq\rho:=\langle h,k\rangle\leq1$, then
\[
 \|h-k\|^2\leq\|V_q(h)-V_q(k)\|_2^2\leq q\|h-k\|^2.
\]
At degree two and equal squared norm $v$, the exact formula is
\[
 \|V_2(h)-V_2(k)\|_2^2
   =(v+\langle h,k\rangle)\|h-k\|^2.
\]
In particular $V_2(h)=V_2(-h)$ as $L^2$ classes, so the global
map is not injective when the source space is nonzero.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For unit directions, \eqref{eq:wickdistance} is $2(1-\rho^q)$,
whereas $\|h-k\|^2=2(1-\rho)$. Since
\[
 1-\rho^q=(1-\rho)\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\rho^j,
 \qquad 1\leq\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\rho^j\leq q,
\]
the bounds follow, including $\rho=1$. For equal squared norm $v$,
the degree-two formula is
$2(v^2-\langle h,k\rangle^2)
 =(v+\langle h,k\rangle)(2v-2\langle h,k\rangle)$.
Taking $k=-h$ makes this norm zero. If $h\ne0$, its source distance
from $-h$ is $2\|h\|>0$, proving noninjectivity. More generally,
\eqref{eq:wickdistance} gives this antipodal identification at every
even degree. Consequently no positive global lower metric constant
on the full unit sphere follows from the positive-correlation bounds.
\end{proof}

Here is a precise fractional application, with a labelled additional
human asymptotic deduction. For $0<H<1$, let $h_t$ be the actual
fractional Gaussian directions with
\[
 \langle h_s,h_t\rangle
 =\tfrac12(s^{2H}+t^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}),\qquad \|h_t\|=t^H.
\]
For $t>0$ define $u_t=t^{-H}h_t$. These are unit directions. Their
inner products are nonnegative: for $0\leq s\leq t$,
$(t-s)^{2H}\leq t^{2H}$, so the covariance is nonnegative.
Cauchy--Schwarz gives the upper bound one. The proposition therefore
applies to the normalized fractional coordinates $V_q(u_t)$.
It does not apply at zero by assigning a value to $0^{-H}h_0$.

For clarity the exact positive-time roughness can be read from these
formulas, without a regularity theorem for sample paths. Fix
$0<a<b<\infty$. For $a\leq s<t\leq b$ and $d=t-s$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fractionalunitmetric}
 \|u_t-u_s\|^2
 =\frac{d^{2H}-(t^H-s^H)^2}{s^Ht^H}.
\end{equation}
Indeed expansion of the normalized covariance gives this equality.
The mean-value theorem bounds $|t^H-s^H|\leq Ld$ with
$L=Ha^{H-1}$. Choose $d_0>0$ so that
$L^2d_0^{2-2H}\leq1/2$, possible since $H<1$.
For $0<d\leq d_0$, \eqref{eq:fractionalunitmetric} and the proposition
give the full two-sided bounds
\[
 \frac{d^{2H}}{2b^{2H}}
 \leq\|u_t-u_s\|^2
 \leq\|V_q(u_t)-V_q(u_s)\|_2^2
 \leq q\|u_t-u_s\|^2
 \leq\frac{q\,d^{2H}}{a^{2H}}.
\]
In particular every fixed degree has the same local mean-square
increment exponent $2H$ on positive-time compact intervals. An upper
bound of order $d^\beta$ with $\beta>2H$ cannot hold uniformly there:
fix $s\in(a,b)$, take $t=s+d$ and divide the positive lower bound
by $d^\beta$. It diverges as $d\downarrow0$.

The source maps the normalized fractional metric comparison; the
last compact-interval exponent calculation is the human deduction
just proved. Normalization changes the process and excludes time
zero. This is a statement about $L^2$ increments, not an optimal
sample-path H\"older theorem. Pointwise Wick polynomials of one
fractional Gaussian family are not identified with a separately
constructed Hermite process, nor with a positive Markov generator.
The calculation uses the classical Wick pairing mechanism credited
above, without a new covariance or roughness principle claim.


\section{A separate temporal threshold for one fractional forcing mode}
The reciprocal-rate condition concerns infinitely many spatial modes.
A singular temporal profile has an independent obstruction even in one
mode. Use a single original Brownian time channel and its isonormal
isometry $I:L^2(\R_+)\to L^2(P)$, with
$I(\mathbf1_{(0,t]})=B_t$. For $\alpha>0$ set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rlforcingprofile}
 r_{\alpha,t}(s)=\frac{\mathbf1_{(0,t)}(s)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
                         (t-s)^{\alpha-1},\qquad t>0.
\end{equation}
An endpoint value at $s=t$ is immaterial. The gamma factor is strictly
positive and finite in this parameter range. This is the zero-generator
Riemann--Liouville response, not an exponential or a postulated
Mittag--Leffler resolvent.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:rlforcing}
For every $t>0$, $r_{\alpha,t}\in L^2(\R_+)$ if and only if
$\alpha>1/2$. In that case the actual centered Gaussian variable
$R_\alpha(t)=I(r_{\alpha,t})$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rlforcingvariance}
 \|R_\alpha(t)\|_2^2
 =\frac{t^{2\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2(2\alpha-1)},
 \qquad R_\alpha(0)=0.
\end{equation}
It belongs to the original closed Gaussian derivative domain, with
its deterministic derivative equal to the embedded time profile.
For every $p>2\alpha-1$, real $C$ and $\delta>0$, some
$0<t<\min(\delta,1)$ satisfies
$Ct^p<\|R_\alpha(t)-R_\alpha(0)\|_2^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Changing variable $u=t-s$ reduces squared integrability to
$\int_0^t u^{2\alpha-2}du<\infty$. The integral is finite precisely
when $2\alpha-2>-1$, and its value gives
\eqref{eq:rlforcingvariance} by the actual isometry. In particular the
critical endpoint is excluded, not admitted by a convention for $0^\beta$.
For clarity, truncating to $\epsilon<t-s<t$ gives the exact energies
\[
 \frac1{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}
 \begin{cases}
 \log(t/\epsilon),&\alpha=1/2,\\[2pt]
 \displaystyle\frac{t^{2\alpha-1}-\epsilon^{2\alpha-1}}{2\alpha-1},
                                      &\alpha\ne1/2.
 \end{cases}
\]
They diverge as $\epsilon\downarrow0$ for $0<\alpha\leq1/2$.
Thus these truncated variables cannot converge in the original
Gaussian $L^2$ space. No distribution-valued extension of that isometry
is asserted.

For the original derivative graph, approximate the time profile by
finite orthogonal coefficient projections in the isonormal source.
Their Gaussian linear observables have their constant deterministic
gradients, by cylindrical differentiation. Both values and gradients
converge in their original $L^2$ norms, so closedness gives the claimed
graph membership and derivative. This is differentiation in the
Gaussian source, not differentiation in physical time.

Finally let $c_\alpha=1/(\Gamma(\alpha)^2(2\alpha-1))>0$.
If $p>2\alpha-1$, then $Ct^{p-(2\alpha-1)}\to0$ as $t\downarrow0$,
so it is smaller than $c_\alpha$ on a sufficiently small positive
interval. Multiplication by $t^{2\alpha-1}>0$ proves the exclusion.
This also covers negative $C$. Conversely, for any $p\leq2\alpha-1$
and $0<t\leq1$, the exact variance is at most $c_\alpha t^p$.
The latter elementary bound and the explicit cutoff energies are
classical human deductions from the displayed integral.
\end{proof}
For $1/2<\alpha<1$ this rules out every proposed $O(t)$ initial variance
bound. At $\alpha=1$ the actual profile is $\mathbf1_{(0,t)}$ and
$R_1(t)=B_t$. These are exact initial-time mean-square statements;
no sample-path H\"older theorem or nonzero-generator stochastic PDE
existence conclusion has been inferred.

\subsection*{Matching the variance does not produce standard fBM}
The distinction can be proved directly, rather than inferred from the
name of the kernel. For $0<H<1$ define on the same Brownian time channel
\[
 U_H(t)=\sqrt{2H}\,I\!\left(\mathbf1_{(0,t)}(s)
                                      (t-s)^{H-1/2}\right).
\]
These are genuinely jointly centered Gaussian variables because finite
linear combinations are images of deterministic $L^2$ profiles.
Their actual variance is $t^{2H}$, and the isometry gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rlcovariance}
 \E[U_H(s)U_H(t)]
 =2H\int_0^{s\wedge t}(s-r)^{H-1/2}(t-r)^{H-1/2}\,dr.
\end{equation}
This is the mapped power-kernel covariance with its explicit variance
normalization. A further \emph{classical human diagnostic} shows that
it differs from standard fBM for every $H\ne1/2$.
Put $\beta=H-1/2$ and take $t\downarrow0$ against time one. Rescaling
$r=tv$ in \eqref{eq:rlcovariance} yields
\[
 \frac{\E[U_H(t)U_H(1)]}{t^{H+1/2}}
 =2H\int_0^1(1-v)^\beta(1-tv)^\beta\,dv
 \longrightarrow\frac{2H}{H+1/2}.
\]
For $0<t\leq1/2$, the second power is bounded by a fixed constant,
and $(1-v)^\beta$ is integrable because $\beta>-1$.
Dominated convergence therefore proves the limit, including negative
$\beta$. In contrast the standard fBM covariance is
\[
 R_H(t,1)=\tfrac12\bigl(t^{2H}+1-(1-t)^{2H}\bigr).
\]
For $H<1/2$ it is asymptotic to $t^{2H}/2$; for $H>1/2$ it is
asymptotic to $Ht$, by the ordinary Taylor expansion at one.
In both cases $H+1/2$ exceeds the respective leading exponent, so
$\E[U_H(t)U_H(1)]/R_H(t,1)\to0$. The covariances cannot be equal.
At $H=1/2$, both formulas reduce to $s\wedge t$ and the constructed
process is the original Brownian motion.
