\begin{abstract}
We study replacing the Gaussian noise of score-based diffusion with an
\emph{iterated-Brownian} (Brownian-time) noise $Z_t=B_1(|B_2^H(t)|)$. Writing
$S_t=|B_2^H(t)|$ for the hidden clock, the corruption is a hidden-scale Gaussian
mixture, $Z_t\mid S_t=s\sim\mathcal N(0,s)$, rather than a single Gaussian blur.
We derive the resulting marginal density, a \emph{hidden-clock Tweedie} score
formula, and show that recovering the posterior mean $\E[X_0\mid X_t]$ requires
an \emph{unavoidable} coupled latent-clock posterior term. We record the
Allouba--Zheng fourth-order PDE shadow for averaged observables and its
fractional generalization. We then test the central hypothesis --- that
Brownian-time noising forces a denoiser to learn latent corruption scale,
yielding a multi-scale geometry distinct from Gaussian diffusion --- with exact
1-D quadrature and sandbox-scale neural experiments under \emph{matched marginal
variance}. The honest finding is two-sided: the hidden-scale \emph{mixture}
genuinely changes the score geometry (saturating-tail scores, leptokurtic
marginals) and the latent scale is strongly inferable from a single corrupted
sample; but the \emph{Brownian-time path structure} and the \emph{Hurst index}
$H$ are \textbf{invisible} to a single-time matched-variance denoiser --- at one
time the construction reduces \emph{exactly} to a Gaussian scale mixture.
Crucially, an exact-score generative comparison (annealed Langevin down a matched
variance ladder, no training confound) finds hidden-clock generation
\textbf{indistinguishable from---marginally worse than} matched Gaussian. We thus
report \emph{no} performance advantage, make no generative claim, and assert no
Markov reverse SDE in $x$-space; the rich hidden-clock structure is a correct
reframing whose only remaining live upside is a possible learning/optimization
effect for trained networks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup and non-claims}
Ordinary diffusion corrupts data by $X_t=a(t)X_0+\sigma(t)\varepsilon$,
$\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, with a deterministic schedule $\sigma(t)^2$. We
replace $\varepsilon$ by a Brownian-time variable
\begin{equation}
  Z_t^H=B_1\!\big(|B_2^H(t)|\big),\qquad
  S_t=|B_2^H(t)|\sim\text{half-normal},\ \ B_2^H(t)\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau),\
  \tau=t^{2H},
\end{equation}
so that $Z_t^H\mid S_t=s\sim\mathcal N(0,s)$. The clock $S_t$ is a
\emph{variance}. We study $X_t=a(t)X_0+\sigma(t)Z_t^H$.

\paragraph{Non-claims (held throughout).} (i)~The Brownian-time forward is
\emph{not} Markov in $x$; we do \emph{not} claim a reverse-time SDE in
$x$-space. (ii)~The fourth-order PDE below governs \emph{averaged observables},
not a transition density. (iii)~We claim no working generative sampler; all
neural experiments are sandbox-scale pipeline validations under matched
controls. These follow the discipline of the Type~II fBM score
paper~\cite{FontesFBMScore}, which establishes scores and a \emph{matching-marginal}
Markov auxiliary \emph{without} asserting process-level time reversal of fBM.

\section{Hidden-clock density and score}
Conditioning on $X_0$ and $S_t=s$ gives a Gaussian channel with random variance
$\sigma(t)^2 s$, hence the hidden-scale mixture marginal
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marg}
  p_t(x)=\int_0^\infty \E_{X_0}\!\big[\phi_{\sigma(t)^2 s}(x-a(t)X_0)\big]\,
  \rho_t(s)\,ds,\qquad \rho_t(s)=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi\tau}}e^{-s^2/2\tau}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Hidden-clock Tweedie]\label{prop:tweedie}
With $\phi_v$ the $\mathcal N(0,v)$ density,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hctweedie}
  \partial_x\log p_t(x)=\E\!\left[-\frac{x-a(t)X_0}{\sigma(t)^2 S_t}\ \middle|\ X_t=x\right],
\end{equation}
the posterior being the joint law of $(X_0,S_t)\mid X_t=x$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent This is the operator-derivative Tweedie of~\cite{FontesFBMScore} but
with the \emph{deterministic} bracket replaced by the random posterior precision
$\E[1/S_t\mid x]$, which does not factor out.

\begin{proposition}[Unavoidable latent-clock term]\label{prop:postmean}
Let $\tilde\E[\,\cdot\,]=\E[\,\cdot/S_t\mid x]/\E[1/S_t\mid x]$ be the
precision-weighted posterior. Then
\begin{equation}
  a(t)\,\tilde\E[X_0\mid x]=x+\frac{\sigma(t)^2}{\E[1/S_t\mid x]}\,\partial_x\log p_t(x).
\end{equation}
The ordinary posterior mean $\E[X_0\mid x]$ coincides with $\tilde\E[X_0\mid x]$
\emph{iff} $S_t\perp X_0\mid X_t$, which fails for non-Gaussian data. Hence
passing between the score and $\E[X_0\mid x]$ requires the joint $(X_0,S_t)$
posterior, not a scalar clock moment.
\end{proposition}

\section{Fourth-order PDE shadow (averaged observables)}
For $u(t,x)=\E f(x+B_1(|B_2(t)|))$ ($H=\tfrac12$, $\sigma=1$), conditioning on
the half-normal clock and using $\partial_s P_s=\tfrac12\partial_x^2 P_s$ for the
heat semigroup $P_s$ together with the heat equation satisfied by $\rho_t$ in its
scale yields the Allouba--Zheng equation~\cite{AllouZheng2001,FontesIBMIto}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AZ}
  \partial_t u=\tfrac18\,\partial_x^4 u+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi t}}\,\partial_x^2 f.
\end{equation}
The same computation with the fractional clock scale $\tau=t^{2H}$
(so $\partial_t\rho_t=H t^{2H-1}\partial_s^2\rho_t$, $\rho_t(0)=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,t^{-H}$)
gives the averaged fractional equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fAZ}
  \partial_t u=\frac{H}{4}\,t^{2H-1}\partial_x^4 u+\frac{H}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,t^{H-1}\partial_x^2 f,
\end{equation}
which reduces to~\eqref{eq:AZ} at $H=\tfrac12$. We emphasize that
\eqref{eq:AZ}--\eqref{eq:fAZ} are statements about the \emph{expectation} of a
fixed observable $f$, and provide motivation (``more than heat flow''), not a
generative guarantee. The source-resolved chaos
expansion~\cite{FontesChaos} provides the orthogonal decomposition of $Z_t$ into
outer-scenery and inner-clock components that underlies the
``infer-the-clock-as-a-separate-source'' picture.

\section{Experiments (matched marginal variance throughout)}
We match the Gaussian baseline's noise variance to the mixture's,
$\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^2(t)=\sigma(t)^2\E[S_t]=\sigma(t)^2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,t^{H}$;
for a target variance $v$ the clock scale is $\tau=\pi v^2/2$. An audit
(Exp.~4) confirms $\Var(\text{Gaussian})=\Var(\text{IBM})=v$ to $<2\%$.

\subsection{Experiment 1: exact 1-D scores by quadrature}
For Gaussian-mixture data we compute~\eqref{eq:marg}, its score, and the clock
posterior by quadrature, validated against finite differences ($<10^{-5}$), an
$8\times10^6$-sample histogram ($0.14\%$), and Monte Carlo. Findings
(Fig.~\ref{fig:matched},~\ref{fig:clock}):
\begin{itemize}
\item The hidden-clock marginal is leptokurtic vs the matched Gaussian; the
  score \emph{saturates} in the tails ($\approx\pm2$) where the Gaussian score is
  linear ($\approx\pm4.5$). $\max_x|s_{\text{IBM}}-s_{\text{Gauss}}|\in[2.3,4.4]$.
\item The clock is inferable: $\E[S_t\mid x]$ ranges from below the prior at data
  modes to $2$--$5\times$ above it in the tails.
\item A fixed-variance Tweedie mis-recovers $\E[X_0\mid x]$ with error $1.5$--$3.1$,
  confirming Prop.~\ref{prop:postmean}.
\item \textbf{At matched variance, $H\in\{\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\tfrac14\}$ give
  bit-identical scores (max diff $=0$).}
\end{itemize}

\noindent Exp.~3: a clock head recovers $\log S_t$ with test $R^2$ rising

\subsection{Experiment 6: exact-score generative comparison}
The decisive test of \emph{generation} (not denoising MSE), free of training
confounds. We run \emph{identical} annealed Langevin dynamics down the
\emph{same} matched marginal-variance ladder, using the exact quadrature scores
of the Gaussian vs hidden-clock families, and measure sample quality against the
true data. Annealed Langevin needs only each marginal's score and makes no
reverse-SDE claim, consistent with the non-Markov forward. Both arms recover the
data faithfully (Fig.~\ref{fig:gen}); 1-Wasserstein to the data, with the
finite-sample floor in parentheses:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
data & Gaussian $W_1$ & hidden-clock $W_1$ & $\Delta$ (floor)\\
\midrule
bimodal       & 0.0410 & 0.0421 & $+0.0011$ \ (0.0217)\\
trimodal asym & 0.0343 & 0.0413 & $+0.0071$ \ (0.0367)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The two are \textbf{statistically indistinguishable}; the hidden-clock arm is if
anything marginally \emph{worse}, within the floor. This is the expected honest
outcome: Langevin equilibrates to each marginal via its exact score, so endpoint
quality is set by the (matched) bottom-of-ladder marginal, not by the score
geometry along the way. The saturating-tail score is a real landscape difference
that confers \emph{no} generative advantage at the exact-score level.

\subsection{Experiment 7: the score-matching obstruction (learned)}
The last hypothesis is a learning/optimization benefit for a trained network.
Probing it exposes a sharp obstruction. Denoising score matching regresses onto
the per-sample target $-\text{noise}/(\sigma^2 S)$; per coordinate this is
$-z/\sqrt S$ with $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, whose second moment is $\mathbb
E[1/S]$. Because the half-normal clock density is \emph{positive at} $S=0$,
$\mathbb E[1/S]=\int_0^\infty s^{-1}\rho(s)\,ds=\infty$: \textbf{the
hidden-clock DSM target has infinite variance}, whereas the Gaussian target
$-z/\sqrt v$ has finite second moment $1/v$. (The score itself, the
\emph{conditional mean} of this target, is finite---it is the training
\emph{estimator} that is ill-posed.) On an 8-mode 2-D ring with matched
architecture/budget/seed/ladder we observe all three consequences: (A) empirical
$\mathbb E[1/S]$ grows without bound as the clock floor $\to0$
($5.6\!\to\!29.1$ for floors $10^{-1}\!\to\!10^{-5}$ at $v{=}0.25$); (B) naive
hidden-clock score matching \emph{diverges to NaN}, while the Gaussian arm trains
normally, and a clock floor $S\ge10^{-2}$ restores trainability; (C) even with
the floor the residual target variance stays higher, so at matched budget the fit
is worse and generation is worse (sliced-Wasserstein $0.36$ vs Gaussian $0.18$;
floor $0.07$). Thus hidden-clock noising carries a \textbf{learning penalty} and
requires regularization the Gaussian baseline does not---no advantage, consistent
with Exp.~6.

\subsection{Experiment 8: the augmented (x, clock) reverse}
The Exp.~7 obstruction is, however, an artifact of the \emph{marginal}-score
parameterization, and it is removed by working in the augmented $(x,S)$ space ---
the coordinate that Allouba's half-derivative generator~\cite{AllouZheng2001}
makes natural for the non-Markov Brownian-time process. The marginal score
factors as $\partial_x\log p_t(x)=\mathbb E_{s\sim p(s\mid x)}[\partial_x\log
p_t(x\mid s)]$, and the conditional score $\partial_x\log p_t(x\mid s)$ is
\emph{bounded} in $s$ (finite data score at $s\to0$, $0$ at $s\to\infty$).
Drawing $s\sim p(s\mid x)$ and returning the conditional score is therefore the
\textbf{Rao--Blackwellized} estimator: same mean, finite variance. On the 1-D
mixtures (exact clock posterior) we confirm: (A) the conditional-clock estimator
has bounded variance ($\le0.6$) and matches the exact score, while the naive
$-(x{-}X_0)/S$ estimator's variance grows with a finer clock floor
($1.5\!\to\!7.9$) and diverges; (B) an augmented annealed Langevin (sample
$s\sim p(s\mid x)$, step with the conditional score) reproduces the data at
exact-marginal quality ($W_1=0.05$ bimodal, $0.11$ trimodal); (C) a conditional
$\varepsilon$-net trains \emph{stably} (target $z$, $\Var=1$) and matches the
exact conditional score to $9\%$, in contrast to the NaN of Exp.~7. The
augmentation thus \textbf{fixes learnability}---but reaches only \emph{parity}
with Gaussian, since Exp.~6 bounds the upside at zero.

\section{What is and is not supported}
\textbf{Supported.} (1)~Hidden-\emph{scale-mixture} noising changes the
score/denoising geometry vs matched Gaussian (saturating-tail scores). (2)~The
latent scale is strongly inferable. (3)~The hidden-clock Tweedie and the
unavoidable latent-clock term (Props.~\ref{prop:tweedie}--\ref{prop:postmean})
hold exactly. (4)~The Exp.~7 infinite-variance obstruction is \emph{removable}:
the augmented $(x,S)$ formulation (Exp.~8) Rao-Blackwellizes the score estimator
to finite variance, trains stably, and yields a working sampler at exact-marginal
quality---making hidden-clock noising well-posed and usable.

\textbf{Falsified / unsupported.} (1)~``Brownian-time path structure matters for
single-time denoising'': \emph{falsified} --- it reduces exactly to a scale
mixture. (2)~``Rougher fractional clocks give stronger matched-variance
corruption'': \emph{falsified} --- $H$ is identically invisible at one time.
(3)~``Predicting the clock improves denoising'': \emph{not supported} (mildly
negative) in the sandbox. (4)~``Hidden-clock noising \emph{generates} better
than matched Gaussian'': \emph{falsified at the exact-score level} (Exp.~6) ---
indistinguishable to marginally worse. (5)~``A \emph{learned} net benefits from
hidden-clock noising'': \emph{falsified} (Exp.~7) --- the DSM target has infinite
variance, so training is ill-posed without a clock floor, and even floored it
carries a learning penalty (worse fit and generation at matched budget).

\textbf{Right framing.} The phenomenon is \emph{hidden-clock / Gaussian
scale-mixture noising}; the Brownian-time and fractional machinery is the
\emph{source} of the clock law and the route to any cross-time structure, not a
single-time advantage. Across denoising MSE, single-time law, exact-score
generation, and learned score matching, we find \emph{no} performance advantage
over a matched Gaussian baseline. The learned-case obstruction is real but
\emph{removable}: the augmented $(x,S)$ reverse (Exp.~8) makes hidden-clock
score matching well-posed and produces a working sampler at exact-marginal
quality---the clock-sourced variance is exactly what the augmentation, and a
source-resolved control-variate treatment in the spirit of the companion
variance-budget work, addresses. The honest synthesis is therefore not ``it
cannot work'' but ``it works, at \emph{parity}'': the augmented construction is
usable, yet the exact-score result (Exp.~6) bounds its generative upside at
zero. The single thing every experiment here discards is the clock's
\emph{cross-time path structure}; whether that ever buys anything is the only
remaining open question, and the burden of proof is on demonstrating a benefit,
not assuming one.
